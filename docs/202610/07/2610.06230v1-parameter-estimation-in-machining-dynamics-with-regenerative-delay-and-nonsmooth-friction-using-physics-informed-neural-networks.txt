# ABSTRACT 

A multi-domain eXtended Physics-Informed Neural Network (XPINN) framework is developed for nonsmooth Delay Differential Equations (DDEs). This is the first implementation to demonstrate the efficacy of partitioning the temporal domain into subdomains of integer multiples of the characteristic time delay and progressively training the associated subnetworks while freezing previously learned parameters. The efficacy of the proposed framework is demonstrated using a machining dynamics model that incorporates both regenerative and nonsmooth frictional effects. Results demonstrate that the proposed multi-domain XPINN framework leads to better solution reconstruction in DDEs and improved parameter estimation compared to a generic PINN (SPINN) formulation. The proposed method works particularly well for extended temporal domains and non-constant history functions. The robustness of inverse XPINN (I-XPINN) is also assessed using reference data contaminated with Gaussian measurement noise. Results indicate that I-XPINN remains resilient to measurement noise and the physics-informed constraints guide the network toward accurately recovering the underlying dynamics. This demonstrates, for the first time, the potential of the proposed framework for reliable parameter identification in DDEs characterised by nonsmoothness and large time delays. 

Keywords Physics-informed neural networks, eXtended PINNs, Delay differential equations, Inverse problem, Parameter identification, Orthogonal metal cutting, Chatter vibrations, Regenerative effects, Nonsmooth frictional effects

# 1 Introduction 

Chatter remains one of the critical challenges in machining. Self-excited vibrations generated during cutting can severely compromise surface finish, reduce dimensional accuracy, accelerate tool wear, and limit productivity [1]. This reflects the inherently dynamic nature of machining, where the cutting tool and workpiece interact through forces that depend on both the instantaneous and past states of the system. The resulting dynamics is thus generated from complex nonlinear interactions between the structural response of the machine-tool system and the cutting process. In machining operations, turning is particularly well suited for such investigations because of its relatively simple geometry, well-defined cutting kinematics, and pronounced regenerative mechanism. This configuration enables 4Parameter Estimation in Machining Dynamics with Regenerative Delay and Nonsmooth Friction using Physics-Informed Neural Networks controlled experiments, reliable force measurements, and direct comparison with mathematical models, making it a representative platform for analysing the chatter vibrations. A characteristic feature of machining chatter is the regenerative effect, where the instantaneous chip thickness is influenced by the surface waviness profile left from the previous pass, introducing intrinsic time delay into the governing dynamics. The cutting force depends on both the current and the past tool positions, which naturally leads to the need for delay differential equation (DDE) models [2, 3]. Friction between the workpiece and the tool’s rake face introduces additional nonlinearities in the cutting forces [4, 5]. The combined influence of regenerative and frictional effects results in a nonsmooth dynamical system with memory, whose mathematical modelling is useful not only for predicting stable and unstable cutting responses but also for investigating the mechanisms underlying transitions between different dynamical states [6, 7]. While DDE models provide a systematic framework for investigating stability, bifurcations, and the onset of chatter for turning by explicitly incorporating delayed cutting-force characteristics, the predictive capability of these models depends critically on the accurate estimation of their physical parameters. In particular, parameters such as the cutting force coefficient and the process damping coefficient determine the magnitude of the cutting forces and the strength of the associated machine-tool interactions, directly impacting the system response and stability predictions. Accurate estimation of these parameters is therefore important for establishing a reliable mathematical model. However, identifying such parameters is challenging because they depend on several factors, such as cutting conditions, tool geometry, cutting speed, tool wear, and flank-face interaction. Conventional identification approaches commonly rely on extensive experimentation-based calibrations or optimisation-based fitting requiring repeated model evaluations [8, 9]. Furthermore, measurement noise, limited observability, nonlinear force characteristics, and the sensitivity of delayed dynamics can severely hinder accurate parameter estimation. Consequently, identifying the underlying machining system parameters from measured system responses constitutes a challenging inverse problem. This difficulty motivates the development of computational approaches that can exploit both the available system-response data and the known underlying physics governing the evolution of the dynamical system. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) provide a powerful framework for tackling such inverse problems. Introduced by Raissi et al. [10] in 2019, PINNs have since gained widespread adoption due to their ability to incorporate physical knowledge directly into the learning process across numerous scientific and engineering domains, including structural mechanics [11–13], bio-engineering [14–16], environmental engineering [17], fluid mechanics [18, 19], nonlinear wave equations [20, 21], stochastic differential equations [22, 23], biomechanics [24], disease spreading dynamics [25, 26], and micromechanics [27]. The application of PINNs to machining problems has also attracted increasing attention. For example, tool wear monitoring [28], prediction of fracture surface quality [29], milling vibration prediction [30], and crater formation and wear-mechanism prediction in Ti6Al4V machining[31]. PINN-based frameworks have also been developed for adhesive tool-wear prediction in high-speed milling, with particular emphasis on improving robustness against inaccurate physical models [32], as well as for tool-wear and remaining useful life prediction in deep-hole boring through adaptive loss weighting [33]. More recently, PINN approaches have been applied to five-axis machining for surface partitioning and tool orientation optimisation [34], milling power-consumption prediction [35], and milling surface-roughness prediction with uncertainty quantification [36]. These studies demonstrate the growing potential of PINN-based frameworks across various applications in machining processes. However, existing applications have predominantly focused on forward prediction and process optimisation of machining systems, whereas parameter estimation of a machining system governed by delayed nonlinear dynamics remains unexplored. The challenges with parameter estimation become more pronounced when PINNs are applied to DDEs. Unlike ordinary differential equations, in which the system state at the current time determines the instantaneous evolution, a DDE depends explicitly on the solution over a preceding time interval. Consequently, approximation using a given physics information must stay consistent with both the current state and the required history of the solution. Recent studies have explored different strategies for incorporating delay information into PINN formulations. He and Tiamiyu [37] developed a PINN approach for solving DDEs, using neural-network trial functions to satisfy the initial conditions and an iterative formulation to obtain accurate solutions. Their approach was demonstrated through six numerical examples involving scalar and coupled DDE systems with different nonlinear and delayed formulations. Wang et al. [38] subsequently proposed a PINN framework to solve forward DDEs and approximate inverse solutions while identifying unknown delay parameters. These studies establish the overall adequacy of PINN-based approaches for both forward solution approximation and inverse parameter identification in DDEs. However, existing studies remain inadequate for addressing the interplay of large delays, extended temporal domains, non-constant history functions, and nonsmooth effects, which presents a significant unresolved challenge in the analysis of such systems. Furthermore, the robustness of PINNs for inverse parameter identification in the presence of measurement noise remains insufficiently explored. These gaps are particularly relevant to machining as it simultaneously involves regenerative delay, nonlinear cutting forces, nonsmooth frictional effects, and process damping, while the characteristic delay is relatively large compared 5Parameter Estimation in Machining Dynamics with Regenerative Delay and Nonsmooth Friction using Physics-Informed Neural Networks with the temporal scales considered in previously mentioned PINN-DDE studies. Such large delays introduce additional challenges in representing the history-dependent solution and maintaining accurate propagation of the delayed state over the computational domain. To address this gap, this work first investigates the capability of a single-domain PINN (SPINN), based on the formulation introduced by Wang et al. [38], for approximating the nonlinear machining DDE. The performance of the single-domain formulation is examined for constant and non-constant history functions and over increasingly extended temporal domains. While SPINN can adequately represent delayed dynamics when a constant history function is prescribed, its performance deteriorates substantially when a non-constant history function is used and over extended time intervals. This limitation is significant for the machining problem under consideration because the history function provides the initial delayed state from which the subsequent regenerative dynamics evolve. Therefore, its accurate representation is essential for reliable prediction of the system response. Motivated by these limitations, a multi-domain eXtended Physics-Informed Neural Network (XPINN) framework is then developed, where the temporal domain is decomposed into multiple subdomains, and the solution is learned through interconnected neural networks. The domain-decomposition strategy enables the solution of the delayed system to be approximated locally across successive temporal intervals while maintaining consistency between neighbouring subdomains, thereby improving the representation of machining dynamics over extended time horizons. The forward solution problem is considered first because reliable parameter estimation requires an accurate representation of the underlying delayed dynamics. The resulting framework is then employed to identify the unknown parameter values. The proposed approach is evaluated for both stable and unstable dynamical regimes, and its robustness is further examined in the presence of noise. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first XPINN-based parameter identification for a nonlinear delayed machining system incorporating regenerative and nonsmooth frictional effects, while explicitly examining the influence of non-constant history functions, extended temporal domains, dynamical regimes, and observational noise. The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section. 2 presents the mathematical formulation of the metal cutting system with regenerative and frictional chatter, the inverse problem formulation, and the stable and unstable regimes of the cutting process. Section. 3 discusses the formulation of SPINN for the forward problem and its outcomes. The forward XPINN framework is introduced, and its performance evaluation in approximating the DDE solution is presented in Section. 4. Section. 5 introduces both the inverse SPINN and XPINN formulation and their outcomes. Finally, Section. 6 summarises the key outcomes of the study. 

# 2 Delayed Dynamics of Metal Cutting 

2.1 Metal cutting system with regenerative and frictional chatter 

In orthogonal metal cutting, a single-point cutting tool positioned perpendicular to the workpiece removes material through deformation by generating chips. Turning is an example of this orthogonal cutting mechanism in which a stationary single-point tool machines a rotating cylindrical workpiece, removing material to reduce diameter and shape external surfaces as shown in Fig. 1. The cutting process refers to the complex dynamical interaction between a machine tool and a workpiece. The interaction between the cutting process and the structural dynamics of the machine-tool system can result in self-excited vibrations known as machining chatter. Chatter is an undesirable dynamic phenomenon that can significantly alter the system’s stability. A major type of chatter that occurs in turning processes is regenerative chatter that arises from the interaction between the current tool position and the surface finish left by the previous revolution of the workpiece [2, 3]. Consequently, a small change in instantaneous chip thickness modifies the cutting force, altering the tool’s displacement, and imprinting this deviation on the workpiece surface. Friction is another key source of chatter in cutting processes [4, 5], occurring at the tool-chip and tool-workpiece contact interfaces and dependent on the relative motion and contact conditions. During turning, the friction component of the cutting force depends on static and dynamic coefficients of friction and relative velocity. Friction introduces additional nonlinear behaviours into the tool dynamics, resulting in changes in the response amplitudes and affecting the stability of oscillations. The regenerative and frictional mechanisms act simultaneously. The regenerative contribution depends on the current and delayed tool states, whereas the frictional contribution depends on the instantaneous relative motion. The interaction between these mechanisms makes the cutting process fundamentally different from a simple linear vibration problem, as the resulting dynamics contain both delayed feedback and nonlinear frictional effects, and their interaction can determine whether a cutting process remains stable or develops self-excited chatter vibrations. 6Parameter Estimation in Machining Dynamics with Regenerative Delay and Nonsmooth Friction using Physics-Informed Neural Networks

2.3 Unknown parameters and inverse-problem formulation 

The mathematical formulation of the turning process presented in the previous section (Sec. 2.2) contains various physical and operating parameters that characterise the machining dynamics. In this study, the cutting force coefficient 

K and the process damping coefficient Cy are selected as the unknown parameters to be estimated using the inverse PINN methods. The remaining parameters are assumed to be known from the machining configuration and experimental conditions. The choice of K and Cy is motivated by their influence on the dynamics of the machining system and also by the practical challenges associated with their experimental identification. The cutting force coefficient K determines the magnitude of the cutting-force contribution through the dimensionless parameter presented in Eq. (8c) and consequently has a direct influence on the cutting force and the resulting tool vibrations. Cutting force coefficients are typically identified from cutting-force measurements across a range of cutting conditions [8, 41]. However, the cutting force coefficient depends on the tool-workpiece combination, cutting conditions, and tool geometry, so it generally requires process-specific experimental calibration rather than being a universal material constant. The process damping coefficient Cy is chosen since the process damping force represents an important source of energy dissipation arising from the flank face of the tool and the uncut workpiece material. Its magnitude is affected by factors such as cutting speed, flank-face interaction, and tool wear. Budak and Tunc reported that process damping had historically been difficult to quantify due to the complexity of directly measuring the associated damping forces and proposed a procedure based on experimentally observed stability limits [42], while Kurata et al. subsequently investigated in-process identification of process damping during turning and milling [43]. Altintas et al. obtained the values of process-damping coefficients using controlled orthogonal cutting experiments with a fast tool servo and piezoelectric excitation [8]. Mechanisms and modelling involved in machining process damping are summarised in the recent review article by Feng and Lie [9]. The process damping force given in Eq. (7) explicitly depends on the velocity of the tool. Thus, the estimation of Cy requires identification of a parameter embedded within the nonlinear dynamics response rather than a simple direct measurement of a damping force. 2.4 Stable and Unstable cases 

The dynamic response of the considered machining system depends on the selected cutting conditions, particularly the spindle speed ( N) and the depth of the cut ( ap). Depending on these operating conditions, the tool vibrations in cutting dynamics can either die down or lead to self-excited oscillations. A stable cutting condition corresponds to a chatter-free response of the system, whereas an unstable cutting condition corresponds to the development of chatter, characterised by growing oscillations in the dynamic response.

4.4 Training Implementation 

Training is performed sequentially across the subdomains. The first subnet is trained using the history constraint and physics loss on the initial interval. Once its parameters are optimised, the network is frozen and no longer updated. The second subnet is then trained using the frozen output of the first subnet to evaluate the delayed state, together with interface continuity constraints. This process is repeated for all remaining subdomains. The optimisation uses the Adam algorithm with an exponentially decaying learning rate. The learning rate ηepo at epoch epo is updated according to 

ηepo = γd ηepo −1, (34) where the initial learning rate is set to η0 = 10 −3, and γd = 0.99995 is the exponential decay factor. The learning rate is therefore reduced incrementally at each epoch, resulting in a cumulative reduction of approximately 39 .35% after 10 4

epochs and helping the optimiser converge more smoothly. The resulting training strategy is domain-decomposed and physics-constrained. It ensures that information flows forward in time only through previously trained subnetworks, which is essential for solving DDEs with regenerative dependence. For the kd -th subdomain, the model parameters of subnetworks 1 , . . . , kd − 1 are set to non-trainable, while the parameters of only the kd -th network are kept trainable. The optimiser is then built using only the parameters that currently require gradients. At each epoch, the collocation points are resampled, the physics residual is evaluated, and the total loss is minimised using backpropagation. # 5 Inverse PINN framework for estimating the machining parameters 

The forward problem presented in Section 4 assumes that the governing system parameters of the machining system are known and determines the corresponding dynamic response. In practical machining applications, however, several parameters are difficult to determine directly from experiments. In particular, the cutting force coefficient ( K) and the process damping coefficient ( Cy) strongly influence the dynamic response. The values of the parameters considered substantially large and vary in magnitude with K = 6.02 × 10 9 [Nm −2] and Cy = 6.11 × 10 5 [Nm −1]. Directly treating these as trainable ANN quantities can therefore introduce an unfavourable scale disparity between the optimisation variables and may affect the numerical stability of the inverse training process. To improve the conditioning of the optimisation, the logarithms of the unknown parameters are used as the trainable variables. (i) It ensures that the estimated parameters remain strictly positive throughout training, which is consistent with the physical meaning of the unknown coefficients. (ii) Optimisation is performed on a logarithmic scale, which reduces the disparity in magnitude between the two parameters and allows their relative changes to be learned more effectively. # 6 Conclusion 

This work developed physics-informed neural network (PINN) inverse identification approaches for unknown physical parameters in machining dynamics, addressing both regenerative delay and nonsmooth frictional effects. The coupled parameters of cutting force coefficient K and process damping coefficient Cy were simultaneously estimated using two complementary PINN frameworks. The forward problem, approximating the DDE solution using the provided physical information, was examined first. The results showed that the single-domain PINN (SPINN) formulation can approximate delayed dynamics over a relatively short time horizon, but it exhibits limitations when a non-constant history function 30 Parameter Estimation in Machining Dynamics with Regenerative Delay and Nonsmooth Friction using Physics-Informed Neural Networks is considered and the solution is extended over longer time intervals. Motivated by these limitations, a multi-domain eXtended PINN (XPINN) framework was introduced to improve the representation of the delayed machine dynamics over an extended temporal domain and a non-constant history function. The temporal domain was decomposed into multiple successive subdomains, with an individual neural network assigned to each subdomain. The subdomains were trained sequentially, with each completed interval’s parameters frozen before training the next, reducing the optimisation burden of learning the full delayed response and enabling the solution to be built progressively across the extended temporal domain. The forward data-driven solutions of these two PINN frameworks for the considered DDE machining system were evaluated using scatter plots and by computing the relative L2 error and the mean absolute error. The proposed frameworks were evaluated for both stable (chatter-free) and unstable (chatter) machining regimes, and it was demonstrated that XPINN substantially improves solution accuracy compared with SPINN, reducing the relative L2

error by approximately 73 .2% in the stable regime and 92 .1% in the unstable regime, relative to the best-performing SPINN configuration. These findings highlight the effectiveness of the XPINN formulation in improving the accuracy of the forward solution of the considered machining model. Later, the loss functions and trainable parameters of these two SPINN and XPINN frameworks were modified for unknown parameter identification. The Inverse Single-domain Physics-Informed Neural Network (I-SPINN) and the Inverse eXtended Physics-Informed Neural Network (XPINN), were used to estimate unknown parameters. The results of inverse problems showed that the I-XPINN delivers substantially improved inverse-estimation accuracy, reducing the error in Cy by about 71 .5% in the stable case and 95 .2% in the unstable case, and reducing the error in K by approximately 99.6% and 98.3% in the respective cases, demonstrating a clear advantage over the I-SPINN formulation. These inverse frameworks were also examined for DDE solution reconstruction using the estimated parameters, and the results showed that the I-XPINN achieved relative L2 errors on the order of 10 −3, indicating its ability to maintain high prediction accuracy across both stable and unstable regimes over the extended temporal domain. The robustness of the I-XPINN framework was also assessed using noisy data generated by adding Gaussian noise to account for measurement uncertainty. The overall results show that I-XPINN consistently maintains low parameter-estimation error even under significant measurement noise, demonstrating strong resilience to measurement uncertainty. The robustness of the proposed I-XPINN framework with noisy data was further investigated by recon-structing the DDE solution from the I-XPINN model trained on noisy training data. The outcomes showed that the L2

and mean absolute errors computed against the deterministic solution remain consistently lower than those computed against the noisy observations, indicating that the physics-informed constraints guide the network in recovering the underlying machining dynamics of the DDE under consideration. Beyond machining dynamics, this work provides the first systematic strategy for addressing inverse problems in nonlinear time-delay systems over extended time horizons exceeding one characteristic delay, τ. The framework addresses substantial identification challenges due to the combined effects of delayed interactions, nonlinear frictional dynamics, and limited parameter observability due to measurement noise, which can severely constrain conventional parameter-estimation approaches.
