# ABSTRACT 

Graph Neural Operators (GNOs) provide flexible surrogate models for learning solution operators of partial differential equations (PDEs). However, standard GNOs typically parameterize the integral kernel with a monolithic neural network and evaluate kernel interactions over graph edges, leading to substantial computational and memory overhead at high resolutions or with large neighborhoods. To address these limitations, we propose Functional Tensor Decomposition Graph Neural Operator (FTD-GNO), a memory-efficient GNO framework that decouples the high-dimensional continuous integral kernel into low-dimensional mode-wise functions. By instantiating the kernel with classical tensor decomposition formats, including CP, Tensor-Train, and Tucker decompositions, FTD-GNO enables algebraic reconstruction of the integral operator without explicitly materializing full edge-wise kernel tensors. This factorized formulation reduces the memory footprint of kernel evaluation and aggregation while retaining the continuous operator-learning structure of GNOs. Theoretical complexity analysis shows that FTD-GNO substantially lowers parameter and activation-memory costs associated with high-dimensional kernel construction. Experiments show lower peak memory than the corresponding unfactorized graph-integral baselines, with shorter recorded training times. Fourier–graph experiments further demonstrate that FTD can improve the efficiency of a graph-integral layer within a hybrid operator and has good scalability. 

# 1 Introduction 

Partial differential equations (PDEs) are the core mathematical tools for characterizing the spatiotemporal evolution of complex physical systems [1, 2]. In practical scientific and engineering applications, such as uncertainty quantification, optimal control, and inverse problem solving, it is often necessary to perform massive, repeated evaluations of PDEs driven by varying physical parameters. Traditional numerical solvers (e.g., finite element [3, 4] or finite difference methods [5, 6] rely heavily on high-resolution grids, and their prohibitive iterative computational overhead struggles to meet the demands of real-time inference in complex systems [7, 8]. Recently, operator learning has emerged as a novel deep learning paradigm. Unlike conventional neural networks that only learn mappings between finite-dimensional vectors, neural operators (NOs) [9, 10] aim to directly construct nonlinear mappings between infinite-dimensional continuous function spaces. This discretization-independent architecture endows models with the remarkable capability of zero-shot cross-resolution generalization. arXiv:2610.04212v1 [cs.LG] 3 Oct 2026 FTD-GNO: Memory-Efficient Graph Neural Operators through Functional Tensor Decomposition of the Kernel

The proposed methodology involves the decomposition of the integral kernel into multiple single-variable neural networks, in conjunction with the utilisation of configurable tensor decomposition modes. This approach facilitates the reconstruction of the integral operator, thereby circumventing the occurrence of memory overflow. Graph Neural Operators (GNOs) [11] discretize continuous spaces into graph topologies and approximate integral transforms via message-passing mechanisms, demonstrating unparalleled flexibility in handling unstructured grids and complex geometric domains. However, when confronted with high-dimensional systems and high-resolution grids, the underlying architecture of standard GNOs exposes intertwined flaws. Specifically, during the integral kernel evaluation and spatial aggregation phases, information interaction relies heavily on edge-based message-passing mechanisms. This implies that the grid must explicitly construct, instantiate and store the complete integral kernel tensor on each edge within the computational graph. As spatial dimensionality increases or grid nodes become denser, the memory requirement to construct the full input exhibits explosive exponential growth, manifesting as the “curse of dimensionality". Such edge-level storage dependency severely limits network depth and renders GNOs highly susceptible to Out-Of-Memory (OOM) [12] errors, profoundly restricting their practical utility. Furthermore, to adequately capture the complex feature couplings in multi-dimensional spaces, the hidden layer dimensions of the network are forced to expand drastically, triggering a nonlinear surge in model parameters. To address the aforementioned challenges, this paper proposes a novel Graph Neural Operator framework based on Functional Tensor Decomposition (FTD-GNO). The essence of this framework lies in the deep decoupling of the high-dimensional tensor construction process within the continuous domain. The overall schematic of this setup is illustrated in Figure 1. Specifically, we elegantly strip the originally massive full-scale network inputs into low-dimensional variable sets corresponding to independent physical dimensions. Under the multi-branch architecture of FTD-GNO, lightweight subnetworks tailored for specific dimensions only need to focus on the 1D feature objects within their respective dimensions. Additionally, we leverage the associativity of tensor decomposition to perform an algebraic reconstruction of the spatial integral operators, with source-dependent factors aggregated before their combination with query-dependent factors. This decomposition and reconstruction strategy thoroughly circumvents the explicit computation of full-size tensors. Without sacrificing the continuous spatial expressive capacity of the neural operator, it successfully compresses the memory complexity during the kernel evaluation and spatial integral aggregation stages from exponential to linear growth. Concurrently, benefiting from the low-rank factorized decoupling of multi-dimensional variables by the integral kernel, this architecture successfully addresses the bottleneck of exponential parameter explosion with respect to dimensionality. This has resulted in a substantial streamlining of the parameter space of the model. Under the proposed framework, we focus on combining Graph Neural Operators with three prominent tensor decompo-sition techniques, including Canonic-Polyadic (CP) [13], Tensor-Train (TT) [14] and Tucker decomposition [15]. The main contributions of our work are summarized as follows: • Parameter Compression via Tensor Decoupling: We integrate functional tensor decomposition into GNOs to decouple high-dimensional kernels into low-dimensional variables, drastically slashing the parameter scale. • Memory Optimization via Algebraic Reconstruction: We algebraically reconstruct the integral operator to shift from edge-based message passing to node-based computation, greatly reducing memory costs. 2FTD-GNO: Memory-Efficient Graph Neural Operators through Functional Tensor Decomposition of the Kernel • Comprehensive Empirical Validation: We demonstrate the highly competitive performance of the proposed method compared to GNOs in PDE benchmarks providing compelling evidence that firmly corroborates our theoretical analysis. 

# 2 Related works 

Neural Operators Distinguished from traditional surrogate models for PDEs, the core advantage of neural operators lies in their construction of a universal approximator defined between function spaces. According to their architectures, they can be broadly categorized into three families [16]. The first family comprises operator architectures based on the universal approximation theorem. DeepONet [17] pioneered a dual-path network architecture, encoding discretized sensor data of input functions via a branch network and processing target query coordinates via a trunk network, thereby laying the foundational theory for operator learning. Subsequently, architectures based on integral kernel transforms further expanded the boundaries of this field. Among them, Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) [18] innovatively maps the integral kernel into the Fourier frequency domain, leveraging the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to extract low-frequency modes, thus achieving highly computationally efficient global convolutions. Meanwhile, Graph Neural Operator (GNO) [11] employs graph neural networks to perform Monte-Carlo integral approximations within the spatial domain, effectively modeling nonlocal operators on complex geometric domains. In recent years, models represented by GK-Transformer [19] and General Neural Operator (GNOT) [20] have further integrated attention mechanisms into operator architectures, significantly enhancing the capability to capture features in multi-scale physical fields. 

Functional Tensor Decomposition in Deep Learning Classical tensor decomposition methods have long been widely utilized to compress high-dimensional data matrices and reduce model ranks [21, 22], including CP [13], TT [14], and Tucker [15] decomposition. In recent years, these algebraic tools have been introduced into deep learning models within the continuous function domain, spawning the emerging research direction of functional tensor decomposition (FTD). In the field of Implicit Neural Representations (INRs), frameworks such as F-INR [23] dismantle the massive Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) used for processing high-dimensional coordinates into a series of axially independent, lightweight subnetworks. This greatly alleviates the curse of dimensionality encountered when fitting complex videos or high-resolution scenes, and significantly accelerates convergence. Similarly, in Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) [24] for solving PDEs, employing tensor decomposition formats to separate spatial and temporal variables not only improves the network’s ability to fit gradients within complex loss landscapes but also effectively circumvents the computational dissipation caused by high-dimensional dense sampling. These advancements thoroughly validate the immense potential of tensor decomposition in handling high-dimensional mappings in continuous domains. 

Tensor Decomposition for Operator Learning As operator learning is applied to increasingly complex scientific computing scenarios, controlling its parameter scale and memory footprint has become a recent research hotspot. To this end, researchers have begun exploring the integration of various tensor dimensionality reduction and compression techniques into advanced neural operator variants. In terms of frequency-domain transformation dimensionality reduction, the Factorized FNO (F-FNO) [25] introduces fourier factorization to process each spatial dimension orthogonally and independently in the frequency domain. This successfully reduces model complexity by an order of magnitude while enabling stable scaling to deeper network structures. Following this dimensionality reduction paradigm, D-FNO [26] decomposes high-dimensional latent representations into a series of rank-1 tensor products, converting 3D FFTs into 1D FFTs, thereby further reducing computational complexity. To address the parameter redundancy of global convolution kernels, architectures like Tucker-FNO [27] and MG-TFNO [28] impose strict low-rank tensor constraints directly on the weight matrices, aiming to capture richer frequency-domain features while simultaneously reducing parameter counts. In the dimension of spatial basis extraction, POD-DeepONet [29] leverages proper orthogonal decomposition to pre-extract the dominant modes of the data as basis functions, significantly elevating the representational efficiency of the operator through effective spatial dimensionality reduction. Furthermore, the advantages of tensor decomposition have even extended to the optimization and solving stages of operator models. TensorGRaD [30] decomposes gradients into the sum of low-rank and sparse tensors, efficiently capturing both global trends and high-frequency outliers in the evolutionary states of optimizers.

3.2 Functional Tensor Decomposition for GNO 

As discussed above, the kernel integral operator in the standard GNO relies on a single, large neural network to parameterize the integral kernel. This design leads to substantial computational complexity. To address this issue, we incorporate functional tensor decomposition (FTD) into GNO and propose the FTD-GNO model. The core idea is to represent the high-dimensional, parameter-intensive kernel as a composition of low-dimensional, low-rank component functions. This method significantly reduces the number of parameters while preserving the dominant components of the tensor structure, thereby enabling effective extraction of key features from the physical field.

# 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose FTD-GNO, a novel Graph Neural Operator architecture that reconstructs the integral kernel via functional tensor decomposition. Through the implementation of variable decoupling and algebraic integral reconstruction methodologies, we have successfully overcome the computational and memory limitations inherent to conventional GNOs when processing high-resolution physical fields. As a result, the memory requirement scales linearly and smoothly with the node size. Moreover, a systematic evaluation of three core variants is conducted, utilising CP, TT, and Tucker decompositions as the underlying methodologies. Across the regular-grid and irregular-mesh tasks, FTD reduces peak memory relative to corresponding unfactorized graph-integral baselines and also benefits a Fourier–graph hybrid. This breakthrough paves a highly efficient path for the engineering deployment of GNOs in large-scale, high-fidelity scientific computing. # A General Framework of Parametric PDEs 

This section delineates the general mathematical framework of parametric PDEs to establish the physical context for the operator learning discussed in Section 3.1. In scientific computing and engineering, the evolution of complex physical systems is typically governed by PDEs. When variable parameters are introduced to account for uncertainty quantification or physical inverse problems, these parameter-driven equations are collectively termed parametric PDEs. Mathematically, let D ⊂ Rdx denote a bounded spatial domain with boundary ∂D . We consider a general class of parametric PDEs formulated as: 

(Lau)( x) = f (x), x ∈ Du(x) = 0 , x ∈ ∂D (13) where a(x) denotes the input parameter field of the system (e.g., physical properties of heterogeneous media, con-ductivity coefficients, or fluid viscosity), and u(x) is the unknown target physical response field. The operator La,which explicitly depends on the parameter field a, mathematically characterizes the underlying conservation laws and spatial differential rules governing the physical system. Meanwhile, f (x) represents the source term or forcing function, corresponding to external driving forces (e.g., external energy sources, mass sinks, or body forces) acting within the interior of domain D. The aforementioned formulation is also subject to homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. To be more specific, taking the steady-state Darcy flow, a prototypical fluid-in-porous-media problem widely bench-marked in the operator learning, can be governed by the following second-order elliptic PDE: 

−∇ · (a(x)∇u(x)) = f (x), x ∈ Du(x) = 0 , x ∈ ∂D. (14) Within this physical context, the input parameter a(x) generally represents a highly heterogeneous permeability field within the medium, with the target solution u(x) corresponding to the resulting fluid pressure field. Assuming the well-posedness of the PDE, for a fixed source term f (x) and given boundary conditions, each specific parameter instance 

a in the input parameter space is associated with to a unique physical response solution u. This strict dependence, inherently dictated by the underlying physical conservation laws, essentially establishes a nonlinear mapping defined between infinite-dimensional function spaces, i.e., a 7 → u.

Architectures. The baseline graph operators use four operator layers with 32 hidden channels. For GNO, the internal kernel network κ is configured as a three-layer feedforward neural network with hidden layer widths of [64 , 64] . For the evaluation of FTD-GNO, we utilize CP, TT, and Tucker decompositions for the kernel function. The decomposition rank is fixed at R = 8 for CP and TT, R = 4 for Tucker. The hidden layer dimension is set to 64. The rank, width, depth, and kernel-input studies explicitly vary these settings, as specified in the corresponding tables. To evaluate extensibility, we construct FNOGNO by replacing the preceding graph operator layers with Fourier layers while retaining the final graph integral. CP-, TT-, and Tucker-FNOGNO replace only the kernel of this final layer. Each 15 FTD-GNO: Memory-Efficient Graph Neural Operators through Functional Tensor Decomposition of the Kernel FNOGNO/FTD-FNOGNO comparison therefore shares the Fourier backbone, lifting and projection settings, and final graph neighborhood. FNO is included as an architectural reference. To distinguish functional factorization from weight compression, we additionally consider LR-GNO. Each dense weight matrix Wl ∈ Rml×nl in its kernel MLP is parameterized as

Optimization and training budgets. In all experiments, we train the models using the AdamW optimizer. The batch size is set to 32, with an initial learning rate of 1e − 3 and a weight decay of 1e − 4. We also employ a cosine annealing schedule for learning rate decay. The primary Darcy baseline and resolution-transfer studies, together with the parameter matched comparison in Table 9, use 300 training epochs. The additional Darcy/NS architectural comparisons in Table 2 and the capacity studies in Tables 12 and 13 use 100 epochs. The elasticity study reports all models at 100 epochs and additional runs of the factorized variants at 300 epochs. The extended Darcy study varies the CP-GNO training budget while keeping its architecture fixed. These experiments are analyzed separately to distinguish equal-epoch comparisons from additional optimization.

LR-GNO and CP/TT-GNO have almost identical parameter counts, but their peak-memory costs differ substantially. LR-GNO compresses the kernel MLP weights while continuing to evaluate the network on neighboring node pairs. In contrast, FTD reorganizes kernel evaluation and aggregation through reusable node-wise factors. This comparison indicates that reducing the number of parameters alone does not explain the memory advantage. The prediction errors of LR-GNO and CP/TT-GNO are close, although LR-GNO performs slightly better. Thus, functional factorization introduces an accuracy–resource trade-off even at matched parameter counts. The result supports the contribution of the factorized contraction without implying identical approximation or optimization behavior between the two parameterizations. E.2.2 Supplementary experiments Capacity: Whether longer training or greater model capacity improves accuracy. 

For Darcy flow, longer training progressively narrows the accuracy gap between CP-GNO and GNO while leaving the CP-GNO architecture unchanged. At the longest tested budget, CP-GNO approaches the baseline accuracy, but a small error gap remains and their recorded total training times become comparable, shown in Table 11. The elasticity results in Table 10 likewise demonstrate the value of additional optimization. Table 12 examines factor network width w, depth l, and decomposition rank R under a fixed 100-epoch budget. Increasing width provides little accuracy improvement in the tested configurations while increasing parameter count and memory. At the tested width and rank, the shallowest configuration achieves both lower error and lower measured resource consumption than the deeper alternatives. Increasing rank also produces a non-monotonic error response. The study in Table 13 further shows that the preferred rank depends on the decomposition. CP and TT achieve their lowest tested error at R = 8 , whereas Tucker performs best at R = 4 .These observations indicate that additional optimization can improve the accuracy of a fixed-capacity factorized model, while increasing capacity alone does not guarantee better finite-budget performance. In practice, rank, width, depth, and training budget should be selected jointly using validation performance and the available memory and runtime budget.

# F Limitations 

Our limitations and future work are summarized as follows: (1) High-dimensional scalability of the Tucker architecture: When handling inputs with more than four variables, the core tensor in Tucker-GNO remains susceptible to a combinatorial explosion in memory footprint. While CP and TT decompositions successfully avoid this issue, optimizing the Tucker architecture to fully harness its powerful cross-dimensional coupling capabilities requires further investigation. (2) Representation bottlenecks of static ranks: Currently, the tensor decomposition rank R must be pre-defined as a hyperparameter. Empirical evidence shows that R = 8 is sufficient for most tasks. However, when modeling highly dynamic and complex physical processes (e.g., strong turbulent fields), a fixed rank may constrain the expressive capacity of the integral kernel. (3) Residual memory risks under dense topologies: Despite deep decomposition and decoupling at the input variable level, FTD-GNO may still face potential OOM risks if the number of neighboring nodes increases drastically. To address these limitations, future research will explore Dynamic Rank Allocation mechanisms, with a view to enabling the model to adaptively accommodate the complexities of varying physical fields. Additionally, we will investigate spatial decomposition models based directly on grid coordinates. The ultimate goal is to fundamentally eradicate the OOM risks inherent to GNOs when processing fully connected graphs or exceptionally large receptive fields. 19
