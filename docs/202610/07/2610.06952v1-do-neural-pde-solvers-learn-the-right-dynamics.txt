## ABSTRACT 

Neural PDE solvers can achieve low prediction errors, but do they reproduce the dynamics of the systems they model? Prediction scores alone offer an incomplete answer: they measure agreement with reference solutions but provide limited in-sight into how errors accumulate, nearby states diverge, or extreme events arise. We propose an evaluation framework that directly examines these behaviors in deterministic and stochastic neural solvers. By evolving ensembles of nearby ini-tial states and comparing them with direct numerical simulation, we assess three complementary aspects of learned dynamics: error formation, ensemble geom-etry, and extreme events . Experiments on two-dimensional Kolmogorov flow reveal limitations that conventional scores can obscure. Smaller trajectory errors can reflect weaker error amplification despite less accurate local updates. Mod-els can match an ensemble’s overall spread and effective dimension while failing to capture the spatial directions where nearby states diverge. Similarly, matching overall event frequencies can conceal failures to predict persistent extreme events. 

These findings show that improved prediction accuracy does not necessarily im-ply greater dynamical fidelity. Our framework makes this distinction measurable, providing concrete criteria for evaluating whether advances in neural PDE solvers better capture the underlying dynamics.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Neural partial differential equation (PDE) solvers have shown considerable success in predicting the evolution of physical systems. Recent pretrained models, including MPP, DPOT, and Poseidon, extend this capability across equations (McCabe et al., 2024; Hao et al., 2024; Herde et al., 2024). Progress is primarily measured through pointwise field errors, with studies of chaotic systems and

These evaluations quantify agreement with reference solutions, but leave a fundamental unsolved question: 

Do neural PDE solvers learn the right dynamics? 

Prediction accuracy alone provides an incomplete answer. During a rollout, each model update intro-duces errors that interact with discrepancies inherited from earlier steps. A smaller trajectory error may therefore reflect more accurate updates, weaker amplification of existing errors, or cancellation between the two. Beyond individual trajectories, the dynamics determine how nearby initial states separate and which spatial patterns dominate their evolution. These responses are not established by matching an ensemble’s overall spread. Similarly, agreement in aggregate event frequency can con-ceal errors under particular initial conditions, while aggregate distributional scores may inadequately expose relative errors in rare-event probabilities (Gneiting & Ranjan, 2011). Understanding learned dynamics thus requires examining how predictions evolve and which properties they preserve. In this work, we investigate three questions: How do model errors form and propagate? Do nearby initial states evolve along the same directions as in the reference system? Do extreme events occur with the right intensities and under the right initial conditions? We study these questions through a common experimental protocol: densely sample perturbed initial states around the initial state of each case, evolve each ensemble using both the neural solver and direct numerical simulation (DNS), and compare their evolution. These perturbations serve as controlled probes of the learned dynamics; they do not presuppose uncertainty in the initial conditions. The protocol accommodates deterministic and stochastic solvers. For stochastic solvers, ensemble members share a random path, with fresh randomness at successive steps, so that differences across members reflect their responses to initial-state perturbations under the same realization of model randomness. Our experiments on deterministic chaotic two-dimensional Kolmogorov flow identify three discrep-ancies between predictive performance and dynamical fidelity. 

Error formation: lower trajectory error need not imply more accurate local updates. We decompose error at successive updates into newly introduced model residuals, propagation of ex-isting discrepancies, and their interaction. The analysis reveals cases in which persistently larger local residuals coexist with lower trajectory errors because existing discrepancies undergo weaker dynamical amplification. The residuals also alter the collective evolution of nearby states: early in prediction, they reduce differences between ensemble members relative to DNS advancement from the same model states, while increasing the error of the centered ensemble against the DNS reference. Thus, keeping trajectories closer together can accompany a less faithful response to initial-state perturbations. 

Ensemble geometry: matching ensemble spread and dimension does not ensure the correct spatial directions. As the DNS ensemble evolves, its variance first concentrates into a few direc-tions and later spreads across more directions, while the dominant directions themselves change. Models reproduce the broad evolution of total variance and effective dimension, yet most tested configurations show substantial misalignment with the dominant DNS subspace at intermediate pre-diction times. This discrepancy persists even when variance and effective dimension closely match DNS. Capturing how much an ensemble spreads and how many directions it occupies therefore does not establish that it evolves along the correct directions. 

Extreme events: correct aggregate event frequencies can conceal incorrect conditional prob-abilities. Models whose overall extreme-event frequencies closely match DNS can substantially overestimate or underestimate event probabilities within individual initial-state ensembles. These errors cancel upon aggregation, allowing apparently correct overall frequencies to conceal failures to predict extreme events that persist under the reference dynamics. Even when predicted vortices remain recognizable and have similar peak intensities, their deformation and rotation can differ sub-stantially from the reference dynamics. Together, these findings show how favorable prediction scores can coexist with systematic errors in learned dynamics. Our investigation complements conventional evaluation by tracing error for-mation, testing the geometry of nearby trajectories, and resolving extreme-event statistics by initial 2condition. These analyses provide concrete criteria for assessing whether improvements in neural PDE solvers correspond to a more faithful representation of the underlying system. 

## 2 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND DYNAMICAL PROBES 

We probe learned dynamics by evolving ensembles of nearby initial states with both direct numer-ical simulation (DNS) and neural solvers. This allows us to examine individual prediction errors alongside the collective response to initial-state perturbations. All three investigations share the same initial states, data split, and prediction origin. 

Reference Dynamics. We consider two-dimensional Kolmogorov flow, governed by the incom-pressible Navier–Stokes equations with sinusoidal forcing and periodic boundaries. Although deter-ministic, this system exhibits chaotic evolution and develops localized intense vortices (Vela-Martín & Avila, 2024). 

Dataset Construction and Initial-State Probes. We sample temporally decorrelated base states from the stationary-flow archive of Vela-Martín & Avila (2024), with each state defining a case 

c. Spatially structured perturbations, constructed from rescaled, randomly signed differences be-tween archive states, define nearby ensemble members m. We denote their vorticity fields by 

xqc,m (t) ∈ RD , where q identifies the solver and D is the spatial dimension. Cases are split into training, validation, and test sets. Training and validation use only unperturbed trajectories; en-semble diagnostics use perturbed test members, each with a matched DNS reference. Appendix C details the numerical settings, dataset sizes, forecast initialization, and stochastic sampling protocol. 

Solvers and Forecast Evaluation. We evaluate configurations of U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015), FNO (Li et al., 2020), AFNO (Guibas et al., 2021), DPOT (Hao et al., 2024), DySLIM (Schiff et al., 2024), PDE-Refiner (Lippe et al., 2023), ACDM (Kohl et al., 2026), PFNO, and DYffusion (Rühling Cachay et al., 2023). Configuration labels specify the input history length H, output block length P , and training unroll length K. We exclude configurations that perform poorly on conven-tional forecast metrics from dynamical evaluation; Appendix D provides the screening details.

Event occurrence captures only part of the dynamics. Once a vortex forms, its deformation and rotation reflect its interaction with the surrounding flow. In the classical elliptic-vortex problem, background strain and vorticity govern changes in axis ratio and orientation (Kida, 1981). These geometric properties therefore provide diagnostics beyond peak intensity. In case 4040, we characterize the evolution of the dominant vortex core using its peak vorticity 

V , the major-to-minor axis ratio χ70 of its 70%-of-peak core, and the accumulated change in its major-axis orientation (Appendix F.3). Figure 7 shows that the solvers preserve recognizable intense vortices but misrepresent their subse-quent geometry. AFNO retains a comparatively round core while the DNS vortex elongates. Con-versely, U-Net H4 K8 and DPOT sustain elongation after the reference vortex becomes rounder. Predicted orientations also advance ahead of or lag behind DNS. These discrepancies persist even when peak intensities are similar, showing that a coherent appearance and plausible strength do not establish correct local evolution. 

Main Findings: Near-correct aggregate event frequencies can hide substantial errors across initial conditions and times. In the examined branch-separation case, 10/11 config-urations miss the persistent event group entirely, while the remaining configuration also predicts frequent events in the non-event group. Even when vortices remain recognizable and have sim-ilar peak intensities, their deformation and rotation can differ substantially from DNS. 

## 6 IMPLICATIONS FOR EVALUATING NEURAL PDE S OLVERS 

Our experiments reveal three gaps between predictive performance and learned dynamics: (1) lower trajectory error can coexist with inaccurate local updates, (2) similar ensemble spread can hide incorrect directions of evolution, and (3) matching event frequencies can conceal missed event-producing conditions. Although these gaps are only demonstrated on turbulent Kolmogorov flow (see Appendix A for limitations and future directions), these findings motivate a broader principle: 

training and evaluation should account for the physical dynamics that matter to the intended application .

Multi-step rollout training presents a related tension: states displaced by earlier prediction errors are trained toward the original reference trajectory, whose continuation may differ from their own. Perturbation scales, forecast intervals, and unroll lengths should therefore be evaluated for their effects on both prediction accuracy and the state evolution during rollout. 

Design benchmarks around physical systems and their uses. Datasets and benchmark platforms such as PDEBench (Takamoto et al., 2022), The Well (Ohana et al., 2024), BLASTNet (Chung et al., 2023), and PDEArena (Gupta & Brandstetter, 2022) broaden access to physical simulation data and tools for evaluating neural solvers. To make comparisons scientifically informative, benchmarks should specify whether the dynamics are deterministic or stochastic, whether the observed state is sufficient to determine its subsequent evolution, and which time scales and physical outcomes matter. These considerations should guide the inputs, forecast horizons, and evaluation criteria. For hazard prediction, for example, identifying event-producing conditions may be more important than a modest reduction in average field error. Broad coverage should therefore be accompanied by diagnostics tailored to each system and task. 

Make dynamical fidelity part of the evaluation standard. Evaluation should establish whether gains in prediction scores also improve relevant dynamical behavior. Our investigation provides three complementary checks: agreement of local updates with reference evolution, preservation of ensemble geometry, and reproduction of extreme events under the appropriate conditions. Other sys-tems may require different diagnostics, but the aim is the same: identify which physical behaviors improve and which remain distorted. For stochastic solvers, predictive-distribution quality and the dynamical fidelity of generated trajectories require separate assessment. 12 A DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK 

Our experiments are limited to two-dimensional Kolmogorov flow. This spatially periodic shear flow provides a simple setting for studying hydrodynamic instability (Meshalkin & Sinai, 1961) and the recurrent flow structures underlying turbulent motion (Chandler & Kerswell, 2013). Its tractable formulation makes it useful for examining learned dynamics, but results from one system do not establish how broadly the observed failures extend. Tests on additional dynamical systems may yield a more comprehensive understanding and reveal different relationships between prediction accuracy and dynamical fidelity. Our diagnostics identify how predicted dynamics depart from DNS, but do not establish which ar-chitectural or training choices cause these departures. Prior studies show that both model design and training objectives can affect rollout behavior (McCabe et al., 2023; Lippe et al., 2023). Establishing which factors account for the failures observed here requires further controlled comparisons. More broadly, future work should examine whether improvements in dynamical fidelity persist across systems and operating conditions, alongside gains in conventional predictive performance. 

## B RELATED WORK 

B.1 ENSEMBLE FORECASTING 

Forecasting with perturbed initial states. Leith (1974) studied finite Monte Carlo ensembles in a test-field model of two-dimensional turbulence to assess forecast skill when the atmosphere is incompletely observed. Ensemble-based estimates improved mean-square wind forecasts, in part by filtering unpredictable small-scale structure; the useful forecast range depended on observational resolution. In operational weather prediction, Toth & Kalnay (1993) used ensembles at NMC to represent growing analysis errors and obtain useful forecasts with few members. Their preliminary experiments found that two bred-perturbation forecasts could outperform substantially larger ran-dom or lagged-forecast ensembles. Ensembles also support investigations of dynamical sensitivity: Vela-Martín & Avila (2024) used DNS ensembles with nearby initial states in Kolmogorov flow to study the predictability of extreme dissipation bursts, connecting event predictability to large-scale circulation patterns. 

What the perturbed states represent. In operational forecasting, perturbed states primarily rep-resent uncertainty about the actual initial state. This uncertainty arises from measurement errors, incomplete observational coverage, and errors in the background forecast used in data assimilation (Leutbecher & Palmer, 2008). Perturbations therefore represent plausible errors of the estimated state, rather than measurement noise alone. Forecast-model errors can also affect that estimate through assimilation; unresolved processes, uncertain parameterizations, and numerical approxima-tions contribute further uncertainty during forecast evolution. Ensemble-based assimilation seeks to sample the initial uncertainty distribution, while dynamically constrained approaches select forecast-sensitive directions when that distribution cannot be reliably estimated (Leutbecher & Palmer, 2008). 

Perturbed states as dense probes of learned dynamics. Many neural PDE solvers are trained and evaluated on numerical simulation data, as in PDEBench and The Well (Takamoto et al., 2022; Ohana et al., 2024). When complete simulated states are supplied without observation noise, in-complete observations and measurement errors are absent from the learning problem. A numerical simulator may differ from the physical system it approximates, but that discrepancy does not itself make the simulator’s specified initial state or deterministic evolution uncertain. Errors in learning that evolution are a separate issue. Our motivation is to probe this learned evolution: we deliberately construct dense ensembles of nearby initial states and compare their evolution under each solver and the reference dynamics. These perturbations provide controlled probes of local dynamical behavior, rather than estimates of uncertainty about an unknown initial state. Their evolving errors, geometry, and extreme-event behavior reveal how the learned dynamics respond to nearby states. B.2 EVALUATION OF NEURAL PDE S OLVERS 

Large datasets and benchmarks. Evaluation suites examine neural solvers across equations, pa-rameters, and prediction horizons. PDEBench includes field errors, errors in conserved quantities 13 and boundary values, and errors separated into Fourier bands (Takamoto et al., 2022). PDEArena compares neural architectures and their generalization across PDE parameters and time scales (Gupta & Brandstetter, 2022). The Well reports variance-normalized errors for individual steps and rollout windows, and provides spectral diagnostics to resolve performance across spatial scales (Ohana et al., 2024). BLASTNet complements forecasting benchmarks with three-dimensional tur-bulence data and a volumetric super-resolution study (Chung et al., 2023). APEBench provides a configurable benchmark across PDE dynamics, architectures, and unrolled training procedures, in-cluding experiments connecting rollout accuracy to the learned numerical update (Koehler et al., 2024). 

Long-term statistics and dynamical invariants. Learned models have also been evaluated be-yond trajectory agreement. Park et al. (2024) provide an ergodic-theoretic account of why small prediction errors can coexist with inaccurate invariant statistics, and study how incorporating Jaco-bian information during training improves statistical accuracy. Pathak et al. (2017) used reservoir computing to reproduce the attractor statistics and estimate Lyapunov exponents of the Lorenz sys-tem and the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation. Jiang et al. (2023) studied neural operators trained on noisy Lorenz-96 and Kuramoto–Sivashinsky data using distributional and contrastive objectives. Their evaluation includes summary-statistic distributions and energy spectra, together with Lya-punov and attractor-dimension diagnostics for Lorenz-96; the results show improved long-term be-havior relative to training only on prediction error. DySLIM evaluates learned chaotic dynamics using distributional distances, energy spectra, spatial covariance, and temporal correlations (Schiff et al., 2024). Its invariant-measure regularization improves rollout stability and statistical agreement in the tested systems. For stochastic PDEs, Srikishan et al. (2026) introduce TRIE, which evalu-ates invariant-measure fidelity, predictive uncertainty calibration, and computational efficiency on stochastic Kuramoto–Sivashinsky and Kolmogorov systems. These studies evaluate learned dynam-ics through long-term statistics, dynamical invariants, and predictive distributions. 

Local dynamical properties of forecasts. Margazoglou & Magri (2023) evaluate whether echo state networks recover tangent-space dynamics, including covariant Lyapunov vectors, finite-time Lyapunov exponents, and angles between stable, neutral, and unstable subspaces. Fang & Mengaldo (2025) directly assess dynamical discrepancies in machine-learning forecasts of Lorenz, Kuramoto– Sivashinsky, and Kolmogorov systems. They estimate instantaneous dimension and inverse persis-tence from recurrences in a training-data reference attractor, relate these quantities to conventional forecast errors, and measure errors in the dynamical indices themselves. States with higher dimen-sion and lower persistence tend to have larger forecast errors, while recursive forecasts can progres-sively distort these local properties. These approaches characterize tangent-space stability and local attractor properties. Our ensemble diagnostics instead follow finite initial-state perturbations and compare their evolving spread and dominant spatial directions with those of the reference dynamics. 

## C DNS, D ATASET CONSTRUCTION , AND FORECAST PROTOCOL 

DNS Settings. DNS uses a 128 ×128 Fourier pseudospectral discretization on [0 , 2π)2, with vis-cosity ν = 0 .01 and an internal time step of 0.002 . The spectral update applies the two-thirds de-aliasing rule, removing modes with |kx| > 42 or |ky | > 42 . Vorticity fields are saved at intervals of ∆t = 0 .2, giving D = 128 2 spatial degrees of freedom. In this two-dimensional incompressible periodic flow, the spatial mean velocity is fixed at zero, and the velocity field is recovered from vor-ticity by solving the streamfunction Poisson equation (Vela-Martín & Avila, 2024). With the forcing and viscosity fixed, the full vorticity field therefore specifies the dynamical state and determines its subsequent reference evolution. 

Dataset and Perturbations. The archive provided by Vela-Martín & Avila (2024) contains 8, 192 

temporally decorrelated states of statistically stationary Kolmogorov flow. We sample 512 base initial states, each defining a case c. Around each base state, we generate spatially structured per-turbations from randomly signed differences between sampled archive states, rescaled to a spatial mean square of 10 −5. Each perturbed initial state defines an ensemble member m and is shared across DNS and the neural solvers; member 0 denotes the unperturbed trajectory. The perturbation generator forms δω = s(ωa − ωb), where s ∈ {− 1, +1 } and the two distinct archive states exclude the base state. It rescales each difference by 

q

10 −5/∥δω ∥2sp before adding 14 it to the base state, so the prescribed scale is a spatial mean square, not an RMS amplitude. The generation configuration uses balanced archive-state pairing and signs; ensemble diagnostics use the matched subset of 256 perturbed members rather than all generated members. We divide the cases into 416 for training, 48 for validation, and 48 for testing. Training and valida-tion use only unperturbed trajectories. Dynamical evaluation uses M = 256 perturbed members per test case, with a complete DNS reference trajectory for every member. The unperturbed member is excluded from ensemble statistics and evaluated separately using conventional forecast metrics. 

Forecast Origin and Stochastic Sampling. Perturbed initial states first evolve under DNS for ten saved time steps to provide input histories. Each solver receives its required input history from data steps 1–10 and predicts from data step 11 onward, using its own outputs for subsequent updates. Predictions are compared with DNS at matching times. Throughout the paper, prediction step 1 corresponds to data step 11. For stochastic solvers, all members within a case share a random path, with fresh draws at successive updates and different paths across cases, so that sampling differences do not confound responses to initial-state perturbations. We generate one predicted trajectory per initial state; experiments using independent randomness across members are identified separately.
