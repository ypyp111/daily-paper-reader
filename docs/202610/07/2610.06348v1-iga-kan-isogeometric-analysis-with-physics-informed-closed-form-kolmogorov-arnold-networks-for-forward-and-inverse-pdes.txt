Abstract 

Isogeometric analysis (IGA) solves partial differential equations accurately on exact NURBS geometry, whereas neural solvers are mesh-free but often orders of magnitude less accurate and typically trained by non-convex optimization without error control. We propose IGA–KAN, which uses local Kolmogorov– Arnold networks, fitted in closed form, to improve the IGA solution instead of replacing it. An IGA Galerkin solve produces uh; on every knot-vertex patch a Kolmogorov–Arnold ridge model is fitted to the strong form of the equation, the exact boundary data and uh, and the models are blended by IGA hat functions. With fixed inner functions the fit is one batched linear least-squares problem, without optimizer, learning rate or initialization. An a posteriori safeguard, motivated by a maximum-principle bound, decides where local models are used, keeping the IGA solution elsewhere. On eight benchmarks with exact solutions, five from the literature and one also posed on a domain fitted to a brain slice from MRI, the method reduces the error of IGA, at an unchanged number of Galerkin unknowns, by factors of 

4.2 to 90 in L2 and 4.1 to 220 in H1 on the reference meshes, and its L2 error is 6 to 6 × 10 4 times smaller than that of the best Kolmogorov–Arnold network trained from scratch on the same equations with a fixed budget. In an inverse problem it recovers an unknown constant source from one noise-free observation 167 

times more accurately than IGA. The gain is attributed to the superconvergence of local averages of the Galerkin solution. 

Keywords: Isogeometric Analysis, Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks, Scientific Machine Learning, Post-Processing, Superconvergence, Physics-Informed Learning, Inverse Problems 

1. Introduction 

Partial differential equations posed on curved geometries, whether described by CAD or segmented from medical images, are at the core of computational mechanics, and for them classical Galerkin discretizations offer what an engineering or clinical computation needs: convergence under refinement, error estimates and a cost that grows predictably with the number of unknowns. Isogeometric analysis (IGA) adds the exact geometry: it uses the NURBS description of a CAD geometry as the trial space of a Galerkin method, so that conic sections are represented exactly and the discrete functions are Cp−1 across knot spans [1, 2]. Its accuracy, however, is paid for in unknowns: at a given degree, a smaller error requires a finer mesh and a larger linear system. Machine learning has been proposed as a way to relax this trade-off. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) train a network on the residual of the equation at collocation points [3, 4]. They are mesh-free and differentiable, but their training is a non-convex optimization without error control, and its failure modes are well documented: spectral bias slows the learning of high frequencies, and the loss landscape becomes ill-conditioned as convection or reaction coefficients grow [5, 6, 7]. Asystematic comparison with the finite element method on linear and nonlinear model problems found that PINNs did not outperform it in either accuracy or solution time [8], and a systematic review of machine learning for fluid-related equations found that most articles claiming to outperform a standard numerical method had compared against a weak baseline [9]. For problems that classical solvers already handle well, the relevant question is therefore not whether a network can replace the solver, but whether it can add accuracy to the solution the solver produces.

Kolmogorov–Arnold networks (KANs) are a natural candidate for such a role, because their building block is close to that of isogeometric analysis: learnable univariate splines replace fixed activations [10], and other univariate families, rational functions in rKAN [11] and radial basis functions in Lynx-Net [12], have been proposed as well. So far, however, KANs have been used in partial differential equations in the same role as other networks: inside a physics-informed loss. KINN [13], across strong, energy and boundary-integral formulations, finds a KAN more accurate than a multilayer perceptron on singularities and discontinuous coefficients and far less affected by spectral bias, provided its spline grid is fine enough, but no more accurate than the perceptron on complex geometries, and PG-KINN [14] moves it to a Petrov–Galerkin weak form. On the isogeometric side, networks of other types produce the control net of an isogeometric space [15, 16, 17], which makes the geometry exact and the essential conditions strict, but bounds the accuracy by that of the discretization being imitated. In both lines the network is the solver: it inherits the non-convex training, and no classical solution is left to fall back on where its prediction is poor. Networks that do correct a classical solution, by super-resolution, solver-in-the-loop training or a fine-scale correction of a coarse finite element solution, are trained on reference solutions [18, 19, 20, 21], and the closest scheme that needs no such data, FEM-LSSVR [22], post-processes a low-order finite element solution element by element (Section 2). What is missing is a correction that needs no reference data, works on the exact geometry and is used only where computable tests support it, with the isogeometric solution kept everywhere else. Such a correction is possible because a Galerkin solution carries more accuracy than its pointwise values show. Its error oscillates on the scale of the elements, whereas, for degree p ≥ 2, its local averages are far more accurate: the error superconverges in negative norms, which classical post-processors exploit by convolution [23], and its derivatives superconverge at particular points, which superconvergent patch recovery exploits by local fitting [24] (Section 2.5). A smooth local model fitted to uh keeps the accurate averages and discards the oscillation; if it is also required to satisfy the equation and the exact boundary data, it restores the fine structure that the averages lack. A small KAN can serve as such a model, provided that it is used according to the following principle: 

Solve the problem classically; use the network only locally, only where its prediction passes a computable check, and with its inner functions fixed, so that fitting it is a linear problem. 

The last condition is a design choice of this paper, not a property of KANs: the KAN-based solvers above train all the univariate functions of the network together by non-convex optimization, whereas fixing the inner functions, and freezing or linearizing any nonlinear term at the classical solution (Section 3.6), turns the fit of the outer functions into a linear least-squares problem with a closed-form solution. The method follows this principle in three steps. A standard isogeometric Galerkin solve produces 

uh on the exact NURBS geometry. On every knot-vertex patch, one small Kolmogorov–Arnold model of 

ridge type (learnable univariate splines composed with fixed linear forms) is fitted to the strong form of the equation, to the exact Dirichlet data on the NURBS boundary crossing the patch and to uh itself. The local models are then blended by the degree-one hat functions of the isogeometric mesh. Because neighboring local models overlap, they can be cross-examined: an a posteriori safeguard decides, from computable quantities alone, where a local model is used. The isogeometric solution is never discarded: it enters every local fit as data and serves as the exact fallback of the safeguard. The method therefore retains the exact geometry and the Galerkin solution, and adds accuracy only where computable tests support a local model. We call the result IGA–KAN or, for short, the hybrid: not a neural solver, but an isogeometric solution with a safeguarded local post-process .We use the Kolmogorov–Arnold form for its structure, not for its expressivity. With the single-span outer splines used throughout, the local model spans exactly the polynomials of its degree, and on the first benchmark of Section 5 a monomial basis of the same space reproduces the results (Remark 1). In this paper the form is therefore a parametrization, and the accuracy comes from what the model is fitted to: the equation, the exact boundary data and uh. We keep the form for two reasons. Every derivative of a ridge function Φ( a · x) is a multiple of a derivative of the univariate spline Φ, so the equation rows of every operator in this paper, in two and in three dimensions, are assembled from univariate splines and their derivatives alone. And it places the local model in the Kolmogorov–Arnold family: its outer functions are B-splines, like the trial functions of IGA and the edge functions of a KAN, and can be given interior knots to leave the polynomial space while the fit stays linear, an option this paper does not use. Across the eight benchmarks of Section 5 the hybrid reduces the error of its input, at an unchanged number of unknowns of the Galerkin solve, by factors of 4.2 to 90 in the relative L2 norm and 4.1 to 220 

in the H1 norm on the reference meshes; the largest factors are those of the disk, whose quadratic solution lies in the local space, and on the six benchmarks with non-polynomial solutions, all but the disk and 2the thick ring, the factors are 4.2 to 27 and 4.1 to 148 . Its L2 error is also 6 to 6 × 10 4 times smaller than that of the best Kolmogorov–Arnold network we trained from scratch on the same equations with a fixed training protocol; these factors describe that protocol, not the best accuracy such networks can reach (Section 5). With the settings used throughout, the hybrid did not return a larger error than its input, beyond round-off, on any mesh of these benchmarks. Improving a Galerkin solution by local fitting is a classical idea (Section 2.5). What this paper adds is a local fit that also uses the equation and the exact geometry, takes the form of a Kolmogorov–Arnold model computed in closed form and is used only where computable tests support it, together with an analysis of its mechanism and numerical evidence on eight benchmarks. Specifically, the paper makes the following four contributions. 1. A closed-form local Kolmogorov–Arnold post-process for isogeometric solutions. The local model (2) has fixed inner functions and Bernstein outer functions, so its fit (4) to the equation, the boundary data and uh is one batched linear least-squares solve over all patches. The same construction covers advection–diffusion–reaction operators, the space–time heat operator, nonlinear terms frozen at or linearized about the classical solution, and triangularly coupled systems. 2. An a posteriori safeguard with an exact fallback. Four computable tests (neighbor consensus, the size of the correction, a residual indicator and a p-consistency test) decide patch by patch whether a local model is used; where it is not, the isogeometric solution is kept. For elliptic operators the constant of the residual indicator is taken from a maximum-principle bound on the error of a local model (Proposition 2). 3. An analysis of the mechanism. The post-process is a local average whose kernel is chosen by the equation; Propositions 1 and 2 bound the error of the blended solution by the residuals of the local models and by their errors on the patch boundaries, for which the residual and consensus tests are computable surrogates, and the analysis identifies the regimes in which the gain vanishes. 4. Numerical evidence on eight benchmarks with exact solutions , five of them taken from published work, on curved and complex NURBS geometries in two and three dimensions and on a ring-shaped domain fitted to a brain slice segmented from magnetic resonance images, with a comparison against isogeometric analysis and against Kolmogorov–Arnold networks (KAN, KINN) and the published results of PI-GGN, KINN and PG-KINN. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature, Section 3 states the method, Section 4 analyzes why it works and when the gain vanishes, Section 5 gives the numerical examples, and Section 6 concludes and lists directions for further work. 

2. Related work 

2.1. Isogeometric analysis 

Isogeometric analysis is a Galerkin method on the spline or NURBS space that already describes the CAD geometry [1, 2]. Two properties matter here. The map F : bΩ = (0 , 1) d → Ω is fixed at the coarsest mesh and unchanged by refinement, so a point can be placed on ∂Ω exactly; and k-refinement gives Cp−1

spaces of degree p at roughly one unknown per element, (n+p)2 on an n×n mesh in two dimensions against the (np + 1) 2 of the C0 finite element space of the same degree and mesh, with established approximation properties, near-optimality against Sobolev n-widths and far more faithful operator spectra [25, 26, 27]. That smoothness is what makes the Galerkin error amenable to local averaging (Section 2.5). 

2.2. Physics-informed neural networks and their failure modes 

Collocating a differential operator on a neural ansatz is an old idea, revived as the physics-informed neural network (PINN) [3, 4]. Its difficulties are well documented. The eigenvector bias of the neural tangent kernel slows high-frequency learning, and the residual and boundary loss terms converge at very different rates [5, 6]. The residual loss landscape becomes ill-conditioned as a convection or reaction coefficient grows even when the PINN can represent the solution, so that the failure is optimization rather than expressivity [7]. Penalty boundary conditions hold only approximately and add a weight to be tuned; the alternative is to build them into the ansatz [28]. For linear equations, randomized networks avoid the non-convex training altogether: the hidden-layer parameters are drawn at random and frozen, and the output weights follow from one linear least-squares problem. The physics-informed extreme learning machine applies this to linear equations, with one network or, in a distributed variant, one per cell [29]. Local extreme learning machines assign one such network to each sub-domain, impose Ck continuity across the sub-domain boundaries and treat nonlinear equations 3by nonlinear least squares, which can be trapped in local minima without random perturbations [30], and the random feature method pieces local random-feature models together with a partition of unity [31]; both report accuracy on par with classical solvers. All three are stand-alone solvers, with random features and a least-squares problem coupled over the whole spatial domain. 

2.3. Kolmogorov–Arnold networks 

A KAN replaces the fixed activations of a multilayer perceptron (MLP) by learnable univariate B-splines on the graph edges [10]. Which univariate family those edge functions are drawn from is itself an open question, and rational ones, Padé approximants and rational Jacobi functions, have been proposed in place of the splines [11]. The transfer to PDEs has mostly substituted a KAN for the MLP inside an existing physics-informed loss. KINN reports gains on singularities and discontinuous coefficients, together with a loss of accuracy on geometrically complex domains [13]. MLP and KAN have been compared in a controlled setting [32]. The same substitution reaches operator learning and point-cloud regression [33, 34]. Two recent schemes move toward variational forms, the second of them, PG-KINN, with an independent, compactly supported piecewise-polynomial test space [35, 14]. Two of the authors of PG-KINN are authors of the present paper. Outside the Kolmogorov–Arnold family, PI-GGN trains a graph network on a finite-element Galerkin residual, for forward and inverse problems alike [36]. Its disk benchmark is one of ours (Section 5.1.2). All of these use the network as the discretization; we are not aware of work that keeps an isogeometric Galerkin solution as the substrate and uses KANs only as a local post-process. 

2.4. Classical solver plus learned correction 

Closest in intent is the hybrid FEM-LSSVR scheme, in which element-wise least-squares support vector regression with higher-order Legendre kernels interpolates, under the PDE, between the nodal values of a low-order finite element solve and stays consistent with them at the element boundaries [22]. Its local fit is convex and closed-form, and the same principle, a fixed feature map followed by one linear least-squares solve with the equation in the rows, is what makes the fit of Section 3 linear. Super-resolution of PDE fields, learned discretizations and closures, and solver-in-the-loop corrections are trained on reference data and carry no guarantee on an unseen field [18, 37, 38, 39, 19]. Networks have also predicted the fine-grid fluctuation above a coarse multigrid solution [40, 21]. Others have corrected a coarse finite element solution, enriched a Lagrange space and generated element stiffness matrices [20, 41, 42]. The isogeometric proposals so far are surrogates that reproduce an IGA solution faster: a network produces the spline coefficients, so accuracy is bounded above by the discretization being imitated [15, 16, 17]. 

2.5. Post-processing and superconvergence 

The mechanism our method relies on is classical. A local average of a Galerkin solution against a compactly supported kernel is accurate to O(h2p) in the interior of a locally translation-invariant mesh for degree-p elements, although the solution itself is only O(hp+1 ), because the error superconverges in negative norms; for p = 1 the two rates coincide and no order is gained [23]. The same holds for derivatives [43]. An early tensor-product instance and a collection of the theory are also available [44, 45]. The convolution post-processor reappears as the smoothness-increasing accuracy-conserving (SIAC) filter for discontinuous Galerkin fields [46, 47]. On the first benchmark of Section 5, a SIAC filter implemented for comparison is 32 

times cheaper than the hybrid on the reference mesh and reduces the IGA error by more on an interior box than the hybrid does globally, but globally its L2 error is about ten times that of the unfiltered IGA solution, because its kernel must be shifted near the boundary. A second classical family recovers a better field by fitting rather than by convolution, and our per-patch fit belongs to it. The superconvergent patch recovery (SPR) fits a higher-order polynomial by least squares to the derivatives sampled at superconvergent points of a nodal patch [24]. The equilibrium residual and the boundary conditions have been added to that patch problem [48, 49], the patch polynomials have been blended by the nodal shape functions [50], and SPR-C imposes equilibrium and boundary conditions as constraints [51]. Polynomial-preserving recovery recovers the solution rather than its gradient [52], and patch recovery has been carried over to adaptive isogeometric analysis as an error estimator [53]. Our fit combines these ingredients: a recovery of the solution itself, with the strong-form residual and the boundary data in its rows, blended by a partition of unity [54]. What is new is their use as a solution post-process on exact NURBS geometry (boundary rows on the exact NURBS trace, patches at knot vertices, a periodic seam and a collapsed side identified), the Kolmogorov–Arnold ridge parametrization, which keeps every operator of Section 3.6 univariate, and the 

a posteriori acceptance filter of Section 3.5. The equation in the rows is what carries the gain: on the first benchmark of Section 5 the fit without the physics rows gains a factor of 4.0 in L2 where the full fit gains 

21 (Remark 1). For splines the superconvergent points are known and yield optimal-order collocation, so the pointwise structure the classical theory predicts is present in the isogeometric setting [55, 56]. 42.6. Positioning of the present method 

This is not a neural solver: a classical isogeometric solve is performed first and never discarded, and the learned object is a family of small local models that post-process it, fitted by one batched linear least-squares problem, with no optimizer, no learning rate and no local minimum. Relative to FEM-LSSVR, whose principle of a fixed feature map and one linear system we adopt, we post-process an isogeometric solution on exact NURBS geometry, replace the Legendre kernel by a Kolmogorov–Arnold ridge model, and fit on overlapping knot-vertex patches blended by a partition of unity instead of element by element [22]. The randomized solvers above share the linear least-squares fit for linear equations; here the inner functions are fixed directions instead of random draws, the patch problems are small and independent of one another instead of coupled over the whole domain, the isogeometric solution enters the rows as data, and nonlinear terms are frozen or linearized at it, so that the fit stays linear (Section 3) [29, 30, 31]. Unlike the KAN solvers for PDEs, our KAN never has to represent the solution globally or resolve a singularity on its own. With the settings of this paper it spans exactly the polynomials of its degree (Remark 1), so what we claim is the safeguarded closed-form post-process built from it, not a gain in expressivity due to the Kolmogorov–Arnold form. An a posteriori safeguard using no exact solution then accepts a correction, refits it on a smaller support, or discards it, patch by patch, so where none is accepted the hybrid returns the isogeometric solution. The safeguard is a computable filter built on the error bound of Section 4.1; with the settings used throughout it did not leave the hybrid with a larger error than its input on the benchmarks of Section 5. Because the mechanism is the superconvergence of local averages, an L2 gain that grows under refinement requires p ≥ 2: at p = 1 the two rates of Section 2.5 coincide, and on the thick ring (Section 5.1.3) the measured L2 gain at p = 1 is smaller than at p = 2 and p = 3 and decreases between the two meshes computed, from 2.8 to 2.0 (Fig. 8a), while the H1 gain does not decrease. What improves is accuracy per degree of freedom of the Galerkin solve rather than the cost of a single solve. 

3. The hybrid IGA–KAN method 

This section states the method. It starts from an isogeometric solve on the exact geometry, whose Galerkin solution is uh, and returns a solution on the same geometry that is built like a piecewise multilinear spline on the isogeometric mesh, except that each nodal value is replaced, wherever a computable test accepts it, by a small local function fitted in the least-squares sense to the equation, the exact boundary data and uh (Section 3.4). The method has three steps: 1. Isogeometric solve (Section 3.1): a standard Galerkin solve on the exact NURBS geometry gives uh.2. Local Kolmogorov–Arnold fit (Sections 3.2 and 3.3): on every knot-vertex patch, a Kolmogorov– Arnold model of ridge type, with fixed inner functions, is fitted to the strong form of the equation, the exact boundary data and uh; all patches are fitted together in one batched linear least-squares solve. 3. Partition-of-unity blend (Section 3.4): the local models are combined by the degree-one hat functions of the isogeometric mesh. An a posteriori safeguard (Section 3.5) decides, patch by patch and without the exact solution, whether a local model is used; a patch whose model is not accepted contributes uh itself to the blend. Section 3.6 lists the operators to which the construction applies, and Section 3.7 its cost. We call the complete method IGA–KAN or, for short, the hybrid, and its second part (fit, safeguard and blend, without the solve) the post-process. Figure 1 compares the method with classical isogeometric analysis and with a stand-alone Kolmogorov–Arnold network, and Figure 3 shows its stages.

4. Analysis: why a smooth local model can improve the Galerkin solution it is fitted to 

The error of the Galerkin solution is orthogonal to the discrete space in the sense of the bilinear form (for a symmetric coercive problem the solution is the best approximation in the energy norm), so it oscillates on the element scale, while the local averages of uh are far more accurate: for smooth data a duality argument gives the negative-norm estimates ∥u − uh∥H−s(Ω) ≤ C h p+1+ s∥u∥Hp+1 (Ω) for 0 ≤ s ≤ p − 1, provided the adjoint problem is Hs+2 -regular, and the Bramble–Schatz post-processes exploit this to recover a higher order by convolution with a compactly supported kernel [23]. The post-process is a local average whose kernel is determined by the equation . Of the three blocks of (4), the data rows tie Kv to uh only weakly ( wdata = 0 .1): a least-squares fit of a smooth model to oscillatory data keeps its local averages and discards the oscillation, so Kv inherits only the accurate low-frequency content. At a re-entrant corner or a crack tip the isogeometric error is a genuine local defect, not an element-scale oscillation with accurate averages, and the singular part of the solution is not approximated to high order by Pdloc on a patch that contains the singularity; the residual test (10) is also uninformative there when f ≡ 0, which is the reason for the p-consistency test (11). None of the benchmarks of Section 5 has a singularity, so the safeguard has been assessed on smooth solutions only and no claim is made here about its behavior at a singularity; enriching the local space for such solutions is left to future work (Section 6). With the settings used throughout, the safeguarded hybrid did not return a larger error than the isogeo-metric solution it post-processed, beyond round-off, on any mesh of the benchmarks of Section 5, the 24 

three-dimensional runs with local degrees 6, 8 and 10 included (Section 5.1.3). 

5. Numerical experiments 

We demonstrate the IGA–KAN hybrid on eight benchmarks: three Poisson problems on curved NURBS domains, one of them in three dimensions and one also posed as an inverse problem, the Laplace equation on a complex geometry, the nonlinear Bratu equation, the Fisher–Kolmogorov equation of protein spreading, on the unit square and on a domain fitted to a brain slice segmented from magnetic resonance images, a high-frequency heat equation and a stiff coupled advection–diffusion–reaction system. Five of them are taken from published work (the disk of PI-GGN [36], the flower and the high-frequency heat equation of KINN [13], the Fisher–Kolmogorov test of Corti et al. [59], and the coupled system of Asmouh and Ostermann [60]), so that the hybrid can be compared with published numbers where they exist (PI-GGN, KINN, PG-KINN); the thick ring is the three-dimensional geometry of GeoPDEs [61]. The methods compared are the following. IGA is the classical isogeometric Galerkin solve uh on the exact NURBS geometry [1]. IGA–KAN is the hybrid: the same solve, followed on every knot-vertex patch 13 by one small Kolmogorov–Arnold ridge model fitted in closed form to the strong form of the equation, to the exact boundary data and to uh, the local models being blended by the hat functions and screened by an 

a posteriori safeguard. KAN is a stand-alone physics-informed Kolmogorov–Arnold network [10] trained on the strong form [4], [2 , 10 , 10 , 1] with grid 6, and KINN is the same network built from the layer of KINN [13]; both are trained by Adam followed by L-BFGS, 1000 + 200 iterations, or 2000 + 400 on the disk and, for the KAN, on the thick ring. In all these runs the boundary and initial data are imposed by a penalty of weight 100 , and Adam starts from the learning rate 5 × 10 −3, decayed to 10 −4; these settings were not adjusted to the individual problems. The one exception is the heat equation, on which all networks follow KINN’s own protocol (Section 5.4.2). KINN’s layer is run on the quarter annulus, the disk, the Bratu equation, the Fisher–Kolmogorov test on the unit square, the heat equation and the coupled system; on the thick ring, the flower and the brain slice only the KAN is reported, and on the flower the errors of KINN are the published ones. The hybrid uses the same settings in every two-dimensional example: 12 

ridge directions, local degree 9, 12 Gauss points per direction per patch; the three-dimensional example uses 45 directions, degree 8 and 7 points. 

Scope of the comparison with the networks. The networks and the hybrid do not solve the same task: each network solves the equation from scratch, without access to uh, whereas the hybrid post-processes an isogeometric solution. The network rows of the tables therefore show what a Kolmogorov–Arnold network trained by a fixed physics-informed protocol delivers on these equations; they do not show the best accuracy these architectures can reach with tuning to the problem, longer training or adaptive sampling. Within this scope, the comparison is set up and reported as follows. First, on every benchmark on which we train both, KAN and KINN share the widths, the grid, the loss, the optimizer and the budget, and differ only in the layer, whose per-edge spline scale gives KINN up to 10% more parameters. Second, the KINN entries computed here are the median of three training seeds, and so are the KAN entries on the disk, the flower and the Fisher–Kolmogorov and heat equations and the perceptron of the heat equation; on the quarter annulus, the thick ring, the Bratu equation and the coupled system the KAN entry is a single seed; IGA and IGA–KAN are deterministic. Third, where a network has not resolved the solution within the budget, this is stated with the result: on the Fisher–Kolmogorov equation neither network has converged at the end of training (Section 5.4.1); on the heat equation the perceptron and the KAN built from our layer return 

u ≈ 0, and the hybrid is compared with the KAN built from KINN’s layer (Section 5.4.2); on the coupled system both networks remain at errors of order 0.4 (Section 5.4.3). Fourth, published results are quoted as reported, each in its own error measure. Fifth, the factors over the networks quoted in Sections 1 and 6 are taken against the more accurate network on each benchmark. They range from 6 on the disk with 25 

control points, where both networks are 13 to 15 times more accurate than IGA in L2, to 6 × 10 4 on the coupled system; against the KAN alone on the Fisher–Kolmogorov square the factor would be 2 × 10 5.The sizes in the tables are not matched: those of the networks count trainable parameters, those of IGA and IGA–KAN the Galerkin unknowns, to which the post-process adds 120 local coefficients per knot vertex ( 405 in three dimensions), each set determined by its own least-squares problem. The like-for-like comparisons of the hybrid are therefore with IGA at the same number of Galerkin unknowns, which is its input, on every benchmark (at equal computing time, mesh refinement can be cheaper in L2,Section 3.7), and, on the quarter annulus, with two classical post-processors of the same uh: a SIAC filter (Section 2.5) and a patch recovery of uh with the boundary data, that is, the same fit without the physics rows (Remark 1). 24 6. Conclusions and future work 

We kept the classical solver and used a Kolmogorov–Arnold network only where its prediction passes a computable check. A standard isogeometric Galerkin solve on the exact NURBS geometry provides uh;on every knot-vertex patch one small Kolmogorov–Arnold ridge model is fitted to the strong form of the equation, to the exact boundary data and to uh, in one batched linear least-squares problem, and an aposteriori safeguard decides patch by patch whether the local model may replace the isogeometric solution. The post-process therefore has no optimizer, no learning rate and no initialization, and wherever no local model is accepted the isogeometric solution is kept. We tested the method on eight benchmarks: three Poisson problems on curved domains, one of them three-dimensional and one also inverse, the Laplace equation on a complex geometry, the nonlinear Bratu equation, the Fisher–Kolmogorov equation on the unit square and on a domain fitted to a brain slice from magnetic resonance images, a high-frequency heat equation and a stiff coupled advection–diffusion– reaction system. On these benchmarks the hybrid reduced the error of the IGA solution it post-processes by factors of 4.2 to 90 in L2 and 4.1 to 220 in H1 on the reference meshes (the upper ends on the disk, whose solution is a quadratic polynomial; 4.2 to 27 and 4.1 to 148 on the six benchmarks with non-polynomial solutions, all but the disk and the thick ring), at an unchanged number of unknowns of the Galerkin solve and at 4 to about 500 times its time, and its L2 error was 6 to 6 × 10 4 times smaller than that of the best Kolmogorov–Arnold network we trained from scratch on the same equations with a fixed training protocol; these factors describe that protocol, not the best accuracy such networks can reach. On the disk of PI-GGN and on the flower of KINN and PG-KINN it is more accurate than the published results, and on the disk it identifies an unknown source from a single observation by one division, with an error 167 

times smaller than IGA. The analysis of Section 4 attributes these gains to the superconvergence of local averages of the Galerkin solution, which the local fit recovers with a kernel chosen by the equation, and bounds the error of the blended solution by the residual of the local models, which the safeguard computes, and by their error on the patch boundaries, for which the consensus of overlapping patches is a computable surrogate. With the settings used throughout, the safeguarded hybrid did not return a larger error than the isogeometric solution, beyond round-off, on any mesh of the benchmarks. 

Future work. The results suggest the following directions. 1. Efficiency. The fits of different patches are independent, so the post-process maps directly onto a GPU, and on the first benchmark a non-redundant basis of the local polynomial space reproduced the result at a quarter of the cost; how far the two together reduce the cost of the post-process remains to be measured. 2. Coarse meshes. On coarse meshes (up to about three elements per wavelength on the heat equation, and on the coarsest mesh of the coupled system) the safeguard returns the isogeometric solution. The patches that are not accepted mark where a smooth local model fails the tests, and this map needs no exact solution, which suggests the safeguard as a solve-free indicator for adaptive refinement; coupling the two, and choosing the patch size, the local degree and the number of sample points automatically (in three dimensions the gain depends on them, Section 5.1.3), is a way to obtain gains in this regime. 3. Non-smooth solutions. Corner and crack singularities and material interfaces are outside a polynomial local space. Enriching the local models with the singular functions, where the exponent is known, or combining the post-process with graded meshes, is the natural extension. Outer splines with interior knots, which leave the polynomial space while the fit stays linear, are the part of the Kolmogorov– Arnold form not used in the method (on the quarter annulus they change the errors by less than 5% ,Remark 1); whether they help near layers and interfaces remains to be tested. 4. Vector-valued and higher-order problems. The ridge identities extend to linear elasticity and to fourth-order operators; what is missing there are counterparts of the residual and p-consistency tests, without which the safeguard does not carry over as it stands. 5. Sharper guarantees. Replacing the consensus test by a computable bound on the boundary term of (14) would turn the safeguard from a filter into a guaranteed error estimate, and a quantitative account of the gain in terms of the negative-norm estimates would predict it a priori ; both could also relax the p-consistency test, which is conservative where the right-hand side vanishes (Section 5.2). 6. Noisy data. In the inverse problem of Section 5.1.2 the advantage of the hybrid decreases once the noise in the observation approaches the discretization error; a noise-aware weighting of the data rows is the natural remedy. 25 Code and data availability 

The code and the result files of this study are openly available at https://github.com/Sima-Naraghi/iga-kan . The repository contains the Python library igakan (the isogeometric solvers, the local Kolmogorov–Arnold fit with its safeguard, and the stand-alone network baselines); one executed Jupyter notebook per benchmark, which solves it with IGA and IGA–KAN and evaluates the network baselines; the scripts that produce every table and figure of this paper, including those that train the networks; and the result files (CSV, JSON and NPZ, with the logs and, for the flower, the Fisher–Kolmogorov equation, the heat equation and the coupled system, the trained network weights) from which every number reported here is read.
