## ABSTRACT 

Automated agentic workflow optimization relies on costly evaluations, making it essential to allocate a limited evaluation budget effectively. Multi-parent fusion can reuse designs from previously discovered workflows, but identifying promising parent combinations requires learning from limited fusion feedback. We introduce 

DAGO (Directed Acyclic Graph Optimization), a contextual-bandit-guided frame-work that learns which parent workflows to fuse under a limited evaluation budget. DAGO formulates each candidate parent combination as an arm , represented by pretrained embeddings of its constituent workflows’ code and prompts. A diagonal LinUCB policy learns a shared reward model across arms and balances exploitation of arms with high predicted offspring quality against uncertainty-driven exploration. After an arm is selected, an LLM generates a child workflow through summary-guided fusion, and the child’s validation score serves as the reward for updating the bandit. A shared directed acyclic graph maintains discovered workflows and their multi-parent lineage, providing an expanding pool of parents for subsequent arm proposals. Across six benchmarks covering mathematical reasoning, code generation, and question answering, DAGO achieves the highest macro-average score among the evaluated baselines. Under matched validation-evaluation bud-gets, it improves over AFlow from 80.3 to 81.7 while reducing aggregate search expenditure by 11.2%. Ablation studies show that LinUCB-guided arm selection outperforms both random selection and its exploration-free variant, supporting the value of feedback-driven selection and exploration–exploitation balance. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Agentic workflows coordinate large language model (LLM) calls, tools, and control-flow logic to solve complex tasks. Automating their design turns workflow construction into a search problem over executable programs. ADAS generates new agentic systems by conditioning on an archive of previous discoveries (Hu et al., 2025), while AFlow explores workflow code through a variant of Monte Carlo Tree Search (Zhang et al., 2025d). In these settings, assessing a candidate requires executing its LLM calls and tools on validation examples, making search evaluations costly. Under a limited evaluation budget, an optimizer must therefore decide not only how to generate new workflows, but also which generation decisions are worth evaluating. Multi-parent fusion offers a way to reuse useful designs from previously discovered workflows. For example, a workflow with effective problem decomposition may provide useful guidance for improving another workflow with a strong verification procedure. Existing approaches already explore workflow recombination: EvoFlow uses LLM-based crossover (Zhang et al., 2025a), and MermaidFlow introduces constraint-preserving crossover over workflow graphs (Zheng et al., 2025). However, an effective fusion operator does not by itself determine which parents should be fused. High individual validation scores do not guarantee that a parent combination will produce a strong child, and the number of possible combinations grows combinatorially as more workflows are discovered. Evaluating every combination is impractical, while selecting parent combinations at random does not use past fusion outcomes to guide future choices. The central challenge is thus to learn which combinations merit the next expensive fusion and evaluation. This motivates our research question: Under a limited evaluation budget, how can we learn from past fusion outcomes to select promising parent workflow combinations? We introduce DAGO

(Directed Acyclic Graph Optimization), a contextual-bandit-guided framework that makes this selection problem explicit. An arm is a selectable action in a bandit problem; here, it corresponds to a candidate combination of parent workflows to fuse. The child’s validation score serves as the reward, rather than an aggregate of the parents’ existing scores. This distinction directs learning toward the quality of the generated workflow, rather than merely favoring parents that already perform well individually. To learn from limited feedback, DAGO uses a shared linear reward model across arms instead of learning an independent reward estimate for every combination. Each workflow is represented by pretrained embeddings of its code and prompts, and an arm’s features concatenate its parent embeddings in descending order of validation score. At each iteration, annealed score-based sampling proposes a manageable set of candidate arms without enumerating the full combination space. A diagonal LinUCB policy (Li et al., 2010; Chu et al., 2011) then selects an arm by combining predicted child-workflow quality with an uncertainty-driven exploration bonus. The shared representation allows feedback from evaluated arms to inform the ranking of newly proposed arms, while the exploration bonus encourages evaluating alternatives beyond those with the highest current predictions. The diagonal approximation keeps arm scoring and online updates lightweight. Thus, parent scores guide candidate proposal , whereas observed fusion rewards guide arm selection , allocating evaluation opportunities through an explicit exploration–exploitation mechanism. DAGO embeds this decision process in a workflow-generation loop. A shared directed acyclic graph (DAG) stores discovered workflows and their multi-parent generation lineage, providing an expanding pool of parents for subsequent arm proposals. This DAG records the search history, not the execution graph within an individual workflow. After the bandit selects an arm, an LLM summarizes the parents’ strengths and constraints and generates a child through summary-guided fusion, starting from the strongest parent and applying targeted modifications informed by the selected workflows. The child’s validation score updates the bandit, and the child is added to the DAG. At the end of search, DAGO returns the workflow with the highest validation score for reuse on the target task; arm selection occurs during optimization rather than separately for each test query. We evaluate DAGO on six benchmarks spanning mathematical reasoning (GSM8K and MATH), code generation (HumanEval and MBPP), and question answering (HotpotQA and DROP). ## 2 RELATED WORK 

Automated Workflow Optimization and Recombination. Automated workflow optimization spans prompt refinement and structural search. DSPy (Khattab et al., 2024), TextGrad (Yuksekgonul et al., 2025), and MIPRO (Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024) improve prompts in modular LLM programs, while GPTSwarm (Zhuge et al., 2024) and G-Designer (Zhang et al., 2025c) optimize multi-agent graphs. ADAS (Hu et al., 2025) searches executable programs using an archive of discoveries, and AFlow (Zhang et al., 2025d) refines code workflows through an MCTS variant with score-based mixed-probability selection. JudgeFlow (Ma et al., 2026) targets problematic blocks using execution diagnostics. ScoreFlow (Wang et al., 2025) and FlowReasoner (Gao et al., 2025) train workflow generators through preference optimization and reinforcement learning, respectively. Multi-parent recombination is also explored: EvoFlow (Zhang et al., 2025a) retrieves query-relevant parents for LLM-based crossover, whereas MermaidFlow (Zheng et al., 2025) applies constraint-preserving crossover to workflow graphs. Building on these approaches, DAGO focuses on learning which parent combinations to fuse from past fusion outcomes, allocating a limited evaluation budget to promising generation decisions rather than relying solely on parent scores or query relevance. 

Bandit-Guided Selection under Limited Evaluation Budgets. Bandits provide a framework for allocating evaluations through exploration–exploitation. OPTS (Ashizawa et al., 2025) applies Thompson sampling to prompt-design strategy selection, while TRIPLE (Shi et al., 2024) formalizes prompt selection as fixed-budget best-arm identification. MBA-RAG (Tang et al., 2025) selects retrieval strategies, and PILOT (Panda et al., 2025) uses contextual bandits for budget-constrained model routing. Linear contextual bandits (Li et al., 2010; Chu et al., 2011) leverage observed features for reward prediction and uncertainty-aware selection. DAGO applies this principle to workflow fusion: each arm corresponds to a parent workflow combination, and its reward is the generated 3child’s validation score, not the parents’ existing scores. Unlike query-time routing among existing models, arm selection guides the generation of new workflows during task-level search. As newly discovered workflows introduce additional arms, the selector must learn from sparse feedback in an expanding search space. A shared linear reward model over pretrained code-and-prompt embeddings allows feedback from evaluated arms to inform newly proposed arms, while diagonal LinUCB balances predicted child quality with uncertainty-driven exploration. This addresses the growing combination space without separately learning each arm’s reward or exhaustively evaluating all combinations. 

## 3 PRELIMINARIES 

Workflows and validation feedback. A workflow w = ( C, P) comprises executable orchestration code and its internal prompts. Following the code-based representation of AFlow (Zhang et al., 2025d), C implements an asynchronous __call__ method that maps a task instance to an output through LLM calls, tools, and control flow. Let Eval q (w) ∈ [0 , 1] be its validation score averaged over q executions of the fixed validation set. A stored score s(v) is an observed evaluation, not the unknown expected performance of that workflow. Validation feedback is available during search; test data are not used for generation, arm selection, or stopping. 

Workflow archive and fusion arms. The archive is a DAG Gt = ( Vt, Et) whose nodes store code, prompts, scores, and cached embeddings. An edge records that a parent was supplied when generating a child; it does not certify that a specific component was inherited. The DAG records generation lineage, not workflow execution. Each arm a = ( v(1) , . . . , v (J)) specifies J distinct parents, canonically ordered by decreasing score, with node identifiers breaking ties. Let ¯At contain all such arms available before round t, and let At ⊆ ¯At be the proposed candidates. We use a fixed arm size with N ≥ J initial workflows. ## 4 METHODOLOGY 

DAGO (Directed Acyclic Graph Optimization) learns which parent workflow combinations to evaluate through a shared, LinUCB-style selection rule. Each round separates inexpensive candidate proposal and scoring from expensive child generation and evaluation. The DAG supplies reusable parents, the fusion operator generates a child, and bandit feedback guides which arm receives the next evaluation. Lineage is retained for provenance; graph topology itself is not an additional input to the arm scorer in this implementation.

This is a lightweight reward surrogate : its additive form does not explicitly model interactions between different parents, and pretrained embeddings do not make LLM fusion linearly realizable.

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmarks. We evaluate DAGO on six benchmarks: GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021) for mathematical reasoning, HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) and MBPP (Austin et al., 2021) for code generation, and HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018) and DROP (Dua et al., 2019) for question answering. We follow AFlow’s dataset splits and evaluation protocols (Zhang et al., 2025d), restricting MATH to level-5 problems. Validation data are used for search and workflow selection; final results are reported on the disjoint test splits. 

Baselines. We compare with prompting and answer-refinement methods: IO, CoT (Wei et al., 2022), CoT-SC (Wang et al., 2023), MedPrompt (Nori et al., 2023), MultiPersona (Wang et al., 2024), Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023), and GoT (Besta et al., 2024). We also include answer ensembling with Blender (Jiang et al., 2023), dynamic agent collaboration with DyLAN (Liu et al., 2023), and automated workflow optimization with GPTSwarm (Zhuge et al., 2024), ADAS (Hu et al., 2025), MaAS (Zhang et al., 2025b), and AFlow (Zhang et al., 2025d). AFlow is our main reference for single-parent workflow search. Blender aggregates candidate answers at inference time, whereas DAGO fuses executable workflows during search. 

Metrics. We report solve rate (%) on GSM8K and MATH, pass@1 (%) on HumanEval and MBPP (Chen et al., 2021), and F1 (%) on HotpotQA and DROP. The overall score is the unweighted mean across benchmarks. Unless otherwise stated, each validation-selected workflow is fixed and executed three times on the same held-out test split; we report the mean test score. These repetitions are test executions, not independent optimization runs.

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We presented DAGO, a framework for LLM-based workflow optimization that learns which parent workflows to fuse from the validation feedback of their offspring. By treating parent combinations as bandit arms, DAGO uses a shared reward predictor and a diagonal UCB-style policy to guide selection, while a DAG archive and summary-guided fusion support the reuse and refinement of discovered workflows. Across six benchmarks spanning mathematical reasoning, code generation, and question answering, DAGO achieves the highest macro-average score among the evaluated methods. Under the same validation-evaluation budget cap as AFlow, it improves the macro-average score from 80 .3 to 81 .7 while reducing aggregate search expenditure by 11 .2% . Ablations further support the value of feedback-guided selection and exploration, alongside the benefits of multi-parent fusion. Together, these results highlight feedback-guided parent selection as a key component of effective workflow fusion: prior search outcomes inform not only which workflows are available for reuse, but also which combinations to explore next.

A.7 EVIDENCE AND FALSIFIABLE DIAGNOSTICS 

The existing random-arm and ν = 0 ablations assess whether feedback-guided selection and the exploration term help end-to-end search under the reported protocol. They do not estimate gt or εt,prove linearity, or calibrate ht as a confidence interval. The following diagnostics specify additional tests; no new numerical results from these tests are claimed here .18 Prequential prediction records. Save every candidate arm, its features, prediction, exploration proxy, proposal seed, selected arm, and eventual reward before updating the model. Evaluate predictions against subsequently observed rewards, not against the same rewards after refitting. Selected-arm residuals are useful but do not reveal errors on unselected arms or certify the residual span. 

Frozen-archive, multi-arm probes. At selected checkpoints, freeze the archive, candidate collec-tion, model state, prompts, and execution settings. Allocate a separately reported diagnostic budget to multiple randomly sampled candidate arms and independently generated children per arm. Repeated executions of each child separate execution variation from generation variation. Compare predicted differences, empirical reward differences, and the observed quality of arms selected by random, greedy, and exploration-aware rules. Finite probes can estimate local ranking behavior, but cannot identify the full-archive optimum or uniformly bound unobserved arms. 

Matched-budget end-to-end controls. Use the same initialization protocol, proposal mechanism, execution models, validation splits, and evaluation accounting for random, greedy, diagonal, and full-matrix selectors. Report repeated-run variation as well as mean outcomes. A full-matrix ridge control can be implemented through the low-rank identity in Appendix E.6; it need not allocate a dense p × p matrix. Any additional diagnostic evaluations must be separated from the search budget used for headline comparisons.
