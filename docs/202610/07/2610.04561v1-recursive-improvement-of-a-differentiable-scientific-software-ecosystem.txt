## ABSTRACT 

Differentiable programming connects scientific computation with gradient-based inference, learning and design. Extending these capabilities across a heterogeneous software ecosystem requires specialized effort to implement derivatives, integrate interfaces and evaluate quality. AI coding agents can accelerate this transformation, but translating their capabilities into useful scientific software requires identifying research needs and evaluating how well implementations meet them. We present an environment for agent-driven evolution of differentiable scientific software that connects demand identification, development and quality evaluation. A unified differentiation interface exposes reusable derivative rules alongside existing nu-merical routines, allowing research tasks to share these capabilities. Research requirements guide development, with implementations assessed through inde-pendent derivative checks, workflow tests and performance evaluation. Validated software, research programs and tests become shared resources for subsequent studies. We construct and validate automatic differentiation extensions across 20 packages spanning physical, chemical and biological modeling, with research workflows demonstrating reuse across tasks. Benchmarks demonstrate computa-tional savings over finite differences in gradient evaluation and complete parameter estimation. Research-driven revisions make previously unsupported workflows differentiable, correct derivatives of scientific observables and eliminate redundant computation. Quantum-control and thermal-design studies revise objectives in re-sponse to physical evaluation, improving designs while reusing existing derivatives. This work provides a practical approach to expanding differentiable programming across established scientific software and organizing AI agents around the recursive improvement of a shared computational ecosystem. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Scientific software is a cumulative record of research practice. It preserves mathematical models, numerical methods and domain conventions as executable code, allowing new studies to build on established computations. Differentiable programming extends this reuse from evaluating a model to computing how its outputs change with parameters, making scientific computations available to gradient-based learning, inference and design (Blondel & Roulet, 2024). The opportunity extends across research fields: reusable derivatives can connect established numerical implementations to new observations, objectives and parameterizations without rebuilding a scientific model for each application. Realizing this opportunity across a heterogeneous software ecosystem requires more than applying an automatic differentiation (AD) engine. Numerical codes expose different data structures, solvers and execution models, and their derivatives must preserve domain-specific meaning. Established AD techniques provide differentiation machinery (Baydin et al., 2018; Moses & Churavy, 2020; Farrell et al., 2013; Mitusch et al., 2019); integrating them into scientific software requires choosing suitable differentiation boundaries, connecting interfaces and checking numerical results across many useful tools. AI agents can investigate repositories and carry out scientific coding tasks (Yang et al., 2024; Wölflein et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Geng et al., 2026), creating an opportunity to extend scientific software across domains. Producing code, however, does not establish which numerical capabilities a research problem needs or whether an implementation meets those needs. A new observable may require dependencies that an existing interface omits; even correct derivatives can optimize a design that fails under physical conditions absent from its objective. Research programs make these requirements concrete and provide tests that guide further development. We ask how agent-driven development and research use can jointly build and improve a differentiable scientific software ecosystem. The evaluated collection spans molecular and cellular modeling, chemical kinetics, materials and quantum physics, and Earth and space sciences. Research programs compose the extensions’ values and derivative actions with their own inputs and objectives, allowing the same numerical capability to serve different studies. ## 2 A DIFFERENTIABLE SCIENTIFIC SOFTWARE ECOSYSTEM 

The environment connects reusable numerical capabilities to research programs through development, evaluation and revision (Figure 1). 2.1 REUSABLE COMPUTATIONS AND DIFFERENTIATION INTERFACES 

An AD extension exposes values and derivatives for specified continuous inputs within a documented domain. It uses original APIs and numerical routines, or kernels checked against original outputs. For y = f (θ; c), θ denotes active inputs and c fixed context such as topology or a numerical branch. Jacobian–vector products (JVPs) and vector–Jacobian products (VJPs) describe the numerical response; research code defines the task’s parameterization, observations and loss. Different objectives can therefore share a derivative implementation (Blondel et al., 2022). Our diffusion rules for FiPy (Guyer et al., 2009) differentiate the conductivity-to-temperature solve: inversion compares the field with observations, whereas cloak design measures its exterior disturbance. The shared abstraction has runtime-specific bindings, not a single execution engine. Our Python ChainRules package registers explicit rules for existing callables, following Julia ChainRules’ separa-tion of rules from AD engines (JuliaDiff contributors, n.d.). The evaluated FiPy programs use custom Autograd rules (Maclaurin et al., n.d.); QuantumOptics (Krämer et al., 2018) uses ChainRulesCore with Zygote (Innes, 2018). Appendix A.3 gives the bindings and a concrete task-to-rule call. Research programs, numerical checks and saved outputs complement these packages as resources for later studies. 2.2 DEVELOPMENT GUIDED BY RESEARCH USE 

Development agents inspect the original software and propose computations to extend, specifying the APIs, active inputs and differentiation strategy. Separate reviewer agents assess the design; developers revise it and then implement the rules, examples and numerical tests (Appendix A). Research agents turn scientific questions from papers, public research code and original-software examples into models, objectives and executable evaluation programs. Alongside these sources, the Large Knowledge Model (LKM) (Huang et al., 2026) represents scientific literature as source-grounded reasoning graphs linking research questions, workflows, conclusions and supporting evidence. Evaluation checks original-value agreement, derivatives against independent references, and scientific outcomes under specified conditions; computational costs are measured separately. Research feedback carries a computation, fixed inputs and numerical checks into a development request. In Kwant, the request supplied an eight-gate device and required gate-to-transmission derivatives checked against original values, finite differences and flux conservation. Development returned a package rule; research-side retests used the same device, then reused its optimized design to test a second implementation update (Section 4.1). Physical evaluation can instead prompt revised research objectives using existing derivatives. Software, programs, checks and saved results remain available to subsequent tasks.

## 4 RESEARCH -DRIVEN EVOLUTION OF SHARED CAPABILITIES 

Research use reveals requirements beyond the initial derivative implementation. We trace how task definitions and computational demands lead to new capabilities, numerical repairs and execution improvements, with research inputs and outputs reused to test revisions. 4.1 ITERATIVE SOFTWARE IMPROVEMENT THROUGH RESEARCH TASKS 

An electronic energy filter should suppress transmission in one energy window while retaining it in another (Zhouyin et al., 2023). We tune eight gates in a 24 × 6 Kwant device connected to two leads. The initial extension differentiated supplied scattering matrices but could not carry gate changes through the solve that produces transmission. The research task specified the missing gate-to-transmission path, together with value, derivative and flux-conservation checks. A new implicit scattering rule passed an independent task-level retest and made the filter objective differentiable. Using that rule, an AD-based design meets the predeclared thresholds at eight energies reserved from the six used for optimization: mean stop/pass transmissions are 0.266/1.737, against limits of at most 0.5 and at least 1.5. FD finds a similar design (Appendix C.1). Figure 3a–b shows the saved potential and native transmission. Repeated gradient evaluations revealed that the first implementation rebuilt and factorized the same scattering system for each gate direction. The next revision shared the linearization and added an adjoint. At both the original and saved-design inputs, values and full gradients remain matched; for 5Preprint one six-energy value-and-gradient request, derivative factorizations fall from 48 to six. At the saved design, median time falls from 1.34 to 0.48 s, compared with 0.88 s for central FD, in three processes per method (Figure 3c). These timings compare fixed inputs, not complete design runs. The saved device and numerical checks are reused to validate the revised gate-to-transmission rule: the design result also supports a subsequent software improvement (Appendix C.1). StochKit2 (Sanft et al., 2011) shows why internally consistent derivatives can still describe the wrong scientific quantity. A research program measures how reaction rates change a terminal-population histogram with specified bins. In a two-sample regression test, the requested probability vector is 

[1 , 0] and its reference derivative is [0 , 0] , yet the old rule returns [−0.5, 0.5] . Both derivative modes agree because both recompute automatic bin edges instead of using those requested by the research program. The repair carries the requested bins through both modes and agrees with an independent recon-struction from native trajectories. The bin-specific test then remains available during later changes, including a revision that reduces duplicate native calls from four to two with bitwise-identical outputs. In the native 512-trajectory screen, one ensemble still misses a low-count-bin analytic-gradient crite-rion: preserving the requested observable and achieving sufficient sampling precision are separate requirements (Appendix C.2). Workflow reports describe further capability changes. An OpenPNM patch adds reaction-source and directed-flow derivatives, bringing reaction–diffusion and advection–diffusion into its checked transport paths. BioDynaMo repairs mechanics updates to preserve the original simulation’s meaning. QuSpin brings propagation derivatives from research programs into a shared package rule, which later supplies both control objectives in Section 5.1. ## 5 EVOLVING RESEARCH APPLICATIONS 

Research applications develop by refining tasks and reusing their results. Here existing quantum-propagation and diffusion derivatives support design revisions guided by physical evaluation. Correct gradients alone do not ensure that a design works under the conditions of interest. Prepared quantum states also become inputs to a subsequent sensing study. 5.1 ROBUST QUANTUM CONTROL AND PHASE SENSING 

Entanglement can improve phase sensitivity (Giovannetti et al., 2011), provided suitable probes can be prepared with the available controls (Yang et al., 2020). We use QuSpin propagation and its derivatives to prepare a four-spin Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger (GHZ) state, an equal superposition of all-up and all-down spins. Local fields along two transverse axes act on each spin over 12 intervals, giving 96 bounded amplitudes. The same propagation derivative supplies the gradient for nominal and coupling-aware objectives (Figure 4a); only the research objective and its evaluation conditions change. Differentiable quantum control and ensemble objectives provide the relevant methodological context (Leung et al., 2017; Schäfer et al., 2020; Goerz et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2014). Fifteen initial nominal designs have fidelity above 0.99999 at the intended coupling J, yet replaying fixed pulses with a common coupling shift of up to ±10% exposes sensitivity. Here δ denotes the fractional change in J. The revised objective averages preparation error at three coupling values. At 

T = 4 /J with ℏ = 1 , three paired initializations compare the objectives with equal budgets of 660 single-scenario value-and-gradient evaluations. On ten additional offsets between the training values, 7Preprint the worst error 1 − F falls from 10.2–15.8% to 0.020–0.090% (Figure 4b). This gain comes with larger root-mean-square control amplitudes and a small loss of nominal fidelity; all six runs stop at their prescribed budgets. The revised objective therefore yields controls that better tolerate coupling errors in this four-spin task, using the existing propagation derivative. We next reuse saved states as probes to estimate an unknown collective spin rotation from four-bit measurement outcomes, without another pulse optimization. We select one state from each control objective at a fixed, known +10% coupling error and use the same ideal readout and estimator. Root-mean-square errors are 2.48–2.86 milliradians across two phases. Ideal unentangled and GHZ probes yield 4.84–5.08 and 2.40–2.53 milliradians, respectively (Figure 4c). Both saved states retain sensing utility close to the ideal GHZ reference under this readout. This subsequent-use test is separate from the paired control comparison (Appendix D.2). 5.2 THERMAL -CLOAK DESIGN UNDER MULTIPLE HEATING CONDITIONS 

A thermal cloak redirects heat so that an inclusion leaves little signature in the surrounding temperature field (Han et al., 2014). A design that works under one heating pattern can remain visible under another. We use FiPy’s implicit diffusion derivatives to choose conductivities for eight concentric layers around a low-conductivity core, under material bounds and a budget. The gradient connects the layer conductivities to exterior temperature disturbance after the steady diffusion solve (Figure 5a– b). It is the same class of diffusion capability used for conductivity inversion in Section 3.2, here composed with a design objective. An initial design trained on two boundary-heating patterns increases, rather than suppresses, the disturbance under a cubic pattern. The revised task adds that load to its objective and trains on a finer grid, retaining the diffusion rule. After this joint revision, on a common 192 2 evaluation grid, cubic root-mean-square (RMS) disturbance relative to the bare core falls from 2.96 to 0.00371; the revised ratio is 0.0446 at 576 2 (Appendix E). ## 6 RELATED WORK 

Differentiable scientific computing. Established AD systems provide operator overloading, source transformation and compiler-based differentiation (Griewank et al., 1996; Hascoët & Pascual, 2013; Moses & Churavy, 2020). Composable transformations and rules at numerical-solver boundaries support differentiable simulation and scientific modeling (Bradbury et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2020; Schoenholz & Cubuk, 2020; Farrell et al., 2013; Mitusch et al., 2019; Blondel et al., 2022; Rackauckas et al., 2020). ChainRules separates derivative definitions from AD engines; GradBench and earlier benchmarks compare implementations across languages and workloads (JuliaDiff contributors, n.d.; Estep et al., n.d.; Šrajer et al., 2018). We build on these techniques to extend established scientific packages. We examine how the resulting derivatives preserve scientific meaning across research uses, and how task-level requirements change shared implementations and their checks. 

Agents for software and scientific research. Agent systems and benchmarks study repository repair (Jimenez et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025), scientific coding and research extensions (Tian et al., 2024; Duston et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025; Edwards et al., 2026), and machine-learning engineering (Chan et al., 2025). ToolMaker builds agent-callable tools from papers with code (Wölflein et al., 2025); Lahlou et al. translate a legacy Fortran model into JAX with a numerical reference (Lahlou et al., 2026). ScienceIDE uses scientific repositories as environments for agent learning and evaluation (Geng et al., 2026). Our studies follow reusable numerical software across research tasks: requirements revealed in use change its implementation, while validated derivatives and saved outputs support subsequent studies. 

Evaluator-driven and recursive improvement. FunSearch and AlphaEvolve use evaluators to guide program search (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024; Novikov et al., 2025); the Darwin Gödel Machine modifies the agent itself (Zhang et al., 2026). Our object of improvement is scientific software together with the research programs that use it. Evaluation can change the numerical capability or the scientific objective, and a preceding research result can become an input to later software validation. The cases examine these connections within a shared ecosystem. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We have built a differentiable scientific software ecosystem through agent-driven construction and research-guided improvement. Across 20 scientific packages, numerical checks validate diverse derivative computations and measured gradient requests cost less than their finite-difference com-parisons. The research studies show how these capabilities support inference and design, and how feedback adds missing dependencies, corrects scientific semantics and reduces repeated computation in shared implementations. The ecosystem develops its numerical capabilities and their uses together. Scientific checks determine whether a computation matches the intended observable; physical evaluation tests whether that computation serves the research objective under the conditions of interest. These different questions motivate changes to shared implementations or to the objectives built on them. Executable research programs, checks and saved outputs then provide inputs for subsequent research and software validation. Recursive improvement connects individual task outcomes to the continuing development of these shared resources. The broader opportunity is to use AI agents to reshape how scientific software ecosystems develop and combine their capabilities. Differentiation provides a common operation through which many established computations can participate in learning, inference and design. Similar connections be-tween development and research could extend other capabilities, including uncertainty quantification and coupled simulation. Accumulating implementations and the scientific knowledge needed to validate them offers a route from individual software advances to resources that support a wider range of research. 9Preprint

A.1 DEVELOPMENT ROLES AND STAGE OUTPUTS 

The development procedure used for the AD extensions separates investigation and specification, independent design review, revision, and implementation (Table 2). The reviewer reconstructs the original interface inventory and checks a working runtime in a separate checkout; the developer revises the specification before implementation. Agents generate the software-specific configurations, derivative designs, code and tests. The development agents used Codex with the gpt5.6sol model. The research team selects software, establishes the common procedure, initiates runs and provides computing resources. Saved prompts, target configurations and preceding outputs connect the stages. The runner also offers a separately invoked delivery review. This optional stage and repeated design reviews are not documented for every package. The code and data guide identifies the runner and prompt versions. In one retained QuSpin construction, the specification stage produced a proposed design, a separate review requested changes, and the revision stage passed an explicit target list to implementation. For hamiltonian.evolve , a review finding required initial-state derivatives to be distinguished from derivatives of the Hamiltonian itself. The revised target fixed the Hamiltonian and time grid, differentiated the initial state, and required original-value agreement, multistep directional checks and JVP/VJP duality. The later Hamiltonian-active propagation used in Section 5.1 is a distinct extension. This example shows how a review changes a numerical design, rather than only approving an API name. Each stage passes its documented outputs to the next.

Research agents use scientific literature, public research code and original-software examples to construct executable tasks and evaluate their numerical results and costs. Alongside these sources, the Large Knowledge Model (LKM) provides source-grounded reasoning graphs linking research questions, workflows, conclusions and supporting evidence. The original code and specified inputs, rather than the reasoning graph alone, define the computation. The detailed cases illustrate complementary forms of rule reuse, composition, numerical repair and execution changes. They were selected retrospectively, so their counts do not estimate a development success rate. Within the control, material-design and transport studies, objectives, budgets and evaluation conditions are fixed before the corresponding execution. Regression tests check repaired behavior; conditions not used to guide a revision assess subsequent use. Feedback specifies the affected computation, the missing derivative or observed defect, and the checks required of a revision. The Python ChainRules implementation associates explicit derivative rules with callables. A rule accepts named input tangents or active parameters and returns a value with a tangent or a pullback; the package does not trace arbitrary Python code. It is separate from Julia’s ChainRulesCore. The QuSpin construction and control experiments use the recorded Python ChainRules 0.1.0 binding; the Kwant inverse-problem archive uses 0.1.1.dev0. The tested FiPy inverse and cloak programs instead use Autograd 1.8 (Maclaurin et al., n.d.), with custom forward and reverse rules registered through its primitive interface. QuantumOptics uses Julia ChainRulesCore (JuliaDiff contributors, n.d.) composed through Zygote (Innes, 2018). Each runtime implements the shared abstraction of a value and derivative action on declared inputs. Experiment-specific versions and bindings accompany each program. For a concrete call, the QuSpin control program loads the package rules and imports vjp from Python ChainRules. With Hamiltonian object H, input state psi_in , fixed duration dt and solver options, one propagation step is: 

psi_out, pull = vjp(hamiltonian.evolve, H, psi_in, 0.0, dt, wrt=("self", "v0"), **solver_options) cotangents = pull(output_cotangent) 

Here self denotes the Hamiltonian receiver and v0 the initial state. The returned mapping contains their cotangents. Task code contracts the Hamiltonian cotangent with its control oper-ators and propagates the state cotangent to the preceding interval. This is the invocation used in analysis/scripts/run_quspin_robust_control.py in the supplementary archive. Appendix D gives the control matrices, objective, solver settings and complex-gradient convention; the reproduction guide links the task to its required package and source versions. 

Separating a solve rule from a research loss. For a real nonsingular system A(θ)y = b(θ), an implicit JVP solves A δy = δb − (δA )y (Blondel et al., 2022). For a scalar loss L(y, θ ), reverse differentiation solves A⊤λ = ∂L/∂y . The solve rule contributes λ⊤[δb − (δA )y] to the directional derivative of the loss. Task code adds the direct parameter derivative of L. Changing the measured observable changes the loss and its derivative with respect to y, while retaining the solve rule. This separation lets task code express new research objectives using the same numerical derivative implementation. A.4 COMPOSING A STEADY -STATE DERIVATIVE

Problem and comparison. Conductivity tolerances can change the behavior of an optimized coating. This follow-up reoptimizes both designs; they are distinct from the fixed design evaluated in Figure 5. The material-error study tests a design objective that includes these errors, using the same geometry, steady diffusion equation, observation annulus and three-load normalization as above. Its training-grid improvement does not persist under finer evaluation, illustrating why the revised objective also needs a discretization check. For each material-error scenario s, Js is the mean of these normalized squared errors over three training loads: linear, quadratic and cubic harmonics. The nominal arm minimizes J0; the finite-scenario arm minimizes max s Js, where the five scenarios are zero error, common ±5% error, and alternating-layer ±5% error. This is a worst material scenario of a three-load mean, not the worst individual load or the whole error box. Coordinates and conductivities are normalized to the square side length and background conductivity; boundary temperatures use the profiles below. Both arms constrain 0.05 /0.95 ≤ ki ≤ 8/1.05 and impose area-weighted mean conductivity at most 2.5/1.05 , using each of the exact annular areas and the 192 2 and 384 2 pixel areas. These constraints keep all componentwise ±5% material perturbations feasible. The shared starts are a uniform conductivity of 1.73411269 and an alternating e−0.8/e 0.8

multiple of that value, rescaled to the area budget. SLSQP uses ftol = 10 −11 and a cumulative 180-iteration cap, with a limit of 3,000 forward plus 3,000 reverse solves per trajectory. The two nominal runs converge; both finite-scenario runs exhaust their solve budget. Uniform-start runs resume from saved parameters with cumulative budgets preserved. The original design-export routine incorrectly used an auxiliary upper-bound variable for the training loss to screen material-feasible cached points. A post-run correction selected each arm’s lowest-training-loss feasible point from 35 Preprint its unchanged cache and computed a consistent upper-bound value from that loss, preserving the original records. Only values and gradients were rechecked; no further optimization or selection on evaluation results was performed. The comparison therefore uses feasible designs within the recorded budgets, not converged optima for both objectives. The original caches and selection record preserve this correction. The extension is FiPy AD 0.2.1, commit f7314e0d , with the original FiPy forward solver.
