ERROR ESTIMATES IN PHYSICS-INFORMED NEURAL NETWORKS TO HEAT-WAVE FLUID-STRUCTURE INTERACTIVE SYSTEM

Abstract 

We study a physics-informed neural network (PINN) approximation of a coupled PDE system that models the interaction between fluid and elastic dynamics. The canonical heat-wave model considered here couples a parabolic and a hyperbolic equation through transmission conditions on a fixed boundary interface, and serves as a prototype for the Stokes-elastic systems that arises in real world applications. This coupling makes the error analysis more delicate than that for a single PDE. We bound the error between the exact and PINN solutions by the associated PINN residuals, and we moreover prove that the generalization error is controlled by the training error, the number of quadrature points, and the size of the network. To the best of our knowledge, these are the first error estimates for a PINN approximation of a coupled fluid-structure interaction PDE system. We also show that numerical experiments with respect to a given test problem. 

Keywords. Fluid-structure Interaction; PINNs; Error Estimates; Scientific Machine Learning.

1. Introduction 

The fluid structure interaction (FSI) PDE models which are composed of elastic dynamics coupled to heat, Stokes or Navier Stokes flows are known to describe a variety of physical phenomena, e.g., vibrations in elastic membranes, aerodynamics of structures such as bridges and tall buildings [1, 5, 6, 8, 9, 16, 21, 22]. In particular, the mathematical analysis of FSI problems in which the governing equations share the same dimensionality; i.e., 2D or 3D structure equation coupled to 2D or 3D fluid flow, and the boundary interface is particularly not smooth becomes quite challenging [5, 17, 36, 37]. Analytical results for various linear and nonlinear 2D (3D)-2D (3D) FSI models, such as those of well-posedness and longtime behavior of solutions, have been studied in various settings; see [1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 12]. In particular, the semigroup wellposedness of the Stokes-Lame interaction coupled PDE dynamics was studied in [1, 2]. Subsequently, in [4], the authors proved that the solution to the PDE system considered in [2] satisfies higher regularity if the initial data is taken to be sufficiently smooth. The stability analysis of the solution to that Stokes-Lame PDE system was also studied in [2] and [5] in the sense of strong and rational decay. The numerical approximation of coupled FSI systems has also been a research objective; see, e.g., [10, 21, 22, 26] and references therein. Although finite element methods have often been invoked for coupled PDE systems, they require meshes that are compatible across the fluid and structure subdomains, which is often difficult to ensure for complex interfaces. PINNs, which enforce the equations and the transmission conditions through a single mesh-free residual loss, have recently emerged as a widely used framework for approximating solutions of PDEs across a broad range of applications in science and engineering. While the foundational ideas for PINNs date back to the 1990s [19, 28, 29], there has been a notable resurgence of interest in recent years [11, 24, 34, 35]. In the PINN approach, a neural network is “trained” by minimizing residuals of the governing equations at collocation points in the interior, on the boundary, and at the initial time, incorporating the underlying physical laws directly into the training process. For a comprehensive overview of recent developments, we refer the reader to [11, 24] and references therein.

Deriving rigorous error estimates for PINNs has drawn considerable attention from the mathematical community, leading to substantial progress for a number of standard PDE settings [13, 14, 31, 32]. In [32], Mishra and Molinaro established an abstract framework in which the stability properties of the underlying PDE are leveraged to bound the generalization error of a trained PINN, in terms of the training error and the number of collocation points. Since then, this approach has been extended to several nonlinear PDEs including the Navier–Stokes and Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equations [13, 14, 33]. However, despite this progress, the application of PINNs to coupled multi-physics systems together with a rigorous mathematical justification remains limited; see [20], where PINNs were applied to a two-dimensional Navier-Stokes fluid flow interacting with an elastic disc and this numerical method was compared against a classical solver, with no error analysis. Error estimates for related settings exist for thermally coupled Navier–Stokes flows [7], single parabolic interface problem [27], and domain decomposition settings [23]; but there is nothing in the literature for the treatment of a parabolic-hyperbolic system coupled through interface conditions. What these references lack are conditions which guarantee that a small training loss implies a small error in the coupled solution. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no error estimates exist for PINNs which approximate coupled fluid-structure interaction PDE systems –even in the canonical case. Motivated by this gap, the present manuscript develops the first rigorous error analysis for PINNs applied to a coupled FSI system. We address the following key theoretical questions: 

• [Q1] Let ( w, w t, u ) denote the exact solution of the coupled system and let ( wθ, w θt , u θ) be the corresponding PINN approximation, where θ ∈ Θ denotes the network parameters (weights and biases). If the generalization error is small, can one assure that the total error remains small, i.e., 

∥(w, w t, u ) − (wθ, w θt , u θ)∥ < δ (ε)where || .|| is some relevant energy norm? 

• [Q2] Given a small training error ET (θ∗) and a sufficiently large training set S, is the corresponding generalization error EG(θ∗) also proportionately small? For the present configuration, the solution is approximated by a neural network whose parameters are obtained by minimizing residual losses of the PDEs in the fluid and solid regions together with the interface and initial conditions evaluated at sampled collocation points, thereby yielding a mesh-free formulation. We answer the above questions [Q1] and [Q2] affirmatively through a rigorous error analysis. In particular, we present here a comprehensive treatment of PINNs applied to a coupled FSI model which describes the interactive dynamics of canonical heat and wave equations. Unlike single equation PDE problems, the heat-wave FSI system involves the interplay between a parabolic (heat) component and a hyperbolic (wave) component coupled at a (fixed) interface, which introduces distinctive challenges in both the energy analysis and treatment of interface conditions. In addition, we provide rigorous error bounds for the proposed PINN approximation algorithm, and numerical experiments in order to validate the accuracy of our result through comparisons with solutions of a given test problem and the theoretical convergence bounds. The PINN analysis for the more complicated FSI model governed by Navier-Stokes fluid and elastic wave/plate equations will be treated in a forthcoming publication.

Network architecture and training. A single fully-connected neural network Φθ : R3 → R4 approx-imates the coupled fields, mapping ( x, y, t ) to (( uθ 1, u θ 2), (wθ 1, w θ 2)), while ( w1 θ t , w 2 θ t ) is obtained by automatic differentiation with respect to t. The architecture uses two hidden layers of width W with tanh activation, giving L = 3 in the notation of Definition 3.1, and output dimension lL = 2 d = 4.

Training is performed in two stages. In the first stage, the Adam optimizer is run for 20 ,000 epochs with an initial learning rate of 5 × 10 −4. The learning rate is reduced by half when the training loss plateaus, to a minimum of 6 .25 × 10 −5; and gradient clipping with threshold 1 .0 is employed throughout. In the second stage, the L-BFGS optimizer is run for six cycles of 1 ,000 iterations each to further reduce the PDE, boundary, and interface residuals. 

Experiment 1: Effect of quadrature points. We fix the network width at W = 80 and vary the number of residual quadrature points M ∈ { 21 ,200 , 35 ,200 , 50 ,450 }. According to the estimate (81), the bound on the total error contains the quadrature-error terms CIC M −1/d IC , CPDE M −1/(d+1) PDE , CBC M −1/2dBC ,and CIF M −1/d IF , which decrease as the corresponding numbers of quadrature points increase. The left panel of Figure 2 reports the total error ∥(w, w t, u ) − (wθ, w θt , u θ)∥L2 , the training error 

ET (θ, S ), and the theoretical bound (81) for each value of M . The results confirm that increasing the number of quadrature points reduces both the training loss and the L2-error, with the most significant reduction occurring at the initial increase from 21 ,200 to 35 ,200 points. The theoretical bound decreases consistently with M , in agreement with (81). Figure 3 displays the manufactured solution, PINN approximation, and pointwise absolute error for (w, w t, u ) based on training with M = 21 ,200 residual quadrature points. 

Experiment 2: Effect of network width. We fix M = 35 ,200 quadrature points and two hidden layers, and vary the width W ∈ { 20 , 80 , 160 }. In (81), the width enters only through the constants 

CPDE , CBC , CIF , and CIC , which by Lemma 3.4 grow with W ; these are worst-case bounds over the whole parameter set Θ L,W,R and need not be sharp for a trained network. At fixed M , the quadrature terms in (81) do not depend on W , so a wider network can improve the estimate only through the training error. The right panel of Figure 2 reports the total error, the training loss, and the bound for each width; the plotted bound is the right-hand side of (81). The training loss decreases from W = 20 to W = 80 and changes little between W = 80 and W = 160, and the L2-error varies only slightly across the three widths. The left panel of Figure 2, the measured error stays below the plotted bound in every run, decreases as 

M grows, and is insensitive to W at fixed M , consistent with the dependence on M and on the training error in (81).
