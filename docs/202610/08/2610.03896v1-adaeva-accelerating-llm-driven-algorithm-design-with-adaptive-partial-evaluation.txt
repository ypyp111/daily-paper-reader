## ABSTRACT 

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used for automated algorithm design. However the computational cost of evaluating the generated algorithms can be excessive. We consider the com-mon LLM-driven automated algorithm design (LLM4AD) setting in which a candidate algorithm is evaluated by aggregating its performance over a shared set of training instances. This instance-wise structure raises a natural question: must every candidate be evaluated on the entire instance set before deciding whether it remains competitive? Taking inspiration from algorithm configu-ration, we introduce AdaEva, a drop-in ada ptive partial-eva luation framework that progressively evaluates candidates on larger subsets of the same instance pool and eliminates unpromising candi-dates as evidence accumulates. Importantly, AdaEva leaves the underlying LLM4AD procedure and per-instance evaluator unchanged and requires no prior knowledge about instance difficulty. We in-stantiate this idea using successive halving (AdaEva-S) and statistical racing (AdaEva-R), and eval-uate both mechanisms across three representative LLM4AD frameworks, multiple LLM backbones, and optimization domains spanning combinatorial and continuous black-box optimization. Under matched evaluation budgets, AdaEva more reliably balances evaluation effort across candidates than fixed partial-evaluation strategies, yielding strong search efficiency and anytime performance to-gether with improved held-out generalization across the evaluated settings.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have generated substantial interest in LLM-driven automated algorithm design (LLM4AD) (Liu et al., 2026b), where LLMs are used to generate and iteratively refine complex heuristic algorithms for challenging optimization tasks (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Stein & B¨ ack, 2025; Ye et al., 2024). While most work focuses on improving generated algorithms or reducing LLM inference cost, another major source of computational overhead has received comparatively little attention: candidate evaluation .In many LLM4AD applications, evaluating a generated algorithm requires executing it repeatedly over a collection of problem instances. In combinatorial optimization, for example, each candidate heuristic may need to solve tens or hundreds of training instances before its quality can be estimated reliably. Similar costs arise in continuous black-box optimization, where candidate algorithms are typically evaluated across multiple functions, dimensions, or random seeds. As the number of generated candidates increases, exhaustive evaluation can therefore consume a substantial fraction of the overall computational budget, limiting the number of algorithms that can be explored. This motivates the central question of our work: Can we reduce candidate-evaluation cost in evolutionary LLM4AD without sacrificing the quality of the algorithms discovered? 

We study the common LLM4AD setting in which candidate quality is measured by aggregating performance over a set of training instances. This structure, arising naturally in heuristic and algorithm design for optimization problems, creates an opportunity for adaptive evaluation . Rather than evaluating every candidate immediately on the full instance set, we can first evaluate candidate algorithms on a small subset and allocate additional evaluations only to those that seem promising. Clearly inferior candidates can thus be discarded early, before consuming the full evaluation budget. Building on this observation, we introduce AdaEva , an Ada ptive Eva luation framework for LLM-driven algorithm design. AdaEva progressively allocates additional evaluations to candidates that remain competitive while eliminating unpromising candidates early, so that adaptive resource allocation is done without modifying the underlying LLM4AD framework’s search mechanism. This general design naturally accommodates resource-allocation techniques developed in the adjacent areas of auto-mated algorithm configuration (Hoos, 2012) and hyper-parameter optimization (Yang & Shami, 2020). We instantiate AdaEva using two complementary approaches. The first, AdaEva-S , is based on succesive halving (Karnin et al., 2013), which progressively allocates larger evaluation budgets to smaller sets of promising candidates. The second, 

AdaEva-R , builds on statistical racing (Birattari et al., 2002), which eliminates candidates once sufficient evidence indicates that they are unlikely to outperform their competitors (L´ opez-Ib´ a˜ nez et al., 2016). Both approaches are par-ticularly well suited to LLM4AD because they do not require predictive models of candidate performance and because they operate directly on instance-level evaluation outcomes. We empirically demonstrate that AdaEva can substantially improve evaluation efficiency while preserving the effec-tiveness of the underlying LLM4AD search. Across combinatorial optimization and continuous black-box optimiza-tion, we assess search progress throughout the evaluation budget, the quality of the final returned algorithms, and their performance on held-out instances. We further examine how adaptive allocation reshapes evaluation consumption and candidate survival, characterize the resulting evaluation dynamics, and assess the robustness of AdaEva across different LLM backbones, allocation strategies, statistical tests, and racing configurations. Together, these experi-ments evaluate not only whether adaptive evaluation improves search efficiency, but also how its allocation decisions influence the behavior and outcomes of evolutionary LLM4AD. Our main contributions can be summarized as follows: • We formulate instance-wise candidate evaluation in evolutionary LLM4AD as an adaptive resource-allocation problem and introduce AdaEva , a drop-in evaluation framework that adaptively allocates evaluation effort while preserving the underlying candidate-generation and evolutionary search mechanisms. • We instantiate AdaEva with two complementary allocation mechanisms: AdaEva-S , based on successive halving, and AdaEva-R , based on statistical racing. • We show that adaptive evaluation provides more consistent search efficiency and held-out performance under matched evaluation budgets than any single fixed number of evaluations per candidate. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

LLM-Driven Algorithm Design. LLM4AD frameworks use LLMs to generate and revise executable algorithms, with evaluation results guiding subsequent search (Liu et al., 2026b). EoH (Liu et al., 2024) evolves a population of heuristics through LLM-generated variation and recombination, while ReEvo (Ye et al., 2024) uses reflective feedback and memory to guide later generations. LLaMEA (Stein & B¨ ack, 2025) applies LLM-guided evolutionary search to the 2Preprint design of black-box optimization algorithms. Subsequent work has explored other ways to guide and organize search: HSEvo (Dat et al., 2025) uses harmony search to balance exploration and exploitation, and MCTS-AHD (Zheng et al., 2025) structures exploration as a search tree. More recently, HiFo-Prompt (Zhong et al., 2026) combines foresight prompts that respond to population dynamics with hindsight prompts that distill reusable insights from earlier generations. Work on generalization has also expanded to more diverse training instances. EoH-S searches for a portfolio of complementary heuristics (Liu et al., 2026a) on a large set of instances with different distributions and sizes, and MoH (Shi et al., 2026) develops heuristic optimizers through self-invocation and multi-instance training. These advances change how candidates are generated, selected, or retained, but they do not address the question of how to efficiently compare generated candidate algorithms. 

Evaluation Efficiency in LLM4AD. Candidate evaluation is often a major computational bottleneck in LLM-driven search, particularly when each candidate must be executed across many problem instances, stochastic runs, or ex-pensive simulations. This imbalance can be substantial when generation is cheap relative to evaluation: Order-Place (Mo et al., 2026), for example, reports that most optimization time is spent evaluating generated strategies, while GEPA (Agrawal et al., 2026) similarly highlights subsampled validation as a promising direction for improved efficiency. Existing methods reduce this cost through pre-specified evaluation cascades (AlphaEvolve (Novikov et al., 2025)), smaller evaluation subsets (HeuriGym (Chen et al., 2026)), learned fitness predictors or proxy eval-uators (Hercules-P (Wu et al., 2025); Janus (Liu et al., 2026c)), or more sample-efficient search policies (ShinkaE-volve (Lange et al., 2026); AdaEvolve (Cemri et al., 2026)). In contrast, AdaEva leaves both candidate generation and the per-instance evaluator unchanged, and adaptively determines how much of a shared instance pool each candidate should be evaluated on before allocating further evaluation effort or making a selection decision. 

Adaptive Partial Evaluation in Adjacent Domains. Adaptive allocation of evaluation effort has a long history in automated algorithm configuration and hyper-parameter optimization. Racing methods such as F-Race (Birattari et al., 2002) and iterated racing (L´ opez-Ib´ a˜ nez et al., 2016) incrementally evaluate candidates on shared instances and eliminate those with sufficient evidence of inferior performance, while successive halving and Hyperband (Jamieson & Talwalkar, 2016; Li et al., 2018) progressively allocate larger budgets to smaller sets of promising configurations. Extensions such as BOHB (Falkner et al., 2018) and DEHB (Awad et al., 2021) combine such resource allocation with model-based and evolutionary search, and more recent work explicitly treats evaluation as a sequential budget-allocation problem under ranking uncertainty (Zouhar et al., 2026). AdaEva builds on these principles but uses them as instance-wise adaptive partial evaluation mechanisms within existing evolutionary LLM4AD frameworks, leaving their candidate-generation and population-update logic unchanged and thereby decoupling evaluation allocation from the underlying search strategy. 

## 3 INSTANCE -WISE ADAPTIVE PARTIAL EVALUATION FOR LLM4AD 

3.1 EVOLUTIONARY LOOP IN LLM4AD For a given problem P and a (typically not further specified) space H of executable algorithms, LLM-driven algorithm design casts the search as an evolutionary loop in which an LLM acts as the variation operators (Figure 1). At generation t, a population Π(t) ⊆ H of size M supplies parents for a generator L. These parents, together with the problem specification, are used to construct prompts from which the generator produces an offspring set O(t) ⊆ H .The current population and offspring are then combined into a candidate pool U(t) = Π (t) ∪O (t), and the M candidates with the best empirical performance are retained as the elites and copied to the next population. For this comparison, the performance of an algorithm h ∈ H is evaluated as the average quality ˆg(h) = P

f (h; x)/|S| that h achieves on a subset S ⊆ I of relevant problem instances I, with f (h; x) being the (average, in case of randomized algorithms) solution quality obtained by this heuristic on instance x ∈ S . In standard LLM4AD frameworks, the evaluation is conducted on the whole instance set ( S ≡ I ). Note that at each generation, only the offprings need to be evaluated, as their parents’ performance were obtained from the previous generation. 3.2 ADAPTIVE EVALUATION -B UDGET ALLOCATION 

Evaluating every candidate on the full pool spends the budget uniformly regardless of how candidates perform. Clearly inferior candidates consume as much as competitive ones, while closely matched candidates may need more evidence than either receives. Our proposed AdaEva framework acts as an evaluation layer that instead allocates budget progres-sively and leaves candidate generation, parent selection, and population update of the underlying LLM4AD framework untouched. We adapt two commonly-used techniques from the adjacent areas of hyper-parameter optimization and automated algorithm configuration. Algorithm 1 summarizes the common procedure that is used by both AdaEva variants at each generation t of their evolutionary loop. As described in Section 3.1, starting from the combined candidate set U(t) of parents and offsprings, the goal is to select M candidates to form the next population U(t+1) . AdaEva performs this selection iteratively. At each round, the current survivor set V(t) is evaluated on additional problem instances, while reusing all previously collected results stored in the evaluation history E (line 3). Based on the accumulated evidence, a subset of inferior candidates is then eliminated (line 5), and the procedure repeats on the remaining candidates. Elimination is capped to retain at least M candidates (lines 6–7). When M remain, they form the next population and the best survivor is returned as the incumbent. AdaEva-S and AdaEva-R share this overall structure and differ only in how additional evaluations are allocated and how candidates are eliminated. Partial evaluation also affects how performance feedback is presented to the LLM. In our main experiments, we retain each framework’s native prompting, without indicating that feedback may be based on a subset of instances, thereby isolating the evaluation layer. A partial-evaluation-aware variant shows no consistent advantage (Sections D and E). 

AdaEva-S as a scheduled elimination strategy. We define the evaluation depth of a candidate as the number of problem instances on which it has been evaluated. AdaEva-S follows the resource-allocation principle of successive halving (Karnin et al., 2013): candidates are evaluated at progressively larger depths, while a fixed fraction of inferior candidates is eliminated at each round. AdaEva-S has two hyper-parameters: the initial evaluation depth n0 and the reduction factor η > 1. At each evaluation round, candidates are ranked according to their accumulated evaluation results and approximately a 1/η fraction is retained, subject to keeping at least M candidates. AdaEva-R as an evidence-driven elimination strategy. AdaEva-R follows the principle of iterated racing (L´ opez-Ib´ a˜ nez et al., 2016), where elimination is driven by accumulated statistical evidence rather than a pre-specified sched-ule. At each round, every surviving candidate is evaluated on an additional ∆ instances. To account for stochasticity in the generated algorithms, the race may continue after all instances in I have been evaluated by collecting additional independent evaluations on previously seen instances. Thus, unlike AdaEva-S, reaching evaluation depth |I| does not necessarily terminate the race. Elimination begins only after candidates reach a minimum evaluation depth n0, after which a statistical decision rule is applied at every round. If the observed performance differences are not statisti-cally distinguishable from instance-to-instance and stochastic variation, all candidates remain in the race; otherwise, candidates with sufficient evidence of inferior performance are removed. Consequently, both the evaluation depth at which elimination occurs and the number of candidates eliminated in each round are adaptive and may vary across generations. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

LLM4AD Frameworks. We evaluate AdaEva as an off-the-shelf evaluation layer in three representative LLM4AD frameworks: Evolution of Heuristics (EoH) (Liu et al., 2024), HiFo-Prompt (Zhong et al., 2026), and LLaMEA (Stein & B¨ ack, 2025). They differ in how candidates are generated and selected, with EoH applying multiple variation strate-gies and selecting once per generation, HiFo-Prompt applying its operators sequentially with selection after each, and LLaMEA evolving parent to offspring with a parent’s measured performance written into the prompt. They also differ in what is evolved, such as a problem-specific decision rule embedded in a fixed solver for EoH and HiFo-Prompt, and a complete executable optimization algorithm for LLaMEA. Adaptive evaluation is therefore tested across both component-level heuristic design and full-algorithm synthesis. We retain each framework’s native generation, varia-tion, population-management, and prompting mechanisms, changing only the evaluation stage. Framework descrip-tions and their instantiations are in Section A. 

Benchmark Settings. We examine AdaEva across two broad optimization domains, combinatorial optimization (CO) and continuous black-box optimization (BBO). The CO suite comprises Online Bin Packing (OBP) (Seiden, 2002), where items arriving sequentially must be assigned to bins; the Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP) (Matai et al., 2010), where the goal is a short tour visiting all cities; and Flow Shop Scheduling (FSSP) (Emmons & Vairaktarakis, 2012), where jobs must be scheduled across multiple machines. The component evolved in each is the bin-scoring function for OBP and the landscape-update heuristic within Guided Local Search (GLS) (Voudouris, 1997) for TSP and FSSP, whereas LLaMEA evolves complete metaheuristic procedures tested on BBOB suite (Hansen et al., 2009). 

Generalization remains a central challenge in machine learning and is equally important in LLM-driven algorithm design. We therefore evaluate incumbents recorded at five checkpoints corresponding to 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100% of the total search budget on held-out instances that are disjoint from the search pools and introduce controlled changes in problem scale, instance source, or dimensionality. For OBP, the held-out instances increase problem scale and vary capacity, using 1k, 5k, and 10 k items with C ∈ { 100 , 500 }, which are originally tested in (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). For TSP, the search uses synthetic 100-city instances with coordinates sampled from 

[0 , 1] 2, whereas held-out evaluation uses TSPLIB (Reinelt, 1991) instances, introducing a shift in instance source and distribution. For FSSP, the search uses synthetic instances with uniformly sampled processing times, while held-out evaluation uses the external Taillard benchmark (Taillard, 1993), again changing the instance distribution. For BBOB, the search uses three instances of each function in d = 5 , while held-out evaluation uses disjoint instances in d ∈ { 5, 10 , 20 }; the higher-dimensional settings additionally require the generated algorithm to transfer to larger search spaces and different optimization landscapes. Brief descriptions of the benchmarks are provided in Table 1, with full details given in Section B.

We additionally use the instruction-tuned Qwen2.5-Coder (Hui et al., 2024), another openly available coding model with strong code-generation, reasoning, and code-fixing capabilities, to test whether the observed effects persist across independently developed LLM generators. This focus on open coding models, however, leaves open whether the findings extend to proprietary frontier models. Finding 1. On unseen instances, AdaEva ranks best or near-best across every framework, benchmark, and LLM backbone tested, whereas no fixed evaluation depth is competitive across them. 4.2 SEARCH PROGRESS UNDER FIXED BUDGET AND ADAPTIVE ALLOCATION 

The held-out results establish which strategies return better algorithms, but not why. We now examine the search itself, reporting the full-pool objective value of the recorded incumbent against budget consumed. A fixed evaluation budget can be spent on many candidates evaluated on few instances each, or on few candidates evaluated on many. We refer to the number of candidates a strategy examines as its breadth and the number of instances each receives as its depth ;their product is fixed by the budget, so a strategy cannot increase one without conceding the other. Figure 3 shows the validation curves on three settings chosen to span the behaviours observed, with the remaining four in Section C. The validation curves show that full-pool evaluation makes the least progress per unit budget in all settings. This is especially clear for EoH/TSP, where it uses nearly the entire budget to reach an objective value attained by other strategies within the first third (Figure 3-middle). Its estimates are the most precise, but this precision shortens the search horizon: each generation costs 640 evaluations, so few fit within B = 6 ,400 . BBOB (Figure 3-right) is an exception, where full-pool evaluation is mid-ranked because heterogeneous instances make shallow evaluation more prone to misranking candidates. Reducing the depth helps, but which reduction helps depends on the setting. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced AdaEva , an adaptive evaluation layer for LLM-driven algorithm design that allocates evaluation effort based on evidence accumulated during search. We study two complementary instantiations: AdaEva-S , based on successive halving (Karnin et al., 2013; Li et al., 2017), and AdaEva-R , based on statistical racing (Birattari et al., 2002; L´ opez-Ib´ a˜ nez et al., 2016). Both integrate into existing LLM4AD frameworks without modifying candidate generation, parent selection, or population update. Across three LLM4AD frameworks, multiple LLM backbones, and combinatorial and continuous black-box optimization problems, no single fixed amount of evaluation per candi-date performs consistently well across settings. AdaEva instead allocates more evaluation to candidates that remain competitive, balancing search breadth against evaluation effort and yielding more consistent search and held-out per-formance than any single fixed evaluation strategy. Our experiments adopt a fixed-budget perspective, asking how well a given evaluation budget is used. An equally natural alternative is fixed-target optimization, which minimizes the evaluation effort required to reach a specified performance level, as common in black-box optimization benchmarking Hansen et al. (2022). Our anytime results suggest AdaEva may also be effective in this setting, motivating future evaluation of AdaEva under fixed-target criteria. Several questions remain open. First, the relative performance of AdaEva-S and AdaEva-R varies across settings, and our experiments do not identify a reliable criterion for selecting between them in advance. Developing mechanisms that adapt not only the amount of evaluation but also the allocation strategy itself is a promising direction for future work. Second, although the default configurations are relatively stable on the combinatorial benchmarks, some design choices have a larger effect on BBOB, suggesting that broader studies of parameter adaptation may further improve performance. Finally, our experiments isolate candidate evaluation by preserving the native generation, prompting, and 9Preprint population-management mechanisms of each framework and by matching methods according to candidate-evaluation budget. Consequently, we do not separate framework and benchmark effects in a full factorial design, nor do we account for the additional LLM inference cost incurred when cheaper evaluation enables more generations. Extending adaptive resource allocation jointly across evaluation and other components of LLM4AD remains as future work. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

## A LLM4AD F RAMEWORKS 

This appendix describes how the LLM4AD frameworks used in our experiments instantiate their evolutionary loop. At a high level, each generation consists of parent selection and prompt construction, offspring generation, evaluation, and population update. Parents are drawn from the current population and combined with the problem specification to construct prompts; the generator LLM L returns offsprings O(t), which are screened for compilation and interface con-formance. Newly generated candidates are evaluated while evaluation results for retained candidates are carried over, and population management determines the candidates retained for subsequent generation. The frameworks differ in how prompts are constructed and how generation and population management are organized; these framework-specific details are described below. AdaEva replaces the evaluation stage while leaving the remaining framework components unchanged. 

Evolution of Heuristics (EoH). EoH (Liu et al., 2024) represents each candidate by a natural-language description of its high-level idea, referred to as a thought , together with its executable code implementation. The initial popu-lation is generated from the problem specification, with one LLM call producing one heuristic. At each subsequent generation, parents are selected from the current population using rank-based sampling, with better-ranked candidates receiving higher selection probability. Five prompt strategies then generate new heuristics: E1 promotes exploration by asking the LLM to produce an algorithm that is substantially different from the selected parents, while E2 asks it to identify the common idea in multiple parents and develop a new heuristic based on that idea. M1 modifies a single parent structurally, M2 changes its parameter settings, and M3 simplifies potentially redundant components. Each strategy is invoked repeatedly, with each LLM call producing one offspring; thus, with population size M , the five strategies generate up to 5M offspring per generation. These offspring are evaluated and added to the current pool when feasible, after which the best M heuristics are retained for the next generation. 

Hindsight-Foresight Prompt (HiFo-Prompt). HiFo-Prompt (Zhong et al., 2026) retains EoH’s thought-and-code representation and its five foundational evolutionary strategies, while augmenting each prompt with two additional sources of guidance. The hindsight component maintains an Insight Pool of design principles distilled from high-performing heuristics; retrieved insights are injected into subsequent prompts, and their utility is updated according to the performance of the offspring they helped generate. The foresight component, implemented by the Evolutionary Navigator, monitors recent population progress, stagnation, and description-level diversity, and selects one of three regimes of explore, exploit, or balance, whose corresponding design directive is added to the prompt. Unlike EoH’s generation-level application of its five operators, HiFo-Prompt applies its evolutionary operators sequentially. For each operator, the LLM is invoked repeatedly to generate an offspring batch of size M , where each offspring is produced by an independent LLM call. ## B BENCHMARK SETTINGS 

Combinatorial Optimization. EoH and HiFo-Prompt are evaluated on Online Bin Packing (OBP), the Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP), and Flow Shop Scheduling (FSSP), following the benchmark formulations used by EoH. For OBP, the original EoH search uses five Weibull instances with 5,000 items and capacity C = 100 , with fitness computed from the ratio between the lower bound and the number of bins used. We enlarge this search pool to 25 independently generated instances while retaining the same distribution and problem size. For TSP, the original search pool contains 64 randomly generated 100-city instances with coordinates sampled from [0 , 1] 2, and fitness is the average gap to the Concorde optimum. For FSSP, the original search pool contains 64 randomly generated instances with 50 jobs and 2–20 machines, with processing times sampled uniformly from [0 , 1] ; fitness is the average makespan. Candidate execution for TSP and FSSP uses the original GLS-based implementations, with at most 1,000 local-search iterations and a 60-second limit per candidate–instance evaluation. For generalization, we use held-out evaluation sets that are disjoint from the search instances. For OBP, the original EoH study evaluates five independently generated instances for each combination of problem size 1k, 5k, and 10 k and capacity C ∈ { 100 , 500 }, reporting percentage gap to the lower bound. We use these size and capacity settings for held-out evaluation. For TSP, EoH evaluates additional random instances at 20, 50, and 100 cities and 29 TSPLIB instances; the latter are evaluated by gap to the TSPLIB best-known solutions, and we use TSPLIB for our held-out evaluation. For FSSP, EoH evaluates Taillard instance sets with 10 instances per job–machine configuration and reports relative gap to the Taillard reference values; we use Taillard instances for held-out evaluation. 

Continuous Black-Box Optimization. LLaMEA is evaluated on the BBOB suite (Hansen et al., 2009). The search pool contains three instances (IDs in {1,2,3 }) of each of the 24 noiseless BBOB functions in five dimensions, giving 15 Preprint 72 function instances in total. BBOB comprises five function groups: separable, low- or moderately-conditioned, highly-conditioned unimodal, multimodal with adequate global structure, and multimodal with weak global structure. Candidate performance is aggregated using the normalized Area Over the Convergence Curve (AOCC). For generalization, the original LLaMEA study evaluates the generated algorithms on five BBOB instances per func-tion in dimensions d ∈ { 5, 10 , 20 }. We follow the same dimensions and number of held-out instances per function, but use explicitly disjoint instance identifiers (IDs in {4,5,6,7,8 }) for our evaluation. Each held-out instance is evaluated 3 times in our study using independent evaluation seeds.

C.1 BENCHMARK HETEROGENEITY 

Metrics. To characterize how consistently benchmark instances distinguish between candidate algorithms, we com-pute Kendall’s coefficient of concordance W (Kendall & Smith, 1939) on a fixed set of candidates evaluated on every instance. Each instance ranks the candidates by objective value, and W measures agreement among these rankings, with W = 1 indicating identical rankings across instances and W = 0 indicating no concordance. Candidates that fail on any instance are excluded so that all instances rank the same candidate set. We additionally report two coefficients of variation (CVs) (McKay, 1932). Across CV is the coefficient of variation of candidates’ mean performance over the instance pool, measuring how differentiated the candidates are on average. Within CV is the mean candidate-level coefficient of variation across instances, measuring how much an individual candidate’s performance varies across the pool. These statistics are computed from the same complete candidate– instance matrix and are independent of the evaluation strategy. 

Discussion. The four benchmarks exhibit substantially different combinations of ranking agreement and perfor-mance variability, as summarized in Table 3. OBP has perfect ranking concordance ( W = 1 .00 ), indicating that the relative ordering of candidates is invariant across the evaluated instances. Its Across CV is nevertheless non-negligible (0.34 ), reflecting meaningful differences in candidates’ mean performance. This combination is consistent with the strong performance of Fixed-1, although candidates differ substantially in quality, their relative ordering can be recov-ered from a single instance. TSP provides a contrasting case. Its ranking concordance is extremely low ( W = 0 .03 ), but its Across CV is nearly zero ( 0.0001 ) and its Within CV is also small ( 0.0348 ). Thus, instances can disagree strongly in their fine-grained candidate rankings even though candidate mean performance is tightly concentrated and individual candidates exhibit relatively little variation across instances. This helps explain why the low W does not translate into a clear need for deeper evaluation, and why Fixed-1 remains competitive on TSP. FSSP lies between these cases, with moderate ranking concordance ( W = 0 .61 ) and a larger Within CV ( 0.12 ). Its instances therefore exhibit more variation in candidate performance than TSP, making additional evaluation more informative. This is reflected in the greater benefit of intermediate or deeper fixed evaluation depths. BBOB combines moderate ranking concordance ( W = 0 .66 ) with substantially larger Across and Within CVs ( 0.68 

and 1.12 ). Candidate performance therefore varies considerably both across candidates and across instances. In this setting, a single-instance estimate provides substantially less information about overall candidate quality, consistent with the poor performance of Fixed-1 and the advantage of deeper or adaptive evaluation. Overall, these results show that no single benchmark statistic determines the appropriate evaluation depth. Kendall’s 

W captures the consistency of candidate rankings across instances, whereas the CVs capture the magnitude of per-formance variability. Protocol. We vary one parameter at a time while holding all others at their default values, so that each ablated configuration differs from the default of its own instantiation in exactly one entry and interactions between parameters are not covered. The sweeps use the Devstral backbone on the three EoH benchmarks and on LLaMEA/BBOB, with five seeds per configuration drawn from the same seed set as the main experiments. For each setting, configuration, and seed, we use the incumbent recorded at each of five budget checkpoints ( 20% , 40% , 60% , 80% , and 100% of the total evaluation budget) and scored on the full search pool. These values are taken directly from the same validation-trajectory data used to produce the corresponding validation curves. Let vk,i (c) denote the resulting value of configuration c at checkpoint k and seed i, and let c0 denote the default configuration of the same AdaEva instantiation. Because configurations and their defaults share the same seed set and therefore the same initial population for each paired seed, differences are computed within seed. We first verify that the seed identifiers match between c and c0; unmatched seeds are excluded from the comparison, and configurations with fewer than four matched seeds are flagged rather than silently averaged. Discussion. The sensitivity results show that the default configurations are generally stable on the three EoH bench-marks, with most changes producing deviations of less than 1% in incumbent quality. The largest effects on CO occur for AdaEva-R on OBP, where replacing Friedman with a paired t-test increases the deviation to +2 .39% , and for AdaEva-S when increasing n0 to 10 , which gives a +1 .41% deviation. Other changes are comparatively small, and several configurations are slightly better than the default on individual benchmarks, indicating that the defaults are not uniformly optimal for every setting. The sensitivity is substantially larger on LLaMEA/BBOB. For AdaEva-R, changing n0 from its default of 5 to ei-ther 1 or 10 increases the deviation to +21 .37% and +32 .29% , respectively, while increasing the evaluation step 

∆ to 3 gives +28 .92% . The statistical and population-management choices also matter: replacing Friedman with a paired t-test, replacing Conover with Nemenyi, disabling population refill, and disabling stochastic evaluation result in deviations of +14 .93% , +21 .26% , +19 .48% , and +21 .16% , respectively. For AdaEva-S, the effects are smaller, with n0 = 10 changing performance by −2.01% and η = 1 .5 by −7.74% on BBOB. Finally, replacing the native LLaMEA prompt with the partial-evaluation prompt increases the deviation by +14 .99% , providing no evidence that the modified feedback is beneficial in this setting. Overall, the results indicate that the sensitivity of AdaEva depends on both the instantiation and the benchmark. The CO results suggest that the default parameters are relatively robust to moderate changes, whereas BBOB is more sensitive to how AdaEva-R accumulates and interprets evaluation evidence.
