## ABSTRACT 

We identify a mismatch between the physical role of transport fields in many PDEs and their usual role in neural operators: PDEs use them to select read coordinates, whereas neural operators typically treat them only as input values. We address this mismatch with the Green-Routed Neural Operator 

(GRNO), which uses the governing equation to determine where latent features are sampled. A parameter-free equation adapter evaluates the diagnostic relation and constructs a departure map whose values are the read coordinates. A multiscale encoder–decoder combines centered and routed reads of latent features to learn the complete finite-time update. Across five two- and three-dimensional PDE systems, GRNO achieves the lowest mean final relative L2 error on four under 40-step autoregressive evaluation and remains competitive on Keller–Segel. Fixed-weight route interventions reveal strong dependence on direction and spatial alignment in four systems, with weak dependence in Keller–Segel. In independently trained ablations, GRNO achieves lower mean errors than variants that supply the transport field only as an input feature, substitute a learned displacement for the equation-specified route, or apply the route with a spatial misalignment, across all five systems. It also substantially outperforms directly advecting the physical state and learning the remaining update, indicating that equation-specified read coordinates provide an effective structural prior for long-horizon PDE forecasting. 

## 1 Introduction 

Neural operators learn maps between functions and provide fast surrogates for partial differential equations (PDEs) [1–3]. During autoregressive forecasting, predictions become inputs, allowing errors to accumulate across the rollout [4–6]. Existing architectures model spatial interactions through kernels, multiscale convolutions, attention, and coordinate-dependent queries; we examine how known transport fields should determine their read coordinates. In many evolution PDEs, the equation already specifies more than which field values are relevant: it specifies the coordinates from which the evolving state should be read. Let q denote the state to be advanced, with μ specifying the physical parameters, geometry, and boundary conditions. The current state may first determine an auxiliary field 

ψμ[q] through an instantaneous diagnostic relation, after which the equation converts ψμ[q] into a velocity, drift, or phase-space vector vμ[q]. We call ψμ[q] the diagnostic field and vμ[q] the diagnostic carrier . The carrier determines a departure map Ξ∆t,μ [q], whose values are the read coordinates. Sampling the previous state at these coordinates gives the transport contribution q ◦ Ξ∆t,μ [q]. Using vμ[q] as an input channel exposes its values at fixed grid indices, whereas using it to construct the departure map changes the coordinates from which features are read. Endpoint supervision constrains the map qn 7 → qn+1 while leaving the carrier-to-coordinate computation unspecified. Models such as Flowers make sampling locations explicit but learn their displacements from forecast data [7]. Our

We address this mismatch with the Green-Routed Neural Operator (GRNO). This work identifies a simple mismatch—a PDE can use a known field to choose coordinates while a neural operator receives it only as values—and provides an architecture and matched experiments for correcting and testing it. 

## 2 Related Work 

Neural-operator interactions. Graph and multipole kernels model nonlocal interactions [8,9]; Fourier and multiwavelet operators learn spectral interactions [2, 10, 11]. CNO and U-NO use multiscale architectures [12, 13], while transformer operators learn interactions through attention and coordinate queries [14–17]. 

Representations and coordinates. G-FNO, SFNO, and ReViT encode spatial symmetries [18–20], RieszNO represents directional structure [21], and RD-JEPA uses predictive latent pretraining for few-trajectory transfer across related PDEs [22]. Spatial transformers and deformable convolutions learn sampling transformations [23, 24]; Geo-FNO and GINO connect physical domains to latent grids [25, 26]. Flowers and PARADIS learn displacements or latent advection [7, 27]. GRNO constructs read coordinates from the diagnostic carrier. 

Hybrid numerical models. Hybrid solvers learn discretizations [28, 29], corrections within numerical updates [30], or inference-time corrections with numerical simulation [31]. GRNO uses the physical transport computation to select latent features, while learning the complete finite-time update. 

Green structure. Prior work learns Green kernels, constructs Green-inspired elliptic operators, or embeds discrete Green superposition [32–37]. GRNO uses the known Green inverse of a diagnostic relation to construct read coordinates for latent features. 

## 3 Motivation: The PDE determines where information is read 

Problem setup. Given fully observed training trajectories of a PDE with known coefficients and boundary conditions, we learn a map Mθ from the current field qn = q(t0 + n∆t) to the next field. Forecasting starts from one exact frame and proceeds recursively: bq0 = q0 and bqn+1 = Mθ (bqn), with prescribed static inputs omitted from the notation. Models are trained separately for each system. We use Navier–Stokes to examine how known diagnostic carriers should enter this prediction map.

## 5 Experiments 

We evaluate long-horizon predictive accuracy and isolate the contribution of equation-specified routing across five PDE systems. 

5.1 Benchmarks and protocol 

We evaluate Kolmogorov flow, dissipative SQG, Vlasov–Poisson, 3D Keller–Segel, and 3D porous convection. These systems span periodic and bounded domains, physical space and phase space, and five distinct diagnostic relations for computing the carrier (Appendix C). The baselines cover four spatial interaction mechanisms. ReViT [20] uses local canonical frames for rotationally equivariant attention. RieszNO [21] combines directional Riesz features with spectral mixing. NIPS-AR [17], our autoregressive adaptation of NIPS, combines linear attention with channel-independent Fourier kernels. Flowers [7] learns multihead displacements for feature resampling, making it the closest coordinate-warp comparison to GRNO’s equation-specified read coordinates. Baseline comparisons use matched splits, normalization, training windows, loss, optimizer updates, and checkpoint selection. Models train through K = 4 steps and are tested by free H = 40 rollouts from prescribed exact frames, with each prediction reused as the next input. We average errors over 12 prescribed test-trajectory windows within each seed and report mean ±standard deviation over seeds {0, 1, 2} for the baseline comparison. Route interventions use seeds {0, 1, 2}, whereas independently trained ablations use seed 0. Full protocols and dimensional or autoregressive adaptations are specified in Appendices F–G.

5.3 Does the fitted model use the route? 

The accuracy gains above raise a direct mechanistic question: do the fitted GRNOs actually exploit the equation-specified read coordinates, or could comparable results be achieved while ignoring the route? We answer this by keeping each checkpoint and all other operations unchanged, and modifying only the departure displacement in Eq. (19): zero 

sets dn = 0 , reverse uses −dn, and shift spatially misaligns the displacement with the current state. Each intervention increases mean final error by at least 16 .7× on Kolmogorov flow, 5.0× on SQG, 41 .0× on Vlasov– Poisson, and 13 .0× on porous convection, while Keller–Segel changes little (Table 2). Figure 2c–d shows degradation factors and rollout profiles; Appendix J.2 shows the corresponding fields and pointwise errors. The fitted predictors depend strongly on route direction and alignment in four systems, and weakly in Keller–Segel.

## 6 Discussion 

GRNO uses the diagnostic carrier both as an input feature and to determine where latent features are read. The diagnostic relation determines the diagnostic field, from which the carrier and departure map are computed. The values of the departure map are the read coordinates used by the neural operator. The equation therefore specifies the read coordinates, while the network learns the complete finite-time update. This interface applies across two-dimensional flow, phase-space dynamics, and three-dimensional transport. Fixed-weight route interventions show that four fitted predictors depend strongly on route direction and alignment. In the independently trained controls, GRNO has lower mean error than Learned route and Shifted equation-specified route 

on all five systems. It also has lower mean error than State advection + residual on every benchmark. These results indicate that performance depends on both supplying the carrier and using it to determine latent read coordinates. Dependence on routing varies by system. Keller–Segel shows little sensitivity to fixed-weight route interventions. In the ablations, GRNO improves only slightly on Carrier feature only , and Centered only has the lowest error. In the other four systems, the trained models are sensitive to route interventions, and GRNO has the lowest mean error in the ablations. These results support equation-specified latent routing when the route materially affects finite-time prediction.

## D Equation adapter, traces, and boundaries 

The adapter is deterministic, has no trainable parameters, and is recomputed from the current predicted state at every rollout step. 

D.1 Diagnostic solves Periodic active scalars. For Kolmogorov flow and SQG, the zero Fourier mode is fixed to zero and 

bψ(k) = −| k|−r bq(k), bv(k) = ( ik 2, −ik 1) bψ(k), (37) with r = 2 for vorticity and r = 1 for SQG. This is the discrete Green multiplier for the respective periodic diagnostic relation. 

Vlasov–Poisson. The adapter integrates f over velocity, removes the neutral Fourier mode, and solves Eq. (31) spectrally for E(x). It returns the phase-space carrier (v, −E(x)) rather than treating E as a second evolved state. 

Keller–Segel. The screened inverse is diagonal in Fourier space: 

bc(k) = bρ(k)

|k|2 + λ2 (k̸ = 0) , v = χ∇c. (38) The mean source is removed as in Eq. (34). 

Porous convection. The adapter first forms the buoyancy on vertical cell faces: interior faces use the average of their two adjacent concentrations, and the top and bottom face values are zero. The discrete divergence of this face buoyancy is the pressure right-hand side. The pressure equation uses the finite-volume Laplacian, diagonalized by Fourier transforms in the periodic horizontal directions and a cosine transform vertically; the zero mode is set to zero. Face velocities are computed from this buoyancy and the corresponding discrete pressure gradient, with zero normal velocity at the top and bottom. Averaging adjacent face velocities gives the cell-centered carrier supplied to the network.
