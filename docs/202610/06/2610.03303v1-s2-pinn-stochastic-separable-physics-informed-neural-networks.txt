Abstract
Uncertainty quantification (UQ) for random partial differential equations (PDEs) is
ubiquitous in computational science and engineering. However, classical spectral
solvers for this class of problems face the curse of dimensionality, and existing
neural solvers often ignore the stochastic structure that makes moments and cal-
ibration tractable. We introduce a stochastic separable physics-informed neural
network, dubbed S2-PINN, that represents the solution u(t, x, Z) of a random PDE
with a learnable Gaussian spatial dictionary, Fourier temporal features, and a gener-
alized polynomial chaos (gPC) stochastic basis, coupled by a low-rank Canonical
Polyadic (CP) tensor decomposition core. The method is trained with a hybrid
strong-form and gPC-projected residual loss. Our theoretical analysis establishes
that the separable class is dense in L2 under mild conditions, and the projected
residual corresponds exactly to a stochastic Galerkin constraint. Furthermore,
we show that mini-batch projection coefficients are logarithmically dependent
on the number of gPC modes, and that the orthogonality penalty controls the
conditioning of the learned spatial dictionary. Using four manufactured random
PDE benchmarks, we show that S2-PINN outperforms nine baselines in terms of
mean and variance accuracy, as well as calibration, while using significantly fewer
parameters. Further evaluations on non-manufactured Poisson and Darcy problems,
a stochastic Navier–Stokes problem, a diffusion scaling study of higher random
dimensions, and two stochastic inverse problems reveal the generalization capabili-
ties of the proposed structure. Together, these results support stochastic separability
as an effective design principle for physics-informed neural UQ. The code for the
experiments can be found in https://github.com/DMax1314/s2pinn.

Introduction
Uncertainty quantification (UQ) for partial differential equations (PDEs) is essential when coefficients,
boundary conditions, or forcing terms depend on random variables Z ∈RR. In this setting, the
solution u(t, x, Z) is a random field, and the primary targets are often its moments and calibration
properties. Classical stochastic solvers such as Monte Carlo Ghanem and Spanos [1991], stochastic
Galerkin Xiu and Karniadakis [2002], and stochastic collocation Babuška et al. [2007] are reliable
but costly because they require many repeated solves or large coupled systems.
Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) Raissi et al. [2019], Karniadakis et al. [2021] avoid
labeled data by enforcing PDE residual constraints, but standard PINNs typically encode Z as generic
inputs and rely on dense sampling to learn stochastic structure implicitly. Operator-learning methods
such as DeepONet Lu et al. [2021] and FNO Li et al. [2020] learn parameter-to-solution maps from
data, but require supervised datasets and degrade in stiff nonlinear regimes. Recent stochastic PINN
and physics-informed polynomial-chaos work Zhang et al. [2019], Yang et al. [2021], Psaros et al.

not jointly exploit separability across space, time, and randomness.
Separable PINNs (SPINN) Cho et al. [2023] showed that explicit separability can improve efficiency
for deterministic PDEs. In parallel, low-rank tensor methods (CP, TT, Tucker) Kolda and Bader
[2009], Oseledets [2011], Bachmayr [2023] have long addressed high-dimensional structure in PDE
and optimal control solvers Nouy [2010], Chinesta et al. [2010], Bachmayr et al. [2016], Benner
et al. [2015, 2016a,b], Antil et al. [2023, 2025a,b]. The missing ingredient is an integrated stochastic
PINN that combines learnable spatial representations, explicit stochastic basis structure, and physics
constraints enforcing both pointwise residual consistency and stochastic projection consistency.
S2-PINN fills this gap. The model represents the solution u(t, x, Z) by a three-way separable ansatz
with a learnable Gaussian spatial dictionary, Fourier temporal features, and gPC stochastic modes,
coupled through a low-rank CP core. Training combines strong-form residual minimization with
a gPC-projected residual term. Our theory supports four concrete claims: finite-rank separable
sums are expressive in L2, the projected residual equals a stochastic Galerkin constraint, its mini-
batch estimator has uniform concentration scaling as O(log G), where G is the number of gPC
modes, and the spatial orthogonality penalty controls feature conditioning. We evaluate both forward
stochastic prediction and inverse stochastic parameter identification within the same physics-informed
framework.
Contributions.
In this work, we make the following three main contributions.
(a) We introduce a stochastic separable PINN that couples a learnable spatial dictionary with
M atoms, Mt temporal Fourier features, and G gPC stochastic modes via a rank-q CP
factorization, reducing coupling complexity from dense O(MMtG) to O((M+Mt+G)q).
The hybrid strong-form and gPC-projected residual loss enforces local PDE consistency and
stochastic Galerkin consistency.
(b) We prove expressivity of the separable ansatz, projection–Galerkin equivalence, mini-batch
concentration for stochastic projection coefficients, orthogonality-conditioning control, CP
complexity, and gauge invariance. These results justify the representation and explain why
projection is useful when the solution is well aligned with the chosen stochastic basis.
(c) Among manufactured and non-manufactured benchmarks, S2-PINN improves moment accu-
racy and calibration. Additional scaling and inverse-problem studies extend the evaluation
beyond standard forward prediction, covering higher-dimensional random inputs and joint
recovery of solution fields and random-field parameters.
Our central claim is that stochastic separability provides a compact and well-aligned inductive bias
for neural UQ of random PDEs, with its strongest benefits in regimes where the solution has low-rank
space–time–random structure and a useful low-order stochastic expansion.

Related Work
Physics-Informed and Operator Learning for UQ.
Physics-informed neural networks typically
enforce PDEs via soft residual constraints Raissi et al. [2019], with recent efforts targeting Uncertainty
Quantification (UQ) via Bayesian frameworks Yang et al. [2021], auxiliary networks Zhang et al.
[2019], and polynomial chaos expansions Novák et al. [2024], Shi et al. [2026]. Parallel to PINNs,
neural operators like DeepONet Lu et al. [2021] and FNO Li et al. [2020] learn parameter-to-solution
maps entirely from data. Very recently, Neural Chaos Bahmani et al. [2025] proposed replacing fixed
polynomial bases with sequentially learned networks to break the independence assumption. However,
a common limitation spans these methods: they either inherently mandate extensive labeled training
data (data-driven operators), fail to exploit multidimensional structural separation, or rely on dense
representations that scale poorly with stochastic dimensions. S2-PINN addresses this by formulating
a strictly physics-informed, uncoupled architecture that mathematically isolates spatial derivative
costs from the stochastic basis size. Physics-informed polynomial chaos expansions (PC2) Novák
et al. [2024] are particularly relevant to our setting because they impose PDE constraints directly
on a PCE surrogate via KKT-style constrained regression. We therefore include a UQpy-based PC2
comparison as an external polynomial-chaos baseline: it tests whether a fixed global polynomial
surrogate with physics constraints can match the accuracy and calibration gains obtained from our
learnable space–time representation and low-rank stochastic coupling.
2


Low-Rank Tensor Methods in Neural Solvers.
Classical low-rank tensor decompositions (CP, TT,
Tucker) have long accelerated stochastic Galerkin solvers Doostan et al. [2013], Bigoni et al. [2016].
In the deep learning regime, recent state-of-the-art neural-tensor hybrids—such as TT-PINN Liu et al.
[2022], functional tensor decompositions Vemuri et al. [2024], and INN-TD Guo et al. [2025]—target
purely deterministic spatial/parametric coordinates. They primarily compress neural weight matrices
for edge deployment or factorize spatial axes without incorporating a statistical structural prior.
Because none embed a stochastic polynomial basis or a UQ-specific Galerkin projection, they cannot
address robust uncertainty quantification. S2-PINN fundamentally diverges by fusing a learnable
spatial dictionary with explicitly separated gPC stochastic modes, governed entirely by a hybrid
strong-form and projection constraint.
Separable PINNs for High-Dimensional PDEs.
SPINN Cho et al. [2023] first demonstrated that
explicit dimensional separability, coupled with a coordinate warp, drastically accelerates deterministic
PDE training. While it has been extended to quantum and elastic systems Es’kin et al. [2024], Zanotta
et al. [2025], seamlessly scaling SPINN to stochastic parameters is highly non-trivial. Naively
introducing random variables into SPINN yields a dense, non-factorized coefficient tensor that grows
multiplicatively with the spatial, temporal, and stochastic resolutions. S2-PINN mitigates this specific
bottleneck by eschewing the dense multi-way block for a low-rank CP coupling kernel, tightly
integrating optimal stochastic bases (gPC) with spatio-temporal atoms, a structural synthesis currently
absent in the SPINN-related literature. To isolate the effect of this design, we additionally compare
against structurally closer baselines: SPINN extended with stochastic coordinates (SPINN+Z) Cho
et al. [2023], functional CP-PINNs Vemuri et al. [2024], and Neural Chaos Bahmani et al. [2025].

Method
S2-PINN represents random PDE solutions with an explicitly separated space–time–random structure.
The method assumes that the uncertainty is encoded by a finite random vector Z with known marginals,
which allows us to choose an orthonormal gPC basis in the stochastic coordinates. The architecture
then couples spatial, temporal, and stochastic factors through a low-rank CP kernel, while the loss
combines pointwise physics constraints with a stochastic projection constraint. Appendix B provides
PDE-agnostic support for the main design choices: expressivity of separable sums, projection–
Galerkin equivalence, mini-batch concentration, orthogonality penalty conditioning, CP complexity,
and gauge invariance.

3.3
Training
At each step we sample PDE, boundary, initial, and Gram mini-batches and optimize Eq. (5) with
Adam. Time derivatives are analytic from Fourier features, while spatial derivatives, including ∆u,
use autodiff through the warped Gaussian dictionary. We periodically resample collocation points and
apply column-wise normalization to CP factors (A, B, D) after updates to fix the continuous gauge
symmetry of the CP format (Theorem B.10), which also improves the Lipschitz conditioning of the
forward map. The column normalization leaves the forward map unchanged (Theorem B.10) but
rescales Adam’s moment buffers; at the default learning rate η = 10−3, we observed no instability
from this engineering choice. Algorithm 1 and default hyperparameters are reported in Appendix E.

Experiments
4.1
Benchmark Problems
We evaluate S2-PINN on four controlled random PDEs: diffusion, Allen–Cahn, Burgers, and Darcy.
The first three are time-dependent problems that share the same manufactured target and differ in
the physical operator, so their performance gaps isolate the effect of linear diffusion, cubic reaction,
and nonlinear advection. Darcy is steady-state and uses exponential Z-dependence in the coefficient,
making it a deliberately harder case for a low-order polynomial stochastic basis.

Baselines and fairness.
We compare against nine baselines: a standard MLP PINN, the same PINN
with the gPC projection loss, PI-DeepONet Goswami et al. [2023], MC-Dropout PINN, a K=5 deep
ensemble Lakshminarayanan et al. [2017], stochastic collocation (SC) with nq=2 Gauss–Hermite
nodes per dimension (25=32 node-PINNs), and three structurally closer methods: SPINN with
the random variables appended as additional separable coordinates (SPINN+Z) Cho et al. [2023],
functional CP-PINN Vemuri et al. [2024], and Neural Chaos Bahmani et al. [2025]. Neural Chaos is
supervised by reference solutions and therefore serves as a data-driven oracle rather than a purely
physics-informed competitor. All neural methods use the same optimizer family, collocation strategy,
and training budget unless stated otherwise. The vanilla PINN has about 28× more parameters than
S2-PINN (roughly 200K versus 7K), so parameter count does not favor the proposed architecture.
Additional architecture and optimization details are in Appendix D.

S2-PINN achieves the lowest observed errors across benchmarks.
Across diffusion, Allen–
Cahn, and Burgers (polynomial stochastic dependence), we observe lower mean errors and lower
variance errors for S2-PINN with projection relative to the best included baseline. The no-projection
variant remains competitive, which separates the architectural gain of the CP/gPC coupling from the
additional regularization effect of the projected residual. Darcy is more nuanced: S2-PINN attains
the best mean accuracy, but the no-projection variant gives the best variance, already indicating that
projection is not uniformly beneficial outside the polynomial regime.
Baselines.
Stochastic collocation trains 32 independent node-PINNs and reaches only vanilla-PINN-
level accuracy under our matched setting; it cannot share representations across random modes. The
moment errors of PI-DeepONet remain about one order of magnitude above S2-PINN. MC-dropout
has substantially larger moment errors and under-covers in the calibration table. Deep ensembles
improve stability over a single PINN, but use five full networks and remain less accurate on the
controlled benchmarks. Among the structurally closer baselines, SPINN+Z is well calibrated but
its moment errors are 1.5–33× larger than those of S2-PINN, functional CP-PINN is less accurate
and less calibrated, and even the supervised Neural Chaos oracle does not reach S2-PINN accuracy,
indicating that the gains stem from the gPC basis combined with CP coupling rather than from
separability alone.

Conclusion
In this work, we have presented S2-PINN (Stochastic Separable Physics-Informed Neural Network),
an efficient framework that extends separable coordinate architectures to forward and inverse un-
certainty quantification for random PDEs. By coupling learnable Gaussian spatial atoms, Fourier
temporal features, and an orthonormal gPC stochastic basis via a low-rank CP tensor decomposition,
S2-PINN enables closed-form statistical moment evaluation and circumvents the curse of dimen-
sionality inherent in dense space–time–stochastic grids. Our analysis also shows that S2-PINN’s
gains come from these coupling three aligned ingredients. For polynomial-in-Z problems, the projec-
tion encourages residual consistency in the stochastic modes used by the model, and Theorem B.3
identifies the corresponding population objective with a stochastic Galerkin constraint. The theory
further supports the main mechanism by specializing standard tools (tensor-product density, orthog-
onal projection, and Monte Carlo concentration) to the proposed architecture and establishing L2
expressivity of finite-rank separable sums, mini-batch concentration for projected coefficients, and
conditioning control for the learned spatial dictionary. Together, the theory and ablations support
low-rank stochastic separability as an effective modeling principle for physics-informed neural UQ.
Limitations and future work. Although S2-PINN shows great promise and outperforms across
forward prediction, calibration, scaling, and inverse problems, there remain some frontiers for
making S2-PINN a more general random PDE solver. First, S2-PINN currently inherits the regular-
domain bias of separable coordinate architectures, and extending the method to complex geometries
will require geometry-aware dictionaries, coordinate maps, or mesh-based variants. Second, our
theoretical results establish asymptotic expressivity and characterize the projection estimator under
stated assumptions, but not provide a finite-q, finite-G end-to-end solution-error guarantee: a finite
rank q introduces low-rank approximation error and a finite G introduces stochastic truncation error.
A promising way forward to overcome these limitations is to combine low-rank tensor representations
with adaptive rank selection, learnable gPC truncation strategies to capture the distribution of random
variables, and geometry-adaptive coordinate mappings.
Broader impact. Fast physics-informed UQ can support safer engineering design, climate and
energy modeling, and medical-device simulation by making uncertainty estimates more accessible
when repeated high-fidelity solves are expensive. The main risk is misuse through overconfident
deployment: inaccurate calibration or unresolved variance in difficult stochastic regimes could
lead to misleading confidence estimates. For this reason, real-world use should pair S2-PINN with
problem-specific validation, calibration checks, and conservative uncertainty reporting.

inputs, the stochastic PDE is transformed into a deterministic, high-dimensional parametric PDE over
the domain Ω× T × RR.

B
Theoretical Analysis
In this section, we collect PDE-agnostic results that support the main design choices in S2-PINN:
finite-rank separable expressivity, projection–Galerkin consistency, mini-batch stability of projected
residual coefficients, orthogonality conditioning regularization, and the algebraic efficiency of the CP
coupling. Instead of proving optimization convergence or finite-rank rates for a specific PDE, these
results formalize the mechanism used by the architecture and loss. We emphasize that this section
does not introduce new abstract functional-analytic tools: the arguments rely on standard results
(density of algebraic tensor products, orthogonal projection and Parseval identities, and Monte Carlo
concentration). Their method-specific content is twofold. (i) Expressive completeness: Theorem B.2
shows that a low-rank CP coupling of spatio-temporal features with a gPC basis preserves universal
approximation in L2, so the factorization introduces no representation bottleneck in the limit. (ii)
Error decomposition: Theorems B.2–B.4 separate the total error into CP-rank approximation error
(controlled by q), gPC truncation error (controlled by p), and Monte Carlo sampling error (controlled
by the batch size B), which guides the balance between network capacity, spectral degree, and
sampling budget.
Remark B.1 (Scope of the guarantees). The results below are asymptotic or estimator-level statements.
They do not provide a finite-q, finite-G end-to-end bound on the solution error of a trained model: a
finite rank q incurs low-rank approximation error, a finite G incurs stochastic truncation error, and
non-convex optimization under a finite budget is not covered.

of the factor matrix A by λ while simultaneously dividing the r-th element of the weight w by λ

by constraining the L2 norm of each column to 1). Enforcing the constraint ∥A:,r∥2 = 1 strictly
limits the permissible diagonal scaling factors to |λ| = 1. This implies that the diagonal entries of

discrete sign permutations. Combined with the inherent column permutation symmetry of the rank-1
components in CP decomposition, we successfully fix the gauge up to permutations and signs.

parameter (e.g., matrix A) is governed by the Lipschitz constant of its partial derivative. In the
multilinear CP format, the partial derivative of u with respect to A heavily depends on the magnitudes

result, the optimization process (such as gradient descent) becomes substantially more stable and

Deep Ensembles.
Following Lakshminarayanan et al. Lakshminarayanan et al. [2017], we train
an ensemble of K = 5 independent Vanilla PINNs, each initialized with a different random seed.
Each member uses the same Vanilla-PINN architecture (4 hidden layers × 256, tanh) and the same
20,000-step training schedule, but draws its own collocation samples and weight initialization from
the seed. Members are trained completely independently and then aggregated post-hoc: the prediction
is the per-point mean and the epistemic uncertainty is the per-point standard deviation across the
K members. Uncertainty-quantification metrics are computed with 1024 stochastic samples each
for moment estimation, interval coverage, and held-out test points (n_z_moment =n_z_interval =
n_z_test =1024). Deep ensembles are widely considered a gold-standard baseline for predictive
uncertainty. Unlike the other no-projection baselines, the Deep-Ensembles launch script does not
override wproj, so each member inherits wproj=1.0 from the base configuration. This is intentional,
since the strongest possible Deep-Ensembles baseline includes the same projection regularizer used
by S2-PINN.

Physics-informed polynomial chaos (PC2).
We implement the PC2 baseline of Novák et al. Novák
et al. [2024] using UQpy’s physics-informed PCE stack. Dirichlet data are used as boundary regression

basis over all variables as the main comparison; instead, we use an anisotropic product basis with
stochastic total degree fixed at pZ=3 and deterministic degree pdet swept over {3, 4, 5} for diffusion
and {4, 6, 8} for Poisson/Darcy. Model selection uses only held-out PDE/boundary residuals. This
keeps the stochastic gPC capacity aligned with S2-PINN while giving PC2 a reasonable deterministic
polynomial space.
SPINN+Z.
A separable PINN Cho et al. [2023] in which the random variables Z1, . . . , ZR are
treated as additional separable input coordinates, each processed by its own coordinate network, with
the per-coordinate features combined by the SPINN outer-product/summation rule. It is trained with
LPINN and contains no explicit stochastic basis or projection term.
Functional CP-PINN.
The functional tensor decomposition PINN of Vemuri et al. Vemuri et al.
[2024], in which each coordinate (space, time, and random variables) is assigned a separate sub-
network and the outputs are coupled by a CP outer product. It is trained with LPINN.
Neural Chaos.
Neural Chaos Bahmani et al. [2025] replaces the fixed polynomial chaos basis
with sequentially learned neural basis functions. As a data-driven operator, it is trained on reference
solution realizations and therefore acts as a supervised oracle rather than a purely physics-informed
competitor; a physics residual is only evaluated post hoc. Since diffusion, Allen–Cahn, and Burgers
share the same manufactured solution field, a single shared checkpoint is used for these three
equations, and only the equation-specific post-hoc residual differs.

the checklist: The papers not including the checklist will be desk rejected. The checklist should
follow the references and follow the (optional) supplemental material. The checklist does NOT count
towards the page limit.
Please read the checklist guidelines carefully for information on how to answer these questions. For
each question in the checklist:
• You should answer [Yes], [No], or [N/A].
• [N/A] means either that the question is Not Applicable for that particular paper or the
relevant information is Not Available.
• Please provide a short (1–2 sentence) justification right after your answer (even for [N/A]).
The checklist answers are an integral part of your paper submission. They are visible to the
reviewers, area chairs, senior area chairs, and ethics reviewers. You will also be asked to include it
(after eventual revisions) with the final version of your paper, and its final version will be published
with the paper.
The reviewers of your paper will be asked to use the checklist as one of the factors in their evaluation.
While [Yes] is generally preferable to [No], it is perfectly acceptable to answer [No] provided a
proper justification is given (e.g., error bars are not reported because it would be too computationally
expensive” or “we were unable to find the license for the dataset we used”). In general, answering
[No] or [N/A] is not grounds for rejection. While the questions are phrased in a binary way, we
acknowledge that the true answer is often more nuanced, so please just use your best judgment and
write a justification to elaborate. All supporting evidence can appear either in the main paper or the
supplemental material, provided in appendix. If you answer [Yes] to a question, in the justification
please point to the section(s) where related material for the question can be found.

1. Claims
Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the
paper’s contributions and scope?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: The abstract and introduction accurately describe the proposed contributions
about theoretical and experimental results.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the abstract and introduction do not include the claims
made in the paper.
• The abstract and/or introduction should clearly state the claims made, including the
contributions made in the paper and important assumptions and limitations. A [No] or
[N/A] answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers.
• The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how
much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings.

2. Limitations
Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: We discuss limitations of our work in Section 5
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper has no limitation while the answer [No] means
that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.
• The authors are encouraged to create a separate “Limitations” section in their paper.
• The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to
violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings,
model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors
should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the
implications would be.
• The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was
only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often
depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.
• The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach.
For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution
is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be
used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle
technical jargon.
• The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms
and how they scale with dataset size.
• If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to
address problems of privacy and fairness.
• While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by
reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover
limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best
judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an impor-
tant role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community. Reviewers
will be specifically instructed to not penalize honesty concerning limitations.

4. Experimental result reproducibility
Question: Does the paper fully disclose all the information needed to reproduce the main ex-
perimental results of the paper to the extent that it affects the main claims and/or conclusions
of the paper (regardless of whether the code and data are provided or not)?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: Yes, we include all experimental details necessary to reproduce. Pseudo code
for the algorithm is given in Algorithm 1 and default parameters are listed in Appendix E.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
• If the paper includes experiments, a [No] answer to this question will not be perceived
well by the reviewers: Making the paper reproducible is important, regardless of
whether the code and data are provided or not.
• If the contribution is a dataset and/or model, the authors should describe the steps taken
to make their results reproducible or verifiable.
• Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways.
For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully
might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may
be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same
dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often
one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed
instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case
of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are
appropriate to the research performed.
• While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submis-
sions to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the
nature of the contribution. For example
(a) If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how
to reproduce that algorithm.
(b) If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe
the architecture clearly and fully.
(c) If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should
either be a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce
the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct
the dataset).
(d) We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case
authors are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility.
In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in
some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers
to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results.

Question: Does the paper report error bars suitably and correctly defined or other appropriate

Answer: [Yes]
Justification:

Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
• The authors should answer [Yes] if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence
intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the
main claims of the paper.
• The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated (for

• The assumptions made should be given (e.g., Normally distributed errors).
• It should be clear whether the error bar is the standard deviation or the standard error

error rates).

• The conference expects that many papers will be foundational research and not tied
to particular applications, let alone deployments. However, if there is a direct path to
any negative applications, the authors should point it out. For example, it is legitimate

strategies (e.g., gated release of models, providing defenses in addition to attacks,
mechanisms for monitoring misuse, mechanisms to monitor how a system learns from
