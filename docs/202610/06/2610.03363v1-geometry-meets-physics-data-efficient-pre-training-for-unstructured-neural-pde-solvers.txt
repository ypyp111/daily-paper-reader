Abstract
Neural surrogate models for Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) on unstruc-
tured 3D geometries are often limited by poor generalization and the high cost of
generating large-scale training datasets. Consequently, pre-training on massive
datasets of related PDE dynamics has emerged as a critical alternative to enhance
the robustness and scalability of these models. However, this strategy is neither
compute- nor data-efficient, as it relies on massive pre-computed data that is very
costly to generate. In this work, we introduce a disk-data-free pre-training frame-
work tailored to both steady-state and transient regimes. For steady-state problems,
we propose a geometry-driven strategy that leverages intrinsic shape descriptors
to learn representations of complex 3D domains. For transient problems, we in-
troduce a physics-driven approach based on online generation of synthetic PDE
data, enabling scalable pre-training without reliance on expensive datasets. Across
multiple experiments, our approach achieves faster convergence, greater data effi-
ciency, and higher accuracy during fine-tuning, particularly under realistic low-data
regimes. This methodology provides a practical pathway toward data-efficient
neural emulators for large-scale simulations.

Introduction
Engineering workflows have long depended on high-fidelity numerical solvers to resolve intricate
physical systems [1–4]. Nevertheless, their immense computational overhead severely limits their
feasibility in time-sensitive settings, iterative design exploration, and closed-loop control. Data-
driven surrogate models provide a compelling pathway toward high-throughput inference [5–8]. Yet,
practical adoption of these emulators remains critically constrained by an underlying bottleneck:
their performance hinges on massive, pre-computed simulation datasets whose generation is itself
computationally prohibitive. Motivated by advances in Large Language Models [9, 10, LLMs],
foundation models for PDEs have adapted pre-training and fine-tuning paradigms to physical sys-
tems [11–15]. Yet, these approaches still rely heavily on extensive offline datasets and predominantly
target structured grids without geometric variation. Although recent work on unstructured meshes
explores geometric pre-training [16, 17], capturing the coupled dynamics of geometry and physics
remains challenging without costly labeled simulations. This motivates our central question: to what
extent can unstructured pre-training be realized efficiently without any precomputed simulation data?
We categorize unstructured physical problems into two regimes with distinct dynamics: steady-state
and transient. Steady-state problems correspond to equilibrium solutions where physical fields, e.g.,
surface pressure and shear stress in aerodynamics, are fully dictated by the geometry, boundary
conditions and operating conditions. Consequently, pre-training purely on geometric descriptors
can yield representations directly transferable to downstream flow prediction. In contrast, transient
simulations evolve states autoregressively, with dynamics conditioned jointly on prior physical states

To circumvent the prohibitive, often intractable burden of generating massive pre-computed datasets,
we develop an entirely storage-free pre-training paradigm that constructs supervisory data on-the-fly.
For steady-state regimes, we generate random assemblies of geometric primitives online and train
the model to reconstruct essential differential descriptors, including surface normals, curvature, and
Vector Distance Fields (VDF). For transient autoregressive settings, we avoid offline trajectory storage
by generating solutions to simple, canonical PDEs online during training. Together, these online
strategies instill robust geometric and physical inductive biases at reduced computational overhead,
bypassing the prohibitive cost of large-scale offline simulation repositories.
Our main contributions are: (I) We present Geometry Meets Physics (GMP), a modular pre-training
framework with two distinct variants for steady-state and transient physics simulations on unstructured
meshes that bypasses the need to store precomputed PDE solutions and expensive CAD-level
geometries. (II) We demonstrate its effectiveness to enable faster convergence, data savings and
superior accuracy during fine-tuning, particularly in realistic low-data regimes. Additionally, we show
that it is inherently architecture-agnostic. (III) We propose GMP-Net, a modified SOTA architecture
specifically designed to efficiently pre-train for both transient and steady-state problems and to boost
performance. In summary, Table 1 shows the key structural advantages of GMP. Relative to prior
work, GMP requires zero offline data storage, dramatically accelerates pre-training time and supports
multiple downstream tasks from a single pre-training stage.

Related work
Neural PDE solvers
Early efforts in neural physics surrogate modeling mainly employed convolu-
tional neural networks (CNNs) over regular Cartesian grids [18–20]. Concurrently, architectures such
as DeepONet [21] and Fourier Neural Operators (FNO) [22] emerged as alternative architectures with
varying advantages and disadvantages. Motivated by the success of attention-based architectures in
LLMs [23] and computer vision (CV, [24–26]), recent research has adapted Transformers to PDEs,
yielding architectures such as FactFormer [27], scOT [13], and PDE-Transformer [28, 29]. Some
of these even include physics biases in the form of hard constraints, such as rotational equivariance
[30, 31]. Nonetheless, conventional CNNs and vision Transformers remain inherently constrained to
Euclidean structured grids. To support irregular computational domains, Graph Neural Networks
(GNNs) rapidly gained traction [32–35]. Early GNNs relied primarily on local message passing
and frequently encountered scalability bottlenecks on large-scale 3D meshes comprising millions
2


of vertices. Subsequent frameworks have addressed these constraints by combining graph-based
topological structures with attention mechanisms [36], extending Transformers to unstructured
point clouds and meshes, including OctFormer [37], OFormer [38], RIGNO [39], UPT [40], and
GIST [41]. A persistent challenge is the quadratic complexity O(N 2) of standard attention, which
becomes prohibitive for high-resolution 3D simulations. Recent models deploy diverse efficiency
strategies: restricting attention to localized geometric neighborhoods (e.g., GAOT [42], Erwin [43],
PTV3 [44]) or the use of subsampled anchor points e.g., AB-UPT [45] and SMART [46]. In parallel,
Transolver [47] and its adaptations, such as GeoTransolver [48], address this scaling bottleneck by
utilizing physics-informed sliced attention.
Pre-training physics surrogate models
While physics foundation models still trail progress in
LLMs and CV, several recent frameworks have narrowed this gap [49]. DPOT [15], Poseidon [13],
PhysiX [12], and Walrus [14] represent recent examples, scaling up to one billion parameters across
domains ranging from astrophysics to plasma physics. Others, such as Yang et al. [50], explore
particular applications in aerodynamic design. However, data-driven physics foundation models
still require massive precomputed datasets. While frameworks like Tadpole [11] utilize online data
generation via GPU-based spectral solvers, they remain restricted to regular grids. Furthermore, the
approaches above are typically designed for autoregressive temporal rollouts rather than supporting
direct geometry-to-physics mappings, such as predicting steady-state aerodynamics around complex
geometries. For this latter setting, recent research emphasizes leveraging intrinsic geometric features
through specialized pre-training. For instance, the masked autoencoder (MAE) paradigm [51]
has been adapted to physics [52], while GeoPT [16] introduced geometric pre-training via lifting.
Similarly, others set up pre-train architectures to predict the occupancy of complex shapes without
corresponding physics labels [17]. To date, no prior work has presented a modular framework
for geometric and physics pre-training, nor demonstrated the effectiveness of storage-free online
pre-training for unstructured simulations.

Architecture
While the pre-training design described so far is agnostic to the specific architecture, assessing
the most competitive architectures remains crucial for achieving optimal performance. Hence, we
benchmark several modern neural operators: AB-UPT, Erwin, PTV3, Transolver++, GeoTransolver
and SMART across three test cases DrivAerML [56], SHIFT-Wing [57], and SHIFT-Crash [58]
(Appendix E). Our evaluation shows that SMART consistently achieves the highest standalone
accuracy. As such, it presents the most challenging baseline to improve upon via pre-training and is
chosen as the central architecture for the experiments below.
Architectural extensions for transient dynamics
Our ultimate goal is to design a modular archi-
tecture that excels across both steady-state and transient regimes while maximizing the benefits of
pre-training. As the regular SMART architecture cannot perform autoregressive rollouts, we general-
ize it into a modular triple-encoder framework (Figure 5). A geometric encoder EG embeds boundary
shapes, while dedicated physical encoders ES and EV process surface and volumetric physical states
ut, respectively. Each branch applies sinusoidal positional encodings and compresses point clouds
via anchor-based cross-attention into latent tokens zG, zS, zV . Decoders then cross-attend target
query coordinates xQ to these latents to predict ut+1, balanced by learned task weights wi:

facilitates decoupled pre-training: the geometric branch is pre-trained using geometric objectives
(Section 3.1), while the physical branches are pre-trained online without stored simulation data
(Section 3.2). We denote this new extended model as GMP-Net.

Transient Datasets
Given that transient benchmarks on unstructured meshes are relatively scarce,
we employ a combination of synthetic experiments and established open datasets. First, we construct
a synthetic testbed by interpolating planar Kuramoto–Sivashinsky (KS) solutions onto deformed,
irregular grids. While pre-training leverages static 3D fields, this setup verifies whether the ar-
chitecture generalizes across spatial dimensions and captures unfamiliar temporal dynamics. For
instance, to solve 2D downstream tasks, coordinates are zero-padded to (x, y, 0). Second, we adopt
the 2D flow past an ellipse from DGN [59] to predict time-dependent pressure and velocity fields.
Lastly, we evaluate the dynamic response of SHIFT-Crash over the given 12 discrete time steps. For
autoregressive rollout experiments, models unroll predictions across the entire temporal sequence;
under the steady-state formulation, the model directly maps initial geometries and crash parameters
to the final deformed state. Comprehensive benchmark details are provided in Table 2, where Type
indicates volumetric (V) or surface-based (S) meshes, and the transient sample count denotes total
trajectories multiplied by the temporal resolution per rollout.

5
Conclusions, limitations and future work
We have introduced GMP, an innovative pre-training framework for unstructured simulations based
on online generation of physics data and advanced geometric descriptors. GMP enhances the accuracy
of diverse downstream tasks, achieving greater data efficiency and faster convergence. Since the pre-
training setup is computationally lightweight, it can be integrated as a standard pre-processing step to
improve performance without a substantial increase in total training time. A remaining challenge
is mitigating overfitting in highly complex, low-data scenarios. Regarding geometric pre-training,
interesting questions remain concerning the selection of geometric descriptors, and in the future we
plan to explore whether additional features enhance model robustness and generalization. Importantly,
unlike training from scratch, GMP amortizes initial computational costs by accelerating downstream
convergence and reducing data requirements, ultimately making the design cycle more efficient. This
paves the way for faster and more accurate surrogate models in science and engineering.

A.3
Family of PDEs
Following the design philosophy of the Tadpole framework for regular grids [11], our physics pre-training
strategy relies on representation learning via spatial autoencoding rather than learning multi-channel temporal
trajectories. Consequently, the primary objective of PDE selection is not to match downstream dynamics, but to
expose the spatial autoencoder to a comprehensive set of spatial features, including smooth diffusive gradients,
sharp shock interfaces, and multi-scale turbulent vortices.
To achieve complete spectral coverage, the chosen suite of initial conditions (IC) and pre-training equations,
e.g., diffusion, advection, wave equations, reaction-diffusion... was curated to cover the full spectrum of spatial
wavenumbers. Sampling single-channel spatial crops on-the-fly ensures the representation remains system-
agnostic and decoupled from any specific state vector or time-stepping scheme, while preserving power spectral
density across high and low frequencies. Figure 12 shows the spectrum of the solutions to different PDEs with
different IC, showing a very varied spectral behavior. Samples are cropped to 323 regions for our experiments.
A.4
Physics pre-training results
Because training samples are synthesized dynamically on-the-fly at each iteration, the optimization pipeline
operates without a static validation set. Quantitatively, the self-supervised masked reconstruction objective
demonstrates high fidelity, reaching a normalized mean squared error (NMSE) on the order of 10−4. This
applies for the pre-training autoencoding objective on single snapshots, without any dynamics. The model is
later fine-tuned to learn dynamic evolution between consecutive time steps, naturally a more difficult task.

More on the comparison against other pre-training strategies
Although GeoPT [16] suggests that static geometric pre-training alone yields negligible downstream gains, our
findings demonstrate that static pre-training can substantially improve fine-tuning accuracy without relying on
complex synthetic dynamics or high-fidelity CAD models as in Zhang et al. [17]. This performance gain is
driven by three key components:
• Enriched geometric descriptors: Complementing signed distance fields (SDF) or binary occu-
pancy with explicit surface normals and curvature significantly improves representation quality and
downstream transfer.
• Conflict-free optimization via ConFIG: As demonstrated in our ablation study, scalarized multi-task
loss balancing fails to fully exploit the geometric objectives due to conflicting gradient directions.
ConFIG resolves this negative transfer across pre-training objectives.
• Staged fine-tuning via backbone freezing: Adapting the model to downstream dimensionalities
requires training task-specific input and output projection layers from scratch. Implementing an initial
warmup phase—where the pre-trained backbone remains frozen—prevents large early gradient updates
from corrupting the learned feature space (representational collapse), preserving transfer performance.
Table 3 presents a direct comparison against GeoPT as a representative geometric pre-training baseline. To ensure
a strictly controlled and equitable evaluation, we executed both the GeoPT pre-training phase and subsequent
fine-tuning within our experimental framework, enforcing identical data partitions, backbone architectures,
and optimization hyperparameters. Because the official GeoPT repository omits the end-to-end pre-training
pipeline required to reproduce their published benchmarks out of the box, we re-implemented their pre-training
methodology adhering strictly to the specifications detailed in their manuscript. While minor numerical
discrepancies are unavoidable in the absence of an end-to-end public release, this unified setup ensures a faithful,
apples-to-apples comparison. For completeness, Table 9 compiles the original numerical results reported by
the GeoPT authors. These results are not directly comparable to ours. Although we both consider three similar
downstream scenarios (car aerodynamics, aircraft aerodynamics, and automotive crash tests), only DrivAerML
uses exactly the same dataset, while the other two are different. Besides, model sizes, train/test data splits, the
particular output fields predicted, and other hyperparameters may also have a major influence on the final results.
Despite these differences, the comparison still provides an informative external reference point.

Fine-tuning in complete datasets
Prioritizing performance in low-data regimes is central to the core
mission of foundation models in scientific machine learning. In real-world engineering workflows, generating
massive 3D CFD/FEM datasets is computationally prohibitive—for instance, generating a single sample in
DrivAerML required 61,000 CPU hours. Practical engineering teams operate predominantly in data-constrained
settings. Prior pre-training frameworks exhibit the exact same trend; for instance, GeoPT achieves its highest
relative gains under lower sample counts. Nonetheless, the transient results in the main text already show that
pre-training can enhance accuracy even when working with complete big datasets. Besides, for steady-state
problems, in Table 25 we now show that pre-training still provides slight improvements in accuracy when using
complete datasets. However, these improvements are marginal for some models. Still, even in data regimes where
accuracy gains plateau, pre-training significantly accelerates training convergence speed, offering substantial
practical savings in training time and computational resources.
