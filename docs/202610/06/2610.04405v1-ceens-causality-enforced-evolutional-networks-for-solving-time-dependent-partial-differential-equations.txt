# CEENs: Causality-enforced evolutional networks for solving time-dependent partial differential equations

Abstract 

Despite the growing popularity of physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), their applicability in the long-time integration of partial differential equations (PDEs) remains constrained. We argue that this prob-lem stems from the lack of consideration of temporal causality in the original PINN formulation, resulting in a bias towards satisfying governing equations at later times before learning the initial condition and hence leading to erroneous solutions. To this end, we propose a novel method that seamlessly integrates temporal causality into the training process. Drawing inspiration from classical numerical methods where the temporal causality is reflected, we divide the time domain into nonoverlapping subintervals, assign a unique neural network to each subinterval, and construct a loss function founded on the integral form of PDEs within these subintervals. The proposed networks undergo sequential training, beginning with the initial time step. Our method demonstrates significant improvement in accuracy for long-time simulations of various PDE problems where the original PINN method fails while it requires less computational cost and memory compared to the PINN method. A parallelization algorithm is provided to further enhance the computational efficiency, showing a significant speedup for solving time-dependent PDEs. 

Keywords: Physics-informed neural network, Long-time integration, Partial differential equation, Predictive modeling, Nonlinear dynamics 

1. Introduction 

The tremendous growth of machine learning technology has led to explosive advancement in various fields such as natural language processing, data mining, and computer vision. In particular, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) [1–5] have been proposed and become a new trend of solving partial differential equations (PDEs) using machine learning. The effectiveness of PINNs has been demonstrated in a variety of PDE problems including the forward and inverse problems [1, 6–9], and uncertainty quantification [8–17]. The use of neural networks for PDE has also been proposed three decades ago [18, 19], but use of large-scale neural networks was computationally prohibitive at the time. Despite the notable achievements of PINNs in solving PDEs, there remains a significant challenge in integrating time-dependent PDEs given initial conditions [23–29]. Time-dependent PDEs frequently arise in various science and engineering models; therefore, it is important to understand the underlying pathologies of PINNs in handling such situations and enhance their efficacy in addressing the challenges. The literature has highlighted that a major obstacle of PINNs in solving time-dependent PDEs is the lack of consideration of temporal causality in the training process. This means that PINNs tend to be biased towards satisfying governing equations at later times before even learning the initial condition, leading to an inaccurate solution while having low training loss. To address this pathology, a number of variants of PINNs have been proposed to incorporate temporal causality during training. In [24, 27, 28], sequential learning strategies of PINN have been proposed to learn the solution from early time steps. The authors in [25, 30–35] employ recurrent neural networks, which are known to effectively handle time series data, to capture temporal causality. In [26, 36, 37], the authors train operators that predict a solution after a certain period of time from the current state. The iterative application of these operators, starting from the initial condition, is used to determine a solution at a specific time. The methods proposed in [23, 24, 38, 39] divide the time domain into short intervals and train a solution in each interval. The authors in [1, 29] assign a neural network at each time step and train them sequentially. The loss functions of the assigned neural networks are defined using the Runge-Kutta method, widely used method of integrating ordinary differential equations (ODEs). Taking cues from classical numerical methods that naturally respect temporal causality, we propose a method involving time-domain decomposition and enforcing sequential learning from the initial time. Our method divides the time domain into non-overlapping subintervals, assigning a unique neural network to each subinterval, and constructs a loss function based on the integral form of PDEs within these subintervals. Sequential training is enforced, beginning with the initial condition and proceeding through each subsequent subinterval. Although similar to the discrete PINN method based on the Runge-Kutta method in [1], our approach offers key distinctions and advantages, including the construction of a loss function using a linear multi-step method that allows for parallelization by avoiding the need for intermediate step calculations. In particular, we use the trapezoidal rule for the loss function, known for its second-order accuracy in terms of the time step. In addition we introduce a parallelization algorithm that can significantly accelerate the training for the long-time integration of PDEs. Our main contributions can be summarized as: • We propose a novel method to address the problem of temporal causality for time-dependent PDEs by dividing the time domain into subintervals, each equipped with a neural network, and enforcing a sequential training that initiates from the very initial time step and proceeds through each subsequent subinterval. • We provide a parallelization algorithm designed to expedite the training process, providing a substan-tial speedup and enhanced computational efficiency. • We demonstrate the effectiveness of our model for learning long-time integration of PDEs where the original PINN method fails. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the original formulation of PINN proposed in [1] and explain why it fails to reflect temporal causality in the training process. To address this 2limitation, we propose a new model in Section 3 that effectively incorporates temporal causality and describe its training process. In addition, we elaborate on practical considerations to improve the efficiency of our proposed method and provide the algorithm. To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method, Section 4 presents state-of-the-art results in a set of challenging time-dependent PDE problems where the original PINN method fails to solve. In Section 5, we conclude the paper with a brief summary, some limitations, and future research directions.

Causality-enforced evolutional networks (CEENs) 

In this section, we propose causality-enforced evolutional networks to enforce the sequential learning and its parallelization to enhance the computational efficiency, followed by a detailed analysis of the proposed method.

Memory efficiency. The memory efficiency of CEENs is superior compared to PINNs, attributed to several reasons. Firstly, unlike PINNs that aim to model solutions across the entire spatio-temporal domain, CEENs focus on modeling solutions within a short temporal domain, thus leveraging smaller networks. This implies that the number of parameters to be updated during the training of CEEN is significantly lower compared to PINNs. Secondly, while PINNs’ training utilizes points spanning the entire spatio-temporal domain, CEENs employ fewer training points, confined to a short temporal domain. Additionally, CEENs avoid the usage of automatic differentiation in the time direction, resulting in a further decrease in memory usage. Therefore, CEEN demands considerably less memory for gradient computation in updating parameters compared to PINN. While PINN occupies the GPU with the update of numerous parameters, CEEN pro-cesses parameter updates swiftly, even within constrained GPU environments. This memory efficiency po-tentially makes large-scale simulations of complex problems feasible, which might prove challenging or even unachievable when using PINNs.

Comparison with classical numerical methods The proposed method is similar to classical nu-merical methods for solving time-dependent PDEs in the sense that it sequentially computes the solution 10 at each time step. However, CEEN presents several advantages over these classical methods. Firstly, CEEN can precisely compute the spatial derivatives of the solution. In contrast, conventional mesh-based numer-ical methods often require the dense placement of spatial points to achieve the desired accuracy in spatial derivatives. CEEN, by leveraging a neural network for each time step, facilitates the exact computation of derivatives via automatic differentiation, thereby eliminating approximation errors common in traditional methods. Secondly, CEEN offers flexibility in the positioning of spatial points. Traditional numerical meth-ods might necessitate specific arrangements of spatial points to ensure accurate computation of derivatives, a restriction CEEN does not impose. This adaptability proves advantageous, particularly in scenarios where data is only available at certain locations within the spatial domain. Thirdly, after training on specific spa-tial points, the solution at any arbitrary point in CEEN can be instantly obtained with a single forward pass through the trained network. This feature significantly enhances the efficiency and applicability of the method, providing immediate solutions at any desired location. Finally, leveraging automatic differentia-tion for the precise calculation of spatial derivatives enables us to overcome traditional constraints on time step and spatial grid sizes mandated by classical methods for stability, such as the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition. This advantage allows for the selection of time step sizes independent of grid dimensions. However, unlike classical methods that have established convergence analyses, such as the Lax Equivalence Theorem, an equivalent convergence analysis for PINNs, addressing aspects like generalization error, is not yet available. This area, requiring a more profound understanding of PINNs, remains beyond the scope of this paper but represents a significant avenue for future research. 5. Conclusion 

In this study, we have developed and demonstrated a novel sequential learning strategy, termed as CEEN, designed to solve long-time integration of PDEs. We achieved this by dividing the time domain into non-overlapping subintervals, assigning a unique neural network to each subinterval, and constructing a loss function based on the integral form of PDEs within these subintervals. Then, we enforced a sequential training, beginning with the initial time step. Throughout the application of our method to various PDE problems, including the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation, Schrödinger equation, convection equation, and Allen-Cahn equation, we have consistently observed superior performance compared to the PINN. We have also demonstrated the second-order accuracy with respect to the time step and the half-order accuracy with respect to the tolerance. These findings validate the theoretical error analysis and provide insights into the accuracy of our method. However, more comprehensive error analysis will be left for future work. While we have used the trapezoidal rule as one of many potential linear-multistep methods for formulating the loss function, the flexibility of our approach allows for the straightforward incorporation of any other linear-multistep methods, such as Adams-Moulton methods, for loss function construction. This extensibility opens up avenues for further research. High-order methods, for example, are anticipated to accommodate larger time steps and ensure high-order accuracy, which will be investigated in future work.
