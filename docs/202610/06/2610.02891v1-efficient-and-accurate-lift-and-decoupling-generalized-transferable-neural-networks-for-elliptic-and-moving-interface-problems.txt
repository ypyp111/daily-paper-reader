Efficient and Accurate Lift-and-Decoupling Generalized Transferable Neural
Networks for Elliptic and Moving Interface Problems
Tao Chenga, Junqing Jiaa, Lili Jub,∗, Xiaoping Zhanga
aSchool of Mathematics and Statistics, Wuhan University, Wuhan, 430072, Hubei, China
bDepartment of Mathematics, University of South Carolina, Columbia, 29208, SC, USA
Abstract
In this paper, we propose a highly efficient and accurate method, called “lift-and-decoupling gener-
alized transferable neural network" (LD-GTransNet), for numerical solution of elliptic and moving
interface problems. Our method makes use of the multi-layer GTransNet approach and incorporates
a novel lift-and-decoupling strategy, in which the hidden-layer neuron parameters are preset and
the physical coordinates and the added auxiliary variable are processed by separate subnetworks.
Such design enables a unified global approximation of piecewise smooth solutions without explicit
domain decomposition, while preserving network expressivity and avoiding the redundancy inherent
in conventional lift approaches. The resulting formulation reduces to a linear least-squares problem,
thereby eliminating the need of nonlinear training and ensuring the high computational efficiency
and accuracy. Extensive numerical experiments on benchmark interface problems in two and three
dimensions demonstrate that, compared with existing neural network-based and traditional numer-
ical methods, our proposed LD-GTransNet consistently delivers superior performance, particularly
for problems with high-contrast coefficients and complex interface geometries.

Interface problems, generalized TransNets, dimension lift, dimension decoupling,

1. Introduction
Interface problems arise in a wide range of scientific and engineering applications involving
heterogeneous media and phase transitions. Typical examples include heat conduction in composite
materials, multiphase flows with surface tension effects, electrodiffusion across biological membranes,
and electromagnetic wave propagation in discontinuous media [12, 16, 23]. These problems are
characterized by domains partitioned into subregions by lower-dimensional interfaces, across which
the solution and/or its flux exhibit discontinuities governed by prescribed jump conditions. Such
discontinuities often induce low regularity or even singular behavior in the solution, posing significant
challenges for numerical approximation.
Classical numerical methods for interface problems can be broadly categorized into body-fitted
and unfitted approaches. Body-fitted methods align the computational mesh with the interfaces,

allowing discontinuities to be explicitly resolved within the discretization framework. Representa-

While these approaches can achieve high-order accuracy when the mesh
conforms well to the interface geometry, generating high-quality fitted meshes remains a major
bottleneck, especially for problems with complex geometries, moving interfaces, or multiscale fea-
tures. To alleviate the dependence on mesh generation, unfitted methods have been extensively
developed. These methods employ interface-independent meshes and incorporate jump conditions
through modified discretizations, enriched approximation spaces, or penalty formulations. Notable
examples include immersed interface methods [26], immersed finite element methods [28, 19, 20],
and unfitted discontinuous Galerkin and finite volume methods [8, 13, 32, 43, 2]. Despite their flexi-
bility, unfitted methods often require sophisticated treatments near cut cells and accurate geometric
reconstruction, which can become highly challenging for interfaces with large curvature or intricate
topology, potentially leading to reduced robustness and accuracy.
The fundamental limitation of both fitted and unfitted approaches lies in their reliance on mesh-
based representations of geometry. This limitation has motivated the development of meshfree meth-
ods, which approximate solutions directly on scattered nodes without explicit mesh construction.
Among them, kernel-based methods [24] and generalized finite difference schemes [14, 30, 31] have
shown promising performance. More recently, neural network-based approaches [45] have emerged
as a powerful alternative due to their strong approximation capability and inherent flexibility in
handling high-dimensional and irregular problems.
Neural network methods for PDEs can be broadly divided into two categories according to their
training paradigms. The first category consists of fully parameterized networks, such as the Deep
Galerkin Method [38], Deep Ritz Method [47], and Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) [34],
where all network parameters are optimized via gradient-based algorithms. Although these methods
are highly expressive, they often suffer from expensive training, sensitivity to hyperparameters, and
difficulties in optimization due to non-convexity. Various strategies, including domain decomposition
[46, 36, 51], weak formulations [45], and input augmentation and embedding [25, 21, 44], have been
proposed to address interface problems within this framework. However, these approaches typically
introduce additional complexity in network design or training. The second category, referred to
as fixed-neuron methods, offers a computationally efficient alternative. In these approaches, the
parameters of hidden layers are preset and fixed, while only the output weights are determined
through the least-squares optimization.
Representative methods include the Extreme Learning
Machine (ELM) [5, 15, 33, 48], random feature methods (RFM) [6, 11, 41], and transferable neural
networks (TransNet) [29, 37, 50, 40, 10]. By avoiding or reducing nonlinear training, these methods
significantly improve efficiency and robustness. Nevertheless, their direct application to interface
problems still remains challenging due to two key issues: (i) the lack of sufficiently expressive
basis functions for capturing sharp discontinuities, and (ii) the difficulty of incorporating interface
conditions without reintroducing geometric complexity.
Existing neural network approaches for interface problems can be broadly categorized into two
types. Domain decomposition methods partition the computational domain along physical inter-
faces and assign separate networks to each subdomain, enforcing jump conditions via additional
loss terms [11, 29, 46]. While conceptually straightforward, these methods suffer from increasing
architectural complexity and computational cost as the number of subdomains grows, and they
require careful balancing of multiple loss components. Dimension lift methods offer an alternative
route by introducing an auxiliary variable to lift a low-dimensional piecewise smooth solution into a
2


higher-dimensional function space, thereby transforming the problem into one with global smooth-
ness. Specifically, an auxiliary variable ξ = ξ(x) acts as a regional indicator, assigning distinct
constant values to each subdomain. A globally smooth function F(x, ξ) is then constructed such
that, by fixing ξ to a subdomain label, the physical solution is recovered [21, 22, 39, 22]. This
approach avoids explicit domain decomposition and enables a single global network to approximate
the solution. However, directly concatenating the physical coordinates and the auxiliary variable as
inputs to a single network leads to an unnecessary increase in input dimensionality. Moreover, dif-
ferent (x, ξ) combinations may correspond to the same physical state, giving rise to representation
non-uniqueness and optimization difficulties.
In this paper, we propose a lift-and-decoupling generalized transferable neural network (LD-
GTransNet) to overcome the above limitations.
Our approach builds upon the GTransNet [9],
a multi-layer extension of TransNet that generates structured and expressive neural basis func-
tions through a theoretically guided. To effectively handle discontinuities, we introduce a lifting
framework with a key modification: unlike existing approaches that directly lift all variables into
a unified high-dimensional space, we decouple the physical coordinates and the auxiliary variable
into two parallel subnetworks. Such design preserves the core advantages of lifting while remov-
ing unnecessary increases in model complexity and mitigating the issues caused by representation
non-uniqueness. We particularly emphasize that, compared with existing dimension lift methods
such as DCSNN [21] and DC-RFM [39], the proposed LD-GTransNet exhibits an essential dis-
tinction: existing methods typically concatenate auxiliary variables with spatial inputs and embed
them into a unified high-dimensional space, thereby producing a large number of redundant basis
functions that are never activated simultaneously; by contrast, our decoupled architecture processes
spatial coordinates and auxiliary labels through parallel subnetworks, and the Hadamard product
of their outputs selectively activates spatial basis functions according to the subdomain label. This
mechanism effectively eliminates representational redundancy while maintaining full approximation
capability across all subdomains. Furthermore, when the original problem contains no interface (i.e.,
the solution is globally smooth), the auxiliary variable degenerates to a constant, and our method
naturally reduces to the standard GTransNet, exhibiting favorable backward compatibility.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the GTransNet architecture
and its use for solving PDEs. Section 3 presents in detail the proposed LD-GTransNet method for
elliptic and moving interface problems. Section 4 provides extensive numerical experiments and
comparisons to demonstrate superior accuracy and efficiency of our LD-GTransNet. Finally, some
conclusions and future work are discussed in Section 5.

3. Lift-and-Decoupling GTransNets for Elliptic and Moving Interface Problems
The GTransNet framework introduced in Section 2 provides an efficient architecture for solving
various partial differential equations with globally smooth solutions, but cannot directly capture
solution discontinuities across interfaces. To address this limitation, we propose a highly accurate
and efficient lift-and-decoupling GTransNet, which lifts the piecewise smooth solution into a higher-
dimensional space by introducing an auxiliary variable and then adopt a decoupling strategy in
the representation of the solution and the construction of the whole network architecture. Section
3.1 presents a detailed description of the LD-GTransNet for solving elliptic interface problems, and
Section 3.2 extends it to moving interface problems.

a piecewise smooth function u(x) into a high-dimensional space via a newly introduced auxiliary
variable ξ, while decoupling the spatial coordinates x from this auxiliary variable ξ so that they
are processed in separate subnetworks. The whole architecture consists of two parallel subnetworks,
namely the spatial subnetwork and the auxiliary subnetwork.
The former is a GTransNet that
processes spatial coordinates x and constitutes the spatial basis functions {ψi(x)}N
i=1, while the

remains fixed during spatial differentiation.

5. Conclusion
We proposed a novel lift-and-decoupling generalized transferable neural network for solving
PDEs with interface discontinuities. By decoupling physical and auxiliary variables, it provides a
unified global representation of piecewise smooth solutions without domain decomposition, avoiding
the redundancy issue. The framework reduces to a linear least-squares problem with fixed hidden-
layer parameters, eliminating nonlinear optimization and enabling efficient, robust, and easy to
implement solvers.
It is broadly applicable to elliptic equations, Stokes flow, anisotropic diffu-
sion–reaction systems, elasticity problems, and moving interfaces. Numerical experiments in two
and three dimensions show that LD-GTransNet achieves high-order accuracy, while using signifi-
cantly smaller linear systems than existing methods. Its advantages are especially pronounced for
problems with high-contrast coefficients or complex, evolving interfaces. Future work will focus on
theoretical analysis, extensions to high-dimensional and nonlinear systems, and integration with
adaptive sampling or multilevel techniques to enhance scalability and efficiency.
