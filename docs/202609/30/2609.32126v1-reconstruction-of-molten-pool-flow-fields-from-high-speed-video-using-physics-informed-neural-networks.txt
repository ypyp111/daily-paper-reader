Abstract:  Molten pool flow significantly influences bead geometry, defect formation, and solidification 

microstructure in wire -arc directed energy deposition (DED). However, direct flow sensing remains an open 10 

challenge due to extreme temperatures, intense arc illumination, and complex flow patterns. Traditional research 

has relied either on computationally intensive fluid dynamics simulations, which require assumed boundary 

conditions and lack empirical anchori ng, or on vision -based tracer particle tracking, which yields sparse, noisy, 

and physically unconstrained velocity estimates. This work bridges these two paradigms by reconstructing 

physics -constrained  2D surface flow fields from high -speed imaging of a GTAW -based wire -arc DED process 

under distinct flow regimes: quasi -steady convection and periodic droplet impact. The pipeline begins with 

homography correction to calibrate the oblique camera view, follo wed by  dense optical flow extraction to provide 

raw velocity observations. For quasi -steady pools, a physics -informed neural network (PINN) reconstructs the 

surface velocity field by jointly enforcing data fidelity,  incompressible Navier –Stokes momentum equations , no -

penetration boundary conditions, and an arc -forcing prior through composite loss minimization. For pools subject 20 

to periodic droplet impact, a phase -conditioned time -dependent PINN is further developed to resolve the cyclic 

flow evolution throughout the impact period. This work provides a unified framework that bridges high -speed 

visual sensing and fluid mechanics, e nabling physics -grounded flow diagnostics for wire -arc DED processes.

# 1. Introduction 

Understanding the flow dynamics of the molten pool is of fundamental importance in wire -arc directed energy 

deposition (wire -arc DED) and fusion welding, as the convective transport of heat and mass within the pool 

directly governs process outcomes from so lidification microstructure to macroscopic bead geometry  [1] , [2] , [3] .

The molten pool flow is driven by a combination of forces including Marangoni convection arising from surface 30 

tension gradients, electromagnetic (Lorentz) force, arc pressure and shear stress, and buoyancy  [4] . These forces 

are tightly coupled through the temperature field, current path, and evolving free surface, giving rise to complex 

flow patterns that are difficult to characterize under the extreme thermal and radiative conditions of the arc 

environment  [5] . Over the past decades, two approaches have been  most  commonly  used  to study molten pool 

flow: multiphysics  numerical simulation, which resolves the governing conservation laws on a computational 

domain, and vision -based flow measurement, which extracts motion information directly from experimental 

observations. 

Multiphysics numerical simulation solves the coupled equations of fluid dynamics, heat transfer, and 

electromagnetism to predict the transient temperature, velocity, and pressure fields within the molten pool  [6] .40 

This approach enables quantitative access to internal flow structures and driving force contributions that are otherwise inaccessible to direct measurement, yielding substantial insights into bead formation, penetration depth, 

and defect generation  [7] . Recent three -dimensional transient models for wire -arc DED have revealed that 

Marangoni force dominates surface flow in plasma arc welding  [8] , that convective flow and arc pressure are the 

primary factors governing weld dimensions  [9] , that droplet impact in GMAW -based DED generates velocities 

up to 0.9 m s −1 [10] , and that Marangoni -driven transport redistributes material from the pool center to the 

periphery  [11] . Fully coupled magneto -hydrodynamic models have further resolved the complex free surface 

evolution during multi -step metal transfer cycles  [12] . While these simulations provide valuable physical insights, 

they are computationally expensive and rely on assumed boundary conditions such as prescribed heat flux 

distributions, arc pressure profiles, and thermophysical property models, leading to poten tial mismatch with 50 

experimental ground truth  [13] .

Vision -based sensing extracts velocity fields from high -speed imaging by tracking tracer particles, either 

deliberately introduced or naturally occurring, across consecutive frames using particle image velocimetry (PIV) 

or particle tracking velocimetry (PT V)  [14] . Studies have applied these techniques across various welding 

processes: dual -camera setups with tracer particles revealed backward flow velocities in plasma arc welding  [15] ,

X-ray transmission systems combined with zirconia surface tracers enabled comprehensive 3D visualization of 

weld pool convection in keyhole plasma arc welding for the first time  [16] , confocal laser scanning enabled 

correlation of surface flow traces with keyhole geometry  [17] , visual measurement systems quantified two -

dimensional surface flow in high -speed GMAW  [18] , and unmelted powder was exploited as in -situ tracers for 60 

PIV analysis in laser cladding  [19] . Particle tracking and two -color thermometry characterized molten pool flow 

and thermal fields in electromagnetic controlled welding, where externally applied magnetic fields were used to 

regulate bead shape and penetration  [20] , [21] , [22] . In TIG -based wire -arc DED, zirconia tracer particles 

combined with 2D motion analysis software were used to characterize melt pool convection under different wire 

positioning conditions, revealing distinct flow patterns ranging from dual -vortex circulati on to periodic droplet -

driven dynamics  [23] . However, these approaches face inherent limitations: severe arc glare obscures particles 

despite optical filtering, tracer distributions are sparse and non -uniform, and spatter and fumes cause intermittent 

occlusion, yielding fragmented velocity measurem ents with incomplete spatial coverage rather than continuous, 

globally resolved flow fields.

Despite the wide application of deep learning -based machine vision for molten pool monitoring tasks such as in -

situ melt pool flaw identification  [24] , online defect detection  [25] , and melt pool characteristic prediction  [26] ,

deep learning methods developed specifically for flow detection remain scarce. One notable study developed an 

attention -based segmentation network (MPivNet) to isolate tracer particles before applying classical PIV 

algorithms  [19] , demonstrating that deep learning primarily serves as a preprocessing step rather than direct 

velocity regression. For general fluid dynamics, optical flow networks such as the Recurrent All -Pairs Field 

Transforms (RAFT)  [27]  estimate dense motion fields by constructing correlation volumes between all pixel pairs 

and iteratively refining predictions through recurrent updates. However, the applicability of such methods to 

molten pool imaging remains largely unexplored, due to e xtreme brightness variations, rapidly deforming free 

surfaces, and the absence of domain -specific training data. 80 

The limitations of vision -based flow detection, such as sparse, noisy , and incomplete velocity measurements, 

motivate approaches that reconstruct  dense  flow fields from partial observations by leveraging underlying physical laws. Physics -informed neural networks (PINNs) integrate governing partial differential equations as 

constraints into the neural network loss function, enabling data -driven solutions that respect fundamental 

conservation principles  [28] . The foundational work demonstrated that neural networks can  solve both forward 

and inverse problems involving nonlinear PDEs by minimizing a composite loss comprising data mismatch and 

PDE residuals  [29] . Subsequent studies have applied PINNs to flow field reconstruction from limited 

measurements in various contexts, including extracting velocity and pressure from flow visualization images  [30] ,

reconstructing turbulent flows from sparse data  [31] , simulating thermal convection  [32] , and super -resolution 90 

reconstruction of cardiovascular flows from sparse Doppler measurements  [33] . These applications demonstrate 

PINNs’ capacity to recover  spatially resolved  flow dynamics from partial information when physics is properly 

encoded, though they predominantly operate on simulation data or controlled laboratory conditions with relatively 

clean observations. 

Physics -informed approaches have also been applied to molten pool modeling in welding and additive 

manufacturing, though primarily for morphology prediction and process monitoring rather than flow field 

reconstruction. Examples include physics -informed mac hine learning for molten pool morphology prediction  [34] ,

discovering dimensionless scaling laws for pool geometry through symbolic regression  [35] , and employing 

generative models to synthesize realistic pool oscillation dynamics for process control  [36] , [37] . These studies 100 

focus on thermal fields, pool boundaries, and process stability, but none has addressed velocity field reconstruction. 

Critically, molten pool flow is governed by unique physics, including steep temperature gradients driving 

Marangoni convection, violent m omentum injection from droplet impact, electromagnetic forces from high 

welding currents, and rapidly evolving free surfaces, that distinguish it from the conventional fluid systems studied 

in existing PINN literature. Moreover, reconstructing flow from sp arse, noisy, real -world molten pool observations 

corrupted by arc glare and occlusion remains fundamentally unaddressed. 

In this work, we propose a physics -informed neural network framework that reconstructs  spatially dense  2D 

surface flow fields from high -speed molten pool videos in GTAW -based wire -arc DED, overcoming the noise, 

occlusion, and incompleteness inherent in raw visual observations. The approach bridges vision -based sensing 110 

and computational fluid mechanics, enabling physics -grounded flow diagnostics under distinct flow regimes. The 

main contributions are: 

1.  A dense optical flow pipeline employing homography  correction and  optical flow  estimator  to extract 

pixel -wise velocity observations directly from noisy, high -dynamic -range molten pool image sequences, 

realizing spatially continuous flow estimation. 

2.  A PINN -based reconstruction framework enforcing the incompressible Navier –Stokes equations, no -

penetration boundary conditions, and an arc -forcing regularization prior to recover dense velocity fields 

from incomplete optical flow observations,  with local agreement assessed against the available PTV 

trajectorie s. 

3.  Extension to a phase -conditioned time -dependent PINN that resolves cyclic flow evolution during 120 

periodic droplet impact, demonstrating applicability to both quasi -steady and highly dynamic molten 

pool conditions. 

The key novelty lies in using PINN -based physical reconstruction to overcome the limitations of traditional vision -

only flow measurement. Instead of reporting sparse tracer velocities or raw optical -flow estimates, the proposed 

framework recovers  dense, physics -constrained  molten pool flow fields from sparse observations. This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides an overview of the methodology. Section 3 describes the 

experimental system and conditions. Section 4 presents the preprocessing pipeline including optical flow 

extraction and geometric correction. Sec tion 5 details the PINN framework and reconstruction results for quasi -

steady pools. Section 6 extends the approach to dynamic pools with periodic droplet transfer  and discusses the 130 

correlation between the reconstructed flow fields and post -weld bead measurements . Section 7  concludes and 

discusses future work. 

# 2. Method Overview 

Fig. 1 illustrates the overall methodology, which reconstructs dense, physics -consistent velocity fields from raw 

sensory observations and physical constraints. 

Figure 1: Methodology illustration. 

The methodology comprises three stages. In the first stage, raw high -speed images are processed to extract optical 

flow and boundary information.  A dense optical flow method  estimates dense frame -to -frame motion fields, 

providing spatially continuous velocity observations rather than discrete particle trajectories. The melt pool 140 

boundary is simultaneously extracted through image segmentation. Geometric correction is then applied to 

compensate for the oblique camera viewing angle, yielding calibrated input data for reconstruction. 

In the second stage, a physics -informed neural network reconstructs the flow field by learning a stream function 

and pressure distribution that jointly satisfy observational data and physical laws. The network is trained using 

composite losses encoding: (i ) governing equations such as the Navier –Stokes  momentum  equations; (ii) no -

penetration boundary conditions at the pool periphery; (iii)  an arc -forcing prior  and energy dissipation; and (iv) 

data fidelity terms penalizing deviation from optical flow observations.  These losses constrain momentum 

transport only, without an energy equation or a reconstructed temperature field.  This multi -source loss formulation 

enables robust reconstruction even when optical flow estimates are noisy or incomplete. 150 

In the final stage, the dense surface velocity field is reconstructed, together with an auxiliary pressure field and  an 

equivalent closure depth , both of which emerge as closure quantities from the depth -averaged momentum balance. Overall, this work demonstrates how  physics -constrained surface -flow fields  can be recovered from noisy sensory 

data through the integration of visual observations and governing equations.

When the wire contacts the melt pool surface (0 mm case), no distinct droplet forms; instead, the wire melts 

continuously and dilutes smoothly into the melt pool with only slight surface vibration. In contrast, when the wire 

is positioned 2 mm or 4 mm abov e the substrate, droplets periodically form and detach from the wire tip, causing 

periodic impacts on the melt pool. Upon impacting the melt pool surface, the droplet displaces the surrounding 

liquid metal, compresses the pool locally, and induces transien t oscillations. 

Figure 4: Experiments. (a) Wire location settings; (b) Convection patterns; (c) High -speed imaging. 

The melt pool geometry and convection patterns also differ distinctively between configurations. In the 0 mm 

case, the melt pool exhibits an irregular, teardrop -shaped profile due to the modified heat distribution. It is wider 200 

at the front near the wire and significantly narrower at the tail. Two distinct convection cells emerge on either side 

of the pool: tracer particles move outward from the wire location at the pool front, travel rearward along the pool 

edges, then return to ward the center and circulate at the widest region of the pool. This dual -vortex pattern arises 

because the bypass current directs heat preferentially into the wire, creating hot spots at the left and right sides of 

the pool that drive Marangoni -induced ci rculation  [23] . In contrast, when the wire is positioned 2 mm or 4 mm 

above the surface, a regular, elliptical pool shape develops with a simpler flow pattern: particles travel 

monotonically from the pool front toward the tail along the pool periphery. Periodic droplet  impacts momentarily 

disrupt this steady flow, causing brief chaotic motion before the pattern re -establishes. 

These high -speed observations reveal the complex, transient nature of molten pool flow in wire -arc DED. The 210 

key challenge is to overcome intense arc radiation, pool surface oscillations, and limited tracer particle visibility. In the prior work  [23] , 2D motion analysis software was used to compute particle velocity, which relies heavily 

on manual operation. A related work  [19]  developed an attention -based segmentation model to detect particles 

and derive velocity fields, but this approach lacks robustness to particle ambiguity and yields only sparse 

measurements. In this work, these challenges are addressed through optical flow  extraction and PINN -based 

reconstruction. 

# 4. Data Preprocessing 

The preprocessing pipeline converts raw high -speed images into calibrated boundary and velocity data for PINN 

reconstruction. Because the PINN operates in a physical coordinate system, the oblique camera view must first be 

rectified into a metric top -view plane. Surface velocity measurements are then obtained in the same coordinate 220 

system to provide observational constraints for the reconstruction. Section 4.1 describes the calibration procedure, 

and Section 4.2 describes the velocity measurements from particle tracking velocimetry (PTV) and dense optical 

flow. 

# 4.1.  Camera  Calibration 

Quantitative flow reconstruction requires a physical coordinate system, but conventional calibration using 

checkerboard targets or fiducial markers is impractical for WAAM. The field of view is occupied by a curved, 

reflective molten pool under intense arc  radiation, and no calibration target can be placed on the liquid surface. 

The calibration therefore uses geometric features visible in the process images, together with an independent post -

weld bead width measurement.  As illustrated in Fig. 5 for the h = 0 mm case, the pool boundary and reference 

features identified in the raw oblique image (Fig. 5a) and the post -weld bead width measured from the cross -230 

section (Fig. 5b) are combined to rectify the oblique image into the metric top -view coordinate system of Fig. 5c. 

Fig ure 5:  Camera calibration for the  ℎ = 0 mm case. 

The pool boundary is first extracted in every frame because it defines the spatial domain and the no -penetration 

constraint for the PINN. A pretrained video segmentation model is used to obtain the initial boundary, followed 

by manual correction of residua l errors. The SAM2 model [40] performs prompt -based video segmentation and 

propagates masks through the image sequence, improving temporal consistency and reducing frame -by -frame 

annotation effort. Manual inspection corrects regions affected by arc illumin ation, droplet transfer, or weak 

contrast. The corrected mask edge is used as the pool boundary. Although this step still involves manual 

intervention, deep learning -based segmentation networks [41] could automate it in future applications. 240 

Visual landmarks are then used to establish a geometric reference. The tail point  P1 is placed at the rear extremity 

of the visible pool boundary near the bead reinforcement, and  head  point  P2 is placed at the foremost point of the 

leading edge along the  centerline . These two points  initialize  the pool length direction. The arc impingement point P3, identified from the electrode tip location and visible arc glow, is used to define the coordinate origin after 

rectification. This point also provides the initial estimate of the arc -center position  xarc  used in the reconstruction 

(Section 5.2). 

The camera is fixed at approximately 45° inclination and views the pool roughly perpendicular to the welding 

direction, as shown in Fig.  2. Under this geometry the dominant distortion is transverse foreshortening: the far 250 

side of the pool appears compressed relative to the near side, producing a keystone effect in the width direction, 

while the length axis is mainly affected by a known cosine factor. A single oblique view, however, does not provide 

enough geometric informat ion to resolve this transverse foreshortening unambiguously. An additional geometric 

constraint is therefore required, and the natural choice is the approximate bilateral symmetry of the molten pool 

about its  centerline . This symmetry is physically reasonable because the time -averaged arc force and thermal field 

are approximately axisymmetric about the electrode axis. It is also supported by the post -process metallographic 

cross -sections and the observed bilateral surfac e-flow circulation.

Two complementary techniques are used to measure surface velocity: particle tracking velocimetry and dense 

optical flow. PTV tracks zirconia tracer particles and other visible features dispersed on the pool surface and, after 

manual verification, provides  sparse but reliable point velocities. Dense optical flow estimates a per -pixel velocity 

field from the full image texture, yielding the spatially continuous observations that the PINN requires. Because 290 

the molten pool surface is specularly  reflective, the tracer distribution is sparse and non -uniform, and the arc 

saturates the sensor near the impingement zone, neither technique alone is sufficient: PTV is too sparse to serve 

as PINN input, while optical flow must be validated before it can  be trusted on this out -of -domain imagery.  PTV 

therefore serves as the manually verified reference for selecting the optical -flow method and as a training -

independent local comparison for the final PINN reconstruction. 

PTV is performed using an automatic feature tracker [15] that identifies invariant features on the pool surface and 

establishes correspondence across consecutive frames. Tracker failures caused by unclear imagery are then 

manually corrected based on empiri cal visual judgement. Velocities are then computed from the temporal 

differences of displacements.  Fig. 6 shows the resulting trajectories overlaid on the raw high -speed images. 300 

Because the bright arc -impingement zone saturates the sensor, it yields few trackable features. The usable tracks 

therefore concentrate in the rear half of the pool, where the tracer particles remain visible.  In the h = 0 mm case 

(Fig. 6a), 21 tracked particles map the rear recirculation. In the h = 2 and 4 mm cases (Fig. 6b and c), 6 and 10 

particles respectively follow a front -to -rear decay after droplet impact, consistent with the flow dynamics in 

Section 3. 2.

# 5. Physics -Informed Flow Reconstruction for Quasi -Steady Molten Pools 340 

This section develops the reconstruction for the quasi -steady regime, using the  h = 0 mm wire height case in which 

the absence of droplet transfer produces a molten pool whose flow can be treated as time -invariant. The goal is to 

recover a  spatially dense, physics -constrained  surface velocity field from calibrated optical -flow observations that 

are noisy and locally incomplete near the arc. The depth -averaged governing equations and the network 

architecture are introduced first, followed by the loss formulation, and then the re construction results together 

with the independent particle -tracking validation and an assessment of the arc -force prior under data occlusion. 

# 5.1.  Governing Equations and Network Architecture 

Fig. 8 illustrates the PINN framework used for both the quasi -steady and time -dependent cases. For the quasi -

steady case, the network takes spatial coordinates as input and outputs a stream function and a pressure field. 

Velocities and governing -equation r esiduals are then obtained through automatic differentiation and used to form 350 

the data, momentum, and boundary losses. For the time -dependent cases ( ℎ = 2 and  4 mm), the same framework 

is extended with normalized time as an additional input and an energy -decay loss, as indicated by the blue elements

High -speed imaging provides only surface velocity measurements, whereas the molten pool flow is inherently 

three -dimensional. The present model therefore does not attempt to recover the full three -dimensional velocity 

field from surface observations alone.  Instead, a depth -averaged formulation is adopted to represent the dominant 

in -plane circulation while accounting for the unresolved vertical structure through a reduced physical model.  In 360 

this formulation,  𝑢  and  𝑣  denote the depth -averaged in -plane velocity components predicted by the network, 

while  𝑢 𝑠  and  𝑣 𝑠  denote the corresponding surface velocities measured by optical flow or PTV . The connection 

between them is obtained by assuming a half -Poiseuille profile for the variation of the in -plane velocity through 

the liquid layer. The profile satisfies no slip at the fusion boundary and zero shear at the free surface, so the 

velocity rise s from zero at the bottom to its maximum at the surface. The surface velocity is therefore the top value 

of this profile, while the depth -averaged velocity is its average over the layer thickness; for a half -Poiseuille profile, 

the top value is 3/2 times t he depth -averaged value [44] :While the actual molten pool is more complex, the half -Poiseuille profile serves as a physically interpretable first -

order link between the measured surface motion and the reconstructed depth -averaged flow. Thus, the network 370 

predicts the depth -averaged velocities, which are converted to surface velocities via this relation when compared 

with observations.

# 7. Conclusions and Future Work 

This work presented a physics -informed neural network framework for reconstructing molten pool surface flow 710 

fields from high -speed video in wire -arc directed energy deposition. By combining dense optical flow sensing 

with Navier –Stokes -constrained reconstruction,  the method reconstructs spatially dense surface -velocity fields 

from sparse, noisy visual observations. The main contributions and findings are summarized as follows. 

1.  A PINN -based reconstruction framework was developed that transforms sparse, partial optical flow 

observations into dense,  physics -constrained  flow fields. From limited surface observations, the 

framework  produces spatially dense velocity fields and  an auxiliary pressure field  across the entire pool, 

along with  an effective arc -forcing prior  and  an equivalent closure depth  that emerges from  the depth -

averaged momentum balance .

2.  The reconstructed flow and  auxiliary pressure fields provide a physically plausible interpretation of  the 

molten pool convection pattern during wire -arc DED.  The inferred pressure gradient  from the pool tail 720 

to the arc center  is consistent with fluid moving inward  along the centerline;  the arc -forcing prior 

represents the radial outward redirection of this flow , establishing the return  flow along the pool boundary 

and the dual -vortex recirculation observed on the pool surface. The surface convection remains organized 

and laminar across all conditions, with the vertical momentum exchange  represented by  the depth -drag 

term and summarized as  an equivalent closure depth .

3.  A phase -conditioned extension resolves the temporal evolution of the flow field after periodic droplet 

impact. The time -resolved reconstruction captures the continuous decay of droplet -induced perturbations, 

providing a continuous  representation of transient momentum redistribution .

4.  Comparison with post -weld bead cross -sections shows qualitative consistency between the reconstructed 

2D surface flow fields and the observed differences in fusion zone geometry. The dual -vortex 730 

recirculation in the quasi -steady case provides a plausible flow -based interpretation of the two -lobed 

fusion zone, while the periodic transient mixing in the dynamic case is consistent with the regular bead 

profile. 

Overall, this work bridges vision -based process monitoring and computational fluid mechanics, enabling 

quantitative molten pool flow analysis directly from high -speed imaging without intrusive sensors or expensive 

simulations. 

The present study has several limitations. The current formulation does not solve the energy equation, so thermal 

effects such as Marangoni convection are not explicitly represented, and the depth -averaged momentum balance 

absorbs vertical shear into a lea rnable drag parameter. It therefore does not resolve the full three -dimensional flow 740 

structure, and the inferred pressure field, arc -forcing parameters, and equivalent closure depth should be 

interpreted as model -dependent closure quantities rather than direct measurements. Accordingly, the relationship 

between the reconstructed circulatio n and fusion -zone morphology is treated as a physically plausible interpretation rather than a demonstrated causal mechanism. 

Future work will extend the present surface reconstruction toward a thermally coupled, three -dimensional model. 

Introducing an energy equation, constrained by independent thermal measurements, would recover the 

temperature field and represent temperature -dependent surface -tension forces explicitly. Extending the 

reconstruction to three dimensions would resolve the vertical flow structure directly, eliminating the need for the 

depth -averaged drag closure.  High -fidelity multiphysics  simulations could also provide complementary validation 750 

and access to internal fields unavailable from surface imaging.  Together, these extensions would capture 

Marangoni and vertical momentum exchange explicitly, strengthening the link between process parameters, 

molten -pool convection, and weld microstructure.

flow velocity, and  vPTV  the independent particle -tracking velocity. Bold symbols denote vectors and |·| denotes the 

Euclidean norm. The operator  ∇ acts in the in -plane coordinates, and  R is the residual of the depth -averaged 

momentum equation introduced in Section 5.1. 

The velocity error  EOF  is the normalized root -mean -square error between the predicted velocity and the held -out 

optical -flow observations, 

# 780 

Normalization by the observed velocity magnitude makes  EOF  dimensionless and comparable across cases. It is evaluated on optical -flow points withheld from training, so a low value indicates that the physics -constrained fit 

generalizes to unseen observations rather than memorizing the training points. Lower is better. 

The direction metric  DPTV  is the mean cosine similarity between the predicted and the measured PTV velocity 

directions, 

Each term is the cosine of the angle between the reconstructed and measured velocity vectors, so  DPTV  approaches 

unity when the reconstructed flow directions match the independent PTV tracks and falls toward zero when they 

are uncorrelated. 790 

The speed -magnitude metric  MPTV  is the mean ratio of predicted to measured speed, 

A value near unity indicates that the reconstructed speed level matches the PTV measurement, whereas values 

below or above unity indicate systematic under - or over -estimation of the flow speed. 

The PDE residual  EPDE  is the mean squared residual of the governing momentum equation, evaluated at 

independent collocation points distinct from the data points, 

It measures how closely the reconstructed field satisfies the depth -averaged momentum balance away from the 

observation points, and therefore quantifies physical consistency rather than data agreement. Lower is better. In 800 

summary,  EOF  and  EPDE  are error measures for which lower values are better, while  DPTV  and  MPTV  are agreement 

measures that approach unity for a perfect match. 

# Appendix B. Arc -force model variants 

The occlusion study in Section 5.3.3 compares three arc -force parameterizations against a no -arc baseline. All 

three models share the same learnable amplitude  A, characteristic width  σ, and arc -center location  xarc . They differ 

only in the radial shape of the force envelope. Let the in -plane radius from the arc center and the outward unit 

vector be 

where  ε is a small constant included only to avoid division by zero at the arc center. Positivity of  σ and  A is 

enforced through a log -parameterization during training. 810 

Model A has a gradient -of -Gaussian radial shape, in which the force rises from zero at the center, peaks near radius 

σ,  and decays outward  [6 ]: 

This produces a radially outward force whose magnitude vanishes at the arc center, peaks at a radial distance of 

order  σ, and decays away from the arc -impingement region. It is the production model whose results are reported 

in the main text. 

Model B is a ring -peaked variant that concentrates the force on an annulus rather than at the center. The standard 

Gaussian model assumes the force maximum  lies on the arc axis, but the true arc -pressure distribution can shift 820 

its peak off -axis under electrode -geometry or electromagnetic effects, producing a ring -shaped footprint. Model 

B tests this possibility by replacing the radial envelope with an annular profile, The factor  r/σ suppresses the force near the center and shifts the maximum outward to a radius of order  σ.

Comparing Model B with Model A therefore probes whether the reconstruction is sensitive to the exact location 

of the radial force maximum. 

Model C is a plasma -drag profile representing the surface shear exerted by the impinging plasma jet, consistent 

with the plasma -flow description of arc –pool interaction [6]. It keeps the same center, width, and amplitude but 

applies a monotonically decayin g radial envelope without an internal node, 830 

Unlike Model A, the magnitude is largest at the center and decreases monotonically outward, giving a smoother 

arc -side forcing pattern. Comparing Model C with Model A tests whether replacing the gradient -of -Gaussian 

shape with a single -lobed drag profile c hanges the reconstruction. 

The no -arc baseline sets  Farc  = 0 while keeping all other loss terms unchanged. The purpose of the comparison is 

not to identify the exact arc -pressure distribution, but to test whether including a physically structured arc -side 

prior, in any of these forms, improves the reconstructio n when observations near the arc are removed.
