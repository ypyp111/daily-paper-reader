## Abstract 

Standardized benchmarks are fundamental to reliable progress in AI for EDA, including learning-based thermal modeling. How-ever, existing thermal prediction studies often rely on different datasets, simulators, data splits, preprocessing pipelines, and met-rics, while most datasets and implementations remain unavailable, making fair and reproducible comparison difficult. We introduce IC-ThermBench, an open and progressive benchmark that combines established 3D-IC steady-state, transient, and industrial package tasks with a new 50,000-sample 2.5D chiplet extension designed to evaluate progressively broader represented physical variation and cross-package OOD transfer. Five Generalization Scopes cover 3D-IC fixed-design prediction, Within-Family Generalization un-der layout, material, and boundary-condition variation, and Cross-Package OOD transfer to unseen package systems. We evaluate eight representative baselines under common data, splits, labels, and metrics. Performance degrades gradually from S2 to S4 as represented physical support broadens, but Cross-Package OOD produces a much sharper degradation: the best RMSE and MAE increase from 1.216 and 0.938 K at S4 to 15.99 and 15.00 K at S5, respectively. With only 10 labeled samples per OOD case, target-domain adaptation reduces the best MAE to 2.60 K. IC-ThermBench further provides a unified generation, training, in-ference, and evaluation pipeline, enabling reproducible and fair comparison of existing and new thermal predictor. Project site: https://github.com/Day333/ThermalBench.

## 1 Introduction 

Thermal analysis is critical to 2.5D/3D-IC design, where chiplet placement, power density, material properties, and cooling con-ditions jointly determine temperature distributions and hotspots. Repeated high-fidelity simulation during placement, routing, and design-space exploration can be expensive, motivating learning-based thermal surrogates. Learning-based thermal predictors have recently attracted increasing attention, achieving high predictive accuracy while enabling fast inference for repeated thermal analy-sis [9, 16, 31, 32].

Prediction accuracy alone, however, does not make models di-rectly comparable. Benchmarks such as ImageNet, SWE-bench, and PDEBench, together with CircuitNet in EDA, demonstrate the importance of shared data, task definitions, splits, metrics, and eval-uation procedures [1, 5, 10, 24]. This is particularly important for thermal learning, where differences in datasets, simulators, pre-processing, and test distributions can substantially affect reported errors. Results obtained under different evaluation settings there-fore cannot be interpreted as model-to-model improvements by default. Existing thermal-learning studies have expanded beyond power-only variation to more challenging geometry, material, and cooling conditions [18, 33, 36, 37]. However, their datasets, representations, splits, and evaluation protocols remain highly heterogeneous, and complete data and code are often unavailable. Table 1 summarizes these differences; the Closest-Scope labels indicate conceptual sim-ilarity rather than protocol equivalence. The field therefore still lacks an open and unified framework for fair and reproducible comparison across thermal predictors. To fill this gap, we introduce IC-ThermBench , an open and progressive benchmark for 2.5D/3D-IC thermal learning. It com-bines established 3D-IC steady-state, transient, and industrial pack-age tasks with a new 50,000-sample 2.5D chiplet extension that systematically broadens the represented physical variation. Five Generalization Scopes span increasingly challenging deployment settings: S1 preserves established 3D-IC fixed-design tasks; S2–S4 progressively introduce layout, material, and boundary-condition variation within represented system families; and S5 evaluates case-disjoint transfer to previously unseen package systems. This orga-nization explicitly separates Within-Family Generalization from Cross-Package OOD transfer. The main contributions are threefold: 

• We establish an open thermal-learning benchmark that uni-fies established public tasks with a new 50,000-sample pro-gressive extension under a consistent data and evaluation framework. 

• We benchmark eight classic and state-of-the-art thermal predictors under a unified protocol. Results show moderate degradation with broader represented physical variation, a sharp drop under Cross-Package OOD, and substantial recovery with limited target-domain adaptation.

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Benchmarks in ML, Scientific ML, and EDA 

Modern benchmarks provide not only datasets but also shared task definitions, data splits, metrics, and evaluation protocols that enable reproducible and fair model comparison. ImageNet, GLUE, SWE-bench, and PDEBench established this paradigm across vision, language, software agents, and scientific ML [5, 10, 24, 26]. Learning-based EDA has likewise benefited from public benchmarks such as CircuitNet, AMSNet, OpenABC-D, EDALearn, and ChiPBench, which provide reusable foundations for model development and evaluation [1, 4, 19, 25, 29]. Such standardization is particularly important for thermal learn-ing, where differences in simulation settings, physical conditions, data splits, input representations, and evaluation metrics can sub-stantially affect reported errors and make independently reported results difficult to compare directly. 

## 2.2 Learning-Based Thermal Simulation 

Learning-based thermal simulation has explored convolutional and image-to-image models [3, 6, 11, 15, 21, 23, 38], as well as neural operators, physics-informed learning, and pretrained PDE models [7, 9, 14, 16, 17, 20, 22, 27, 30, 34]. These approaches have achieved high prediction accuracy across a variety of fixed-design or represented-distribution settings. Recent studies further extend thermal prediction toward cross-configuration and cross-system generalization. Therunet-SP stud-ies geometry scaling, ThermGCN evaluates unseen chiplet lay-outs, FSA-Heat jointly varies geometry, power, material, and heat-exchange conditions, Therm-PCT targets unseen unstructured ge-ometries, and Adaptive Graph Learning considers process, mate-rial, and interface variation [2, 13, 33, 36, 37]. COOL further rep-resents geometry, power, material, and cooling conditions in a unified formulation and evaluates generalization with a design-disjoint split [18]. Alpha EV6-based tasks have also been adopted by multiple studies, including ARO, T-Fusion, SAU-FNO, and Therm-FM [7, 9, 27, 35]. Despite this progress, evaluation settings in thermal learning remain highly fragmented. Different studies often use different datasets, simulators, input representations, data splits, and metrics, while complete data and implementations are frequently unavail-able. As a result, existing results are difficult to reproduce and com-pare fairly, and evaluating new methods often requires rebuilding study-specific data and evaluation pipelines.

## 4 Benchmark Design and Protocol 4.1 Benchmark Scope and Progressive Design 

IC-ThermBench organizes thermal prediction into five deployment-oriented Generalization Scopes , rather than a universal difficulty ranking. S1 evaluates within-design prediction; S2–S4 progressively broaden the represented physical support over the same observed system families through layout, material, and boundary-condition variation; and S5 evaluates case-disjoint transfer to previously un-seen package systems. The distinction between S2–S4 and S5 is central to the bench-mark. S2–S4 are independently sampled and therefore represent progressively broader training distributions rather than paired one-factor interventions. S5 instead changes the underlying package families, which may jointly shift structural properties and the empir-ical distribution of model inputs. We therefore call S2–S4 Within-Family Generalization : test samples come from package/system families represented during training, while their physical condi-tions broaden. S5 is Cross-Package OOD : all test package systems are unseen during training. Figure 1 summarizes this organization. 

## 4.2 Five Generalization Scopes 

Figure 1 summarizes the systems, inputs, sample counts, and pri-mary variation of the five Scopes. 

S1: established source suite. S1 consolidates the established Therm-FM source suite [9], including Alpha EV6 steady-state/transient tasks and industrial package tasks. It preserves the source task definitions and fidelity settings while using the split fixed by the current benchmark release. It serves as the benchmark’s within-design reference. 

S2–S4: Within-Family Generalization. S2–S4 share the same ten ATPlace2.5D system families [28] while progressively broadening the represented physical variation. S2 varies chiplet placement and orientation; S3 additionally varies filler thermal conductivity; and S4 further varies ambient temperature and convective cooling. The three datasets are independently sampled and therefore characterize progressively broader represented distributions rather than sample-wise paired ablations. 

S5: Cross-Package OOD. S5 contains five held-out package sys-tems, Cases 11–15, that do not participate in S2–S4 training or validation. They primarily stress chiplet count, power density, size heterogeneity, power concentration, and package utilization. For example, Case 11 increases the chiplet count from a training maxi-mum of 61 to 80, while Case 14 concentrates 90.9% of total power in one chiplet compared with a training maximum of 69.2%. The remaining held-out cases primarily stress power density, size het-erogeneity, and utilization. The five held-out systems and their stress characteristics were fixed before baseline evaluation and are shared by every model and metric. Changing the package family can also shift the empirical ranges and joint distribution of directly provided model inputs. Accord-ingly, S5 is treated as Cross-Package OOD rather than a controlled single-factor structural intervention. 

## 4.3 Data Generation and Input Representation 

For S2–S5, feasible chiplet layouts are rasterized onto a 64 × 64 grid and simulated with the open-source HotSpot thermal simulator using its grid-based detailed-3D flow. All generated tasks are steady-state, with temperature fields stored in kelvin as prediction targets. S1 instead preserves the source task definitions and fidelity settings while using the split fixed by the benchmark release. The model input expands cumulatively with the Generalization Scope. ## 5 Experimental Evaluation 5.1 Experimental Setup and Baselines 

We evaluate eight configurations spanning three model families: U-Net as a convolutional predictor; FNO, U-FNO, SAU-FNO, and Deep-OHeat as neural-operator or operator-style models; and Therm-FM T/B/L as pretrained PDE foundation models. S1 follows the source task definitions and fidelity settings with the released benchmark split, whereas S2–S5 use the same IC-ThermBench datasets, splits, labels, input semantics, normalization, and metrics. Fairness is enforced at the data and evaluation interfaces rather than by forcing identical optimization across heterogeneous ar-chitectures. FNO, U-FNO, SAU-FNO, U-Net, and DeepOHeat use Adam with learning rate 10 −3, batch size 20, and 100 training epochs. Therm-FM uses learning rate 5 × 10 −5, batch size 40, and 100 epochs.

## 5.3 Cross-Package OOD and Adaptation (S5) 

S5 evaluates whether the within-family performance observed in S2–S4 transfers to previously unseen package systems. We directly evaluate frozen S4 checkpoints on Cases 11–15 without target-domain labels or updated normalization statistics. Unlike the grad-ual degradation from S2 to S4, cross-package transfer produces a sharp performance break. Figure 3(a) shows the zero-shot RMSE, MAE, and MaxAE, while Table 4 reports the complete condensed comparison: the best RMSE increases from 1.216 K at S4 to 15.99 K at S5, approximately a 13 .1× increase, while the best MAE rises from 0.938 to 15.00 K, approximately a 16 .0× increase. The model ranking also changes substantially. U-FNO and SAU-FNO are among the strongest models under S2–S4, yet degrade severely on S5, whereas Therm-FM T becomes the strongest zero-shot model despite not leading S4. Even the best S5 result remains far above the S4 within-family accuracy. The per-case RMSE profiles are also strongly case dependent: DeepOHeat ranges from 4.84 K on Case 11 to 41.80 K on Case 14, while U-Net ranges from 7.72 K on Case 15 to 34.93 K on Case 13. We next test whether this failure can be recovered with limited target-domain supervision. Using only 𝐾 = 10 labeled samples per OOD case, all eight models improve substantially, with MAE reductions of approximately 67–88%. The best MAE decreases to 2.60 K, and reaches 0.78 K at 𝐾 = 500; additional labels provide progressively smaller gains. Figure 3(b) additionally shows that the model minimizing global-field error can still retain a substantially larger worst-pixel error at 𝐾 = 10.

No single architec-ture dominates all dimensions, motivating their separate reporting within the benchmark.

Finding 1: Broader represented thermal variability remains learn-able. Across S2–S4, progressively broadening the represented vari-ation in layout, material, and boundary-condition increases pre-diction error gradually, without the abrupt failure observed under cross-package transfer. Thus, when the package families remain represented, broader physical support primarily increases learn-ing difficulty rather than producing a clear generalization break. 

Implication: generalization under represented physical variation should be evaluated across multiple support ranges rather than compressed into a single within-family score. 

Finding 2: Cross-package transfer is a distinct generalization chal-lenge. The sharp S4-to-S5 performance break, ranking reversal, and heterogeneous per-case failures show that strong within-family accuracy under represented physical variation does not reliably predict behavior on unseen package families. Implication: Cross-Package OOD should be evaluated as a separate benchmark track, with per-case results reported alongside aggregate scores. 

Finding 3: Model advantages are strongly Scope dependent. Dif-ferent model families exhibit different strengths across the source-suite reference, within-family, and Cross-Package OOD settings, and increasing model scale does not yield monotonic gains in either prediction accuracy or cross-package transfer. A model leading one Scope therefore cannot be assumed to provide universally stronger thermal modeling capability. Implication: thermal predictors are bet-ter compared through cross-Scope capability profiles than through a single aggregate leaderboard ranking. 

Finding 4: Target-domain adaptation is effective, but answers a different question. A small amount of target-domain supervision recovers a substantial fraction of the zero-shot accuracy loss, but an adapted model no longer measures frozen cross-package trans-fer. Implication: zero-shot cross-package generalization and target-domain sample efficiency should be treated as separate evaluation dimensions, with the target-label budget reported explicitly. 

## 8 Discussion and Limitations 

Benchmark scope. IC-ThermBench focuses on generalization in learning-based thermal prediction rather than attempting to cover every thermal-analysis setting. The controlled S2–S5 track cur-rently focuses on steady-state 2.5D chiplet systems generated with HotSpot, while S1 provides a source-suite reference covering estab-lished fixed-design steady-state, transient, and industrial package tasks. The current benchmark should therefore not be interpreted as a comprehensive characterization of all industrial 2.5D/3D pack-age systems. Future extensions can incorporate broader multi-tier 3D structures, additional cooling technologies, transient generated tasks, and higher-fidelity or measured data. 

Data coverage density. The S2–S4 datasets maintain comparable sample counts while progressively expanding the physical sup-port that the models must cover. Their increasing errors therefore characterize performance under broader represented distributions rather than the isolated effect of any single added variable. More-over, as the number and range of varying physical factors increase, the effective sampling density over the represented space decreases, which may also contribute to the observed degradation. Larger or more targeted datasets could help disentangle limitations of model generalization from limitations of data coverage. 

Simulator-domain validity. The generated S2–S5 labels are pro-duced by HotSpot, so the benchmark measures prediction and gen-eralization within this simulation domain rather than silicon-level or signoff-level accuracy. Higher-fidelity simulation or measure-ment remains necessary for validating deployed thermal-design flows. 

## 9 Conclusion 

We presented IC-ThermBench, an open benchmark for evaluating generalizable 2.5D/3D-IC thermal learning under a common eval-uation contract. Five Generalization Scopes connect established 3D-IC fixed-design tasks with progressively broader layout, mate-rial, and boundary-condition variation, followed by Cross-Package OOD evaluation. Across eight representative baselines, observed physical variability remains largely learnable, whereas transfer to unseen package systems exposes a much larger generalization gap;

## E Complete Within-Family Generalization Results (S2–S4) 

The main paper retains four metrics per Scope to expose the central trend: RMSE and MAE for global field accuracy, together with worst-pixel MaxAE and Top-50 MAE. Tables 15–17 restore all six reported metrics for every baseline. The datasets are independently generated under a common case-balanced protocol; the progression should therefore be read as increasing represented-distribution difficulty, not as a paired causal ablation on the same samples. 

Reading S2. SAU-FNO leads five of the six metrics (U-FNO is marginally ahead on 𝑅 2), and U-FNO is close on the global field errors. The relative gap is larger for DeepOHeat and the pretrained Therm-FM variants, showing that fixed-design strength in S1 does not automatically transfer to represented placement diversity. U-Net’s Top-50 MAE is better than its global RMSE rank, which also illustrates why hotspot-region and field-wide errors should be re-ported together. 

Reading S3: Material. U-FNO overtakes SAU-FNO on every met-ric once conductivity becomes an explicit input field. The best RMSE rises by about 22% relative to S2, yet 𝑅 2 remains above 0.997, so represented material variation increases difficulty without caus-ing a generalization break. Model scale remains a poor predictor: Therm-FM L does not improve over Therm-FM B, and both trail the considerably smaller Fourier baselines. 

Metric-wise behavior across represented support. The six metrics expose a stable distinction between reconstructing the full field and tracking the thermally critical region. U-Net is never the best global predictor, yet its Top-50 MAE is disproportionately competitive in S2–S3; a deployment concerned mainly with ranking candidate hotspots could therefore reach a different model choice from one that requires an accurate temperature field everywhere. Conversely, DeepOHeat’s global and hotspot errors deteriorate together as input dimensionality grows, suggesting that its present flattened branch is a limiting factor under the 64 × 64 representation rather than a metric-specific failure. U-FNO and SAU-FNO remain comparatively balanced across all six criteria. 

Implication for progressive evaluation. No architecture should be described as generally superior from one Scope. SAU-FNO wins five of six S2 metrics and every S4 metric, U-FNO wins every S3 met-ric, while Therm-FM dominates the fixed-design S1 source suites reported later. This reversal is informative: the inductive bias that is effective when only power changes need not be the one that best absorbs represented placement, material, and boundary variation. The progression therefore functions as a capability profile rather than a single scalar leaderboard. The first Scope at which error rises sharply indicates where performance begins to degrade as broader physical variation is introduced.

## F.1 Target-Case Adaptation 

For each held-out case, adaptation uses the released fixed nested index sets for 𝐾 ∈ { 10 , 50 , 100 , 250 , 500 } samples from a 500-sample adaptation pool (identical for every model) and evaluates on the separate 500-sample holdout. Counts are per case, so 𝐾 = 10 uses 50 labels in total. The 𝐾 = 0 column below evaluates the frozen checkpoint on the same common 2,500-sample holdout; it is not a replacement for the official 5,000-sample zero-shot result in Ta-ble 19. 

Controlled adaptation comparison. Every run starts from its re-leased S4 checkpoint, retains the source normalizers, reinitializes only the optimizer, and trains for 50 epochs. The target holdout is never used to fit normalization or update weights. The non-Therm-FM models use their registered adaptation rate of 10 −4 and batch size 20; Therm-FM uses 5 × 10 −6 because the larger rate used by the operator baselines is unstable for its pretrained stack. These choices preserve architecture-appropriate optimization while holding the data, labels, split membership, metric code, and final-checkpoint rule fixed. 

Reading the label budget. 𝐾 is defined per held-out system rather than as one pooled count. Thus 𝐾 = 10 means 50 labels in total and guarantees that even the smallest budget observes every target case. This protocol tests low-cost target supervision, not zero-shot generalization, and the two results must not be conflated. A method that improves the frozen S5 score addresses cross-package transfer, whereas one that reaches a lower adapted score with the same 𝐾 

addresses target-domain sample efficiency. The complete curve is required because those rankings need not agree. The complete tables reinforce two points hidden by RMSE alone. First, the best model changes with both label budget and metric: Therm-FM T is strongest at low budget on peak-oriented errors, whereas SAU-FNO reaches the lowest large-budget errors on most metrics and Therm-FM B retains the best 𝐾 = 500 peak-temperature error. Second, the first 10 labels per case recover most of the global correlation, but worst-pixel errors remain several kelvin and im-prove more slowly. 

Optimization record. All adaptation tables use the final check-point after the same 50-epoch budget and evaluate every five epochs. The release stores the intermediate JSON records so that conver-gence and checkpoint-selection studies can be reconstructed with-out changing the official final-checkpoint comparison. The appen-dix freezes the complete final-checkpoint numbers for the eight registered baselines; intermediate traces remain machine-readable rather than being summarized by an additional, potentially redun-dant figure.

## H Complete S1 Source-Suite Results 

These tables preserve the source task definitions and fidelity set-tings rather than pooling tasks, resolutions, or solvers. The HS-QC values come from the supplied comparison table; the other records and the qualitative visualizations below are taken directly from Therm-FM [9]. The visualizations use the best-performing Therm-FM configuration reported for the corresponding S1 case; they are source illustrations rather than new reruns under the current IC-ThermBench split. Training time and GPU memory are source-reported and are not hardware-normalized. 

Why the source suites remain separate. S1 consolidates estab-lished public tasks rather than regenerating them under the S2–S5 protocol. The cases therefore retain their MTA labels, spatial reso-lutions, temporal lengths, and source metrics, while the released benchmark uses the fixed split described below. Regridding or pool-ing them would make the table look more uniform but would no longer reproduce the published tasks. This separation also pre-vents the sub-kelvin errors of fixed-design prediction from being averaged with the materially different cross-package question in S5. 

S1 dataset setting. The public HotSpot portion follows the Therm-FM setup closely. It uses Alpha EV6-derived single-core (HS-SC), quad-core (HS-QC), and octa-core (HS-OC) stacks with TIM and TSV structures, adiabatic lateral boundaries, and convective cool-ing at the top surface. Steady and transient tasks retain the source spatial resolutions and temporal lengths. Each case contains 5,000 samples; the IC-ThermBench release uses 4,000 for training and 1,000 for testing, whereas Therm-FM reports a 3,600/400/1,000 train-ing/validation/test split. This split change can lead to small nu-merical differences even though the physical setup is otherwise unchanged. All HotSpot methods use high-fidelity labels only. 

Therm-FM reproduction setting. Unless otherwise stated, we fol-low the source implementation: PyTorch 2.0.1 with CUDA 11.8 on an NVIDIA A100 80 GB GPU, AdamW with batch size 40 and weight decay 10 −6, learning rates of 5 × 10 −5 for the pretrained backbone and 5 × 10 −4 for the newly initialized embedding and recovery layers, and channel-wise standardization fitted on the training set and frozen for testing. The industrial IND-8C and IND-32C records retain the Therm-FM protocol of 900 training samples with a 1:3 high-to-low-fidelity ratio and 100 high-fidelity test samples; multi-fidelity training is used only for these industrial cases. 

Comparison boundary. Rows within the same case, grid, and state regime are directly comparable; rows across different source tasks are descriptive. MAPE and PAPE are kept because they were reported by the source line, while the controlled S2–S5 track uses the six unified metrics defined in Appendix C.5. Source-reported time and memory are useful for reconstructing the original experi-ments but cannot support a normalized efficiency ranking because hardware and software details differ. The training-cost table above is the controlled record for that purpose. 

Steady Alpha EV6 cases. The source-suite ordering is highly con-sistent: SAU-FNO is the strongest non-foundation baseline, while Therm-FM substantially reduces field and hotspot errors at every reported resolution. Increasing resolution does not produce a uni-form change across methods, so results are preserved per task and grid rather than pooled into a single S1 score. Therm-FM scale also has a small exception on HS-QC at 40 × 40, where the B check-point slightly improves RMSE over L even though L leads the other reported errors.

What S1 does and does not establish. These source suites verify that a method can learn repeated power-to- temperature mappings for a fixed system and, in the transient rows below, can predict a fixed-design temporal response. They do not establish robustness to new package structure, material fields, or boundary support. That limitation explains the later ranking reversal: Therm-FM’s decisive S1 advantage coexists with weaker performance than U-FNO on S3–S4 and large errors for every frozen model on S5. 

Steady versus transient behavior. The three transient tasks re-tain different frame counts and spatial grids, but the same broad ranking appears: operator baselines improve over FNO and Therm-FM further reduces average error. The absolute difficulty is case-dependent—HS-OC has larger errors than HS-SC and HS-QC for most methods—which is precisely why the benchmark keeps the source protocol and time dimension visible. These results should not be mixed with the generated steady-state S2–S5 leaderboard. 

Industrial source cases. IND-8C and IND-32C provide an impor-tant check that the source-suite conclusion is not limited to the Alpha EV6 layouts. Therm-FM L reaches 0.011/0.010 K RMSE, while the relative ordering of conventional operators remains similar across the two systems. Training time and memory in this table remain source-reported, however, and are therefore descriptive rather than a hardware-normalized efficiency comparison. 

Qualitative observations. Figure 5(a) shows that the selected Therm-FM results preserve the global temperature distribution and dominant hotspot locations across the three HotSpot cases and two industrial packages; the visible residuals remain localized mainly near sharper thermal transitions. Panel (b) shows close agreement through the sampled transient steps for HS-SC and HS-OC, includ-ing the temperature rise and spatial diffusion from 0 to 8 ms. These observations are qualitative and refer only to the source Therm-FM visualizations; quantitative comparison remains defined by the tables above. 

Generated-Scope visualizations. Figures 6–10 show representa-tive samples of each generated Scope with the prediction of the best-performing model for that Scope, in the same format as the S1 visualizations (Figures 5). For S2–S4 the columns are Cases 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 of the Scope’s own test split; for S5 they are the five held-out Cases 11–15, scored zero-shot and after 𝐾 = 100 target-case adaptation. Each column shows the test sample whose RMSE is the median within that case, so the panels are representative rather than selected; ground truth and prediction share the temper-ature scale above each column (predictions outside that range are clipped), and the error panel has its own scale below. 

Reading S2–S4. Within the represented system families the pre-dicted fields are visually indistinguishable from the HotSpot ground truth at the scale of the figure. The residual error is not spatially uniform: it concentrates along chiplet boundaries, where the tem-perature gradient is steepest, and at the corners of high-power dies, while the package interior and the background are reproduced to a fraction of a kelvin. Adding the material channel (S3) and the boundary channels (S4) raises the error scale from roughly 1.4–5 K to 5–8 K at the worst pixels, but the error pattern keeps the same edge-localized character; the additional physics is absorbed rather than breaking the predictor. These are the same median samples whose Scope-level averages appear in Tables 15–17. 

Reading S5 zero-shot. Figure 9 shows the strongest frozen model, Therm-FM T, and it is still qualitatively wrong on every compound cross-package OOD. The failure is structural rather than numerical: on Case 11 the 80-chiplet array is rendered as one saturated hot plateau, on Case 13 the scattered micro-chiplets are merged into a single vertical hot band in the wrong place, and on Case 14 the hot spot of the dominant chiplet is predicted with the correct position but a much larger footprint and magnitude. The error maps reach 15–50 K and follow the misplaced structures instead of the chiplet edges. This is the visual counterpart of the order-of-magnitude jump in Table 19: a predictor that is accurate across diverse layouts, materials, and cooling conditions of the represented families does not carry a usable thermal mapping to an unseen package structure, and no baseline in the benchmark is exempt from this. 

Reading S5 after adaptation. Figure 10 applies the same protocol to U-FNO, the strongest model at 𝐾 = 100, after 100 labeled samples per OOD case. With 500 target labels in total, the global thermal feature is recovered on all five packages: the hot region of Case 12, the dominant die of Case 14, and the stacked dies of Case 15 are placed and scaled correctly, and the error scale drops from 15–50 K to 3–8 K at the worst pixel and to about one kelvin over most of the field. The remaining residuals are again edge-localized, ap-proaching the error pattern observed in the S2–S4 within-family evaluations; only the finely distributed hot spots of Case 13 remain under-resolved. Target-case adaptation is therefore a practical rem-edy for cross-package OOD failure—a handful of labels can move an otherwise poor zero-shot prediction substantially toward the S2–S4 accuracy regime—but this recovery requires target labels and is reported separately from, not as a substitute for, zero-shot cross-package generalization. 

## I Benchmark Extension Protocol 

New simulators, silicon measurements, transient cases, or larger package families may be added without changing the primary IC-ThermBench tracks. Each extension should publish its case manifest, channel semantics, label provenance, immutable split identifiers, and reference metrics, and state whether it expands a Generalization Scope or defines a separate domain-shift track. Registered baselines can then be rerun through the shared adapter and evaluator without benchmark-code changes.
